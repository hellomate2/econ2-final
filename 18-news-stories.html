Story questions

Each story opens with real facts, cited, and then switches to made-up numbers so the arithmetic comes out clean. The made-up numbers are practice numbers and are not estimates of the real economy. Parts are worth 2 points each, and graph parts are worth 4 points, as on the Fall 2025 final. Work each story on paper before opening the answer. Answers here are longer than you need so you can learn from them; on the exam, two to four sentences per part is enough.

Story 1: The Fed raises rates, and bond prices fall

10 points Higher for longer. On September 16, 2026, the Federal Reserve (the Fed) raised its target range for the federal funds rate by 0.25 percentage points to 3.75% to 4.00%, its first increase since 2023 (Fed press release; MUFG). On October 28 it held the range there, with two officials voting for another increase. In early October the yield on 10-year Treasury bonds reached about 5.3%, its highest since 2002 (FXStreet, secondary). The numbers below are made up. Recall that the present value of payments Ft received in year t is PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2, where i is the nominal interest rate.

(a) (2 points) A bond pays $40 one year from now and $1,040 two years from now. Find its price when i = 4% and when i = 5%. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Explain fully why the rise in interest rates in fall 2026 lowered the prices of Treasury bonds that had already been issued.

(c) (2 points) What would the bond in part (a) sell for if i = 0? Express it in terms of the payments.

(d) (2 points) A cloud company can buy a block of AI servers for $1,880 million. The servers will earn a marginal revenue product of capital (MRPK) of $1,000 million in each of the next two years and then wear out. Should the company buy them if i = 4%? If i = 5%? Show your work.

(e) (2 points) Using part (d), explain what a rate increase does to planned investment, to planned aggregate expenditure (PAE), and to short-run output in the Keynesian cross. Explain fully.

Show answer

(a) At i = 4%: 40 ÷ 1.04 = 38.462, and 1,040 ÷ 1.042 = 1,040 ÷ 1.0816 = 961.538. The price is 38.462 + 961.538 = $1,000.00. At i = 5%: 40 ÷ 1.05 = 38.095, and 1,040 ÷ 1.052 = 1,040 ÷ 1.1025 = 943.311. The price is 38.095 + 943.311 = $981.41. The bond lost $18.59 of value.

Full credit: $1,000.00 and $981.41, with each payment discounted by the right power of (1 + i).

(b) A bond's payments are fixed when it is issued. Its price is the present value of those payments, and a higher i makes every denominator (1 + i)t bigger, so the present value falls. Another way to see it: once new bonds pay about 5.3%, nobody will pay full price for an old bond paying less, so the old bond's price must fall until its return matches the new rate.

Full credit: fixed payments, higher i, so the present value and therefore the price falls.

(c) With i = 0, every (1 + i) equals 1, so PV = F1 + F2 = 40 + 1,040 = $1,080. This mirrors question 2(d) on the Fall 2025 final.

Full credit: PV = F1 + F2 = $1,080.

(d) The servers are worth the present value of their MRPK. At 4%: 1,000 ÷ 1.04 = 961.538 and 1,000 ÷ 1.0816 = 924.556, for a total of $1,886.09 million. That is more than the $1,880 million cost, so buy. At 5%: 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = 952.381 and 1,000 ÷ 1.1025 = 907.029, for a total of $1,859.41 million. That is less than the cost, so do not buy.

Full credit: $1,886.09 million versus $1,880 million at 4% (buy), $1,859.41 million versus $1,880 million at 5% (do not buy).

(e) When i rises, the present value of what new capital will earn falls, so some projects that were worth doing are no longer worth the cost, exactly as in part (d). Planned investment I falls at every level of income. Because prices adjust slowly, a higher nominal rate also means a higher real rate (r = i − π), which is what matters for these decisions. Lower I shifts the PAE line down in a parallel way, its crossing with the 45-degree line moves left, and short-run output falls by more than the drop in I because of the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), where mpc is the marginal propensity to consume.

Full credit: higher i lowers the value of new capital, so I falls; PAE shifts down; equilibrium Y falls by a multiple of the drop in I.

Story 2: The oil shock, the CPI, and real wages

10 points Paying at the pump. The war with Iran that began in late February 2026 disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and Brent crude rose back above $100 a barrel in September (Coeur d'Alene Press, secondary). On October 14, 2026, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported that the consumer price index (CPI) rose 3.7% over the 12 months to September. Core CPI, which leaves out food and energy, rose 2.6%. Energy prices rose 20.4% and gasoline 34.2% (BLS CPI release). Average hourly earnings rose 3.0% over the year (BLS jobs report, Oct 2, 2026). From part (b) on, the numbers are made up.

(a) (2 points) Why was the headline CPI increase (3.7%) so much bigger than the core increase (2.6%)? Which one shows the oil shock?

(b) (2 points) A worker's wage rose from $26.00 to $26.78 an hour while the CPI rose from 250 to 260. What is the new wage in last year's dollars? Did the worker's purchasing power rise or fall? Show your work.

(c) (2 points) A gallon of gas rose from $3.00 to $4.00 over the same year. Has the real price of gas increased, decreased, or stayed the same? Show your work.

(d) (2 points) Suppose the Fed follows the made-up reaction function r = 1 + 0.5 × (π − 2), with r and inflation π in percent. The oil shock raises inflation from 2% to 4%. What real and nominal interest rates does the Fed set? Is this a movement along the reaction function or a shift of it?

(e) (2 points) The Fed's September statement said "Inflation remains elevated" as it raised rates. Explain fully the tradeoff the Fed faces when an oil shock raises inflation.

Show answer

(a) The CPI is a weighted average of prices in a basket of goods. Energy prices rose 20.4% and gasoline 34.2%, far faster than everything else, and they pull the headline average up. Core CPI removes food and energy, so it barely shows the oil shock. The headline number shows the shock; the core number shows how fast the other prices were rising.

Full credit: energy is in headline but not core, and energy prices jumped, so headline > core; headline shows the shock.

(b) Convert to last year's dollars by dividing by the price ratio: 26.78 × (250 ÷ 260) = 26.78 ÷ 1.04 = $25.75. In last year's dollars the wage fell from $26.00 to $25.75, a drop of $0.25, or about 1%. The nominal wage rose 3% (26.78 ÷ 26.00 = 1.03), but prices rose 4% (260 ÷ 250 = 1.04), so the worker can buy less. This is the same comparison the real data make: wages up 3.0%, prices up 3.7%.

Full credit: real wage $25.75 in last year's dollars; purchasing power fell because prices rose faster than the wage.

(c) Compare growth factors, as in the Big Mac question on the Fall 2025 final. Gas rose by a factor of 4.00 ÷ 3.00 = 1.333. The CPI rose by a factor of 1.04. Gas rose much faster than prices in general, so its real price increased. In last year's dollars, the new price is 4.00 ÷ 1.04 = $3.85, up from $3.00, which is about 28% higher in real terms.

Full credit: 1.333 versus 1.04, so the real price of gas rose (about $3.85 in last year's dollars).

(d) Before the shock: r = 1 + 0.5 × (2 − 2) = 1%, and i = r + π = 1 + 2 = 3%. After: r = 1 + 0.5 × (4 − 2) = 2%, and i = 2 + 4 = 6%. The real rate rises to 2%. This is a movement along the reaction function: inflation changed and the Fed followed its existing rule. The rule itself did not change.

Full credit: r = 2%, i = 6%, a movement along the reaction function.

(e) An oil shock raises inflation right away, for reasons that have nothing to do with how output compares with potential. If the Fed raises the real interest rate to bring inflation down, investment and other interest-sensitive spending fall, PAE shifts down, and output falls below potential, so workers lose jobs. If it does not raise rates, output is protected for now but higher inflation can persist. Either way something bad happens, which is why the Fed's choice is hard after an oil shock.

Full credit: raising r fights inflation but lowers PAE and output; not raising it lets inflation stay high.

Story 3: Tariffs struck down and replaced

12 points Same tariff, new law. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled in Learning Resources v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize tariffs, and left refunds to the Court of International Trade and Customs and Border Protection (McCarter & English). The administration then imposed a 10% global tariff under Section 122, which expired July 24, 2026. On July 23 it imposed Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on goods from 60 economies (CRS report R49481, Oct 8, 2026). Now a made-up market: in the U.S. market for bicycle helmets, demand is P = 60 − 0.5Q and domestic supply is P = 20 + 0.5Q, with Q in millions of helmets per year and P in dollars. The world price is $32.

(a) (2 points) Find the price and quantity with no trade. Then find domestic quantity supplied, quantity demanded, and imports at the world price. Show your work.

(b) (4 points) A 12.5% tariff is placed on imported helmets. Graph the market, showing the world price, the price with the tariff, and quantities supplied and demanded before and after. Find imports after the tariff.

(c) (2 points) How much tariff revenue does the government collect, and how large is the deadweight loss? Show your work.

(d) (2 points) Who wins and who loses from the tariff? Use dollar amounts.

(e) (2 points) Suppose a court orders a tariff refunded. Who gets the money back, and does the refund undo the deadweight loss? Explain fully.

Show answer

(a) No trade: set 60 − 0.5Q = 20 + 0.5Q, so 40 = Q and P = 60 − 20 = $40. At the world price of $32: demand gives 32 = 60 − 0.5Q, so QD = 56 million. Supply gives 32 = 20 + 0.5Q, so QS = 24 million. Imports are 56 − 24 = 32 million. The world price is below the no-trade price, which is why the U.S. imports helmets.

Full credit: no-trade $40 and 40 million; at $32, QS = 24, QD = 56, imports 32.

(b) The tariff is 12.5% × $32 = $4 per helmet, so the U.S. price rises to $36. Supply: 36 = 20 + 0.5Q gives QS = 32. Demand: 36 = 60 − 0.5Q gives QD = 48. Imports fall to 48 − 32 = 16 million.

Full credit: D, S, a flat line at $32 and another at $36, the four quantities 24, 32, 48, 56 marked, and imports 16 million.

(c) Revenue = tariff × imports after = $4 × 16 million = $64 million. Deadweight loss is two triangles. On the production side: ½ × (32 − 24) × 4 = 16. On the consumption side: ½ × (56 − 48) × 4 = 16. Total = $32 million a year.

Full credit: revenue $64 million, deadweight loss $32 million, with the areas shown.

(d) Consumers lose the area between $32 and $36 to the left of demand: (56 + 48) ÷ 2 × 4 = $208 million. Domestic producers gain the strip left of supply: (24 + 32) ÷ 2 × 4 = $112 million. The government gains $64 million. Check: 208 = 112 + 64 + 32, so the $32 million left over is the deadweight loss that nobody gets. Helmet makers and the Treasury win, buyers lose, and the country as a whole loses.

Full credit: consumers −$208 million, producers +$112 million, government +$64 million, net loss $32 million.

(e) The refund goes back to whoever paid the duty at the border, which is the importing company, so it reverses the revenue rectangle. Buyers who paid $36 instead of $32 do not get a check, unless the importer passes the money on. The deadweight loss cannot be refunded at all: the helmets that were made at home at a cost above $32 used up real resources, and the people who stopped buying went without helmets they valued above the world price. That surplus is gone.

Full credit: importers get the refund (the revenue area); the deadweight loss stays lost because it was never collected by anyone.

Story 4: The AI investment boom in the Keynesian cross

14 points Data centers all the way down. After the third-quarter 2026 earnings reports, the largest cloud companies' combined capital spending for 2026, much of it on AI data centers, was estimated at $803 billion to $828 billion, up from about $725 billion estimated in April. The companies said spending would rise again in 2027 (Hargreaves Lansdown and Yahoo Finance, both secondary). Now a made-up model, in trillions of dollars, with a larger boom than the real one so the graph is easy to read. Planned aggregate expenditure is PAE = C + I + G + NX, and

C = 6 + 0.5Y, I = 5, G = 6, NX = −2.

(a) (2 points) Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find PAE when Y = 0, the vertical intercept in the Keynesian cross diagram. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Set PAE = Y and solve for short-run equilibrium output. Show your work.

(c) (2 points) The AI boom raises planned investment from 5 to 7. Find the new intercept and the new equilibrium output. Show your work.

(d) (4 points) On axes running from 0 to 40 with the same scale on both, graph the 45-degree line, the original PAE line, and the new PAE' line. Label intercepts and both equilibria.

(e) (2 points) By how much did PAE change at a given Y, and by how much did GDP change? Which is larger, and why? Explain fully, referencing the model.

(f) (2 points) Suppose $1 trillion of the $2 trillion of new investment buys imported chips and servers. What is the new equilibrium output? Explain fully why it differs from part (c).

Show answer

(a) PAE = 6 + 0.5Y + 5 + 6 − 2 = 15 + 0.5Y. At Y = 0, PAE = 15, which is autonomous spending (the part that does not depend on income).

Full credit: PAE = 15 + 0.5Y and intercept 15.

(b) 15 + 0.5Y = Y, so 15 = 0.5Y and Y = 30. Check: C = 6 + 0.5 × 30 = 21, and 21 + 5 + 6 − 2 = 30.

Full credit: Y = 30 with the algebra shown.

(c) PAE' = 6 + 0.5Y + 7 + 6 − 2 = 17 + 0.5Y, so the intercept is 17. Setting 17 + 0.5Y = Y gives 17 = 0.5Y and Y = 34. Check: C = 6 + 17 = 23, and 23 + 7 + 6 − 2 = 34.

Full credit: intercept 17, Y = 34.

(d)

Full credit: 45-degree line through the origin, two parallel PAE lines with intercepts 15 and 17, crossings at Y = 30 and Y = 34 labeled on both axes.

(e) PAE rose by $2 trillion at every level of Y, a vertical shift up. GDP rose by $4 trillion, from 30 to 34, a move right along the 45-degree line. GDP rose more. The new investment is income for the workers and firms who build data centers, and they spend half of each extra dollar (mpc = 0.5), which becomes someone else's income, and so on. That is the multiplier, 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2, so ΔY = 2 × 2 = 4. On the graph, the PAE line is flatter than the 45-degree line, so a vertical shift of 2 moves the crossing point 4 to the right.

Full credit: PAE +2, GDP +4, GDP larger because the mpc creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 2.

(f) Imports are subtracted in NX, so buying $1 trillion of foreign chips raises I by that amount but lowers NX by the same amount. Spending on imports creates income abroad, not in the U.S. So NX falls from −2 to −3, and PAE'' = 6 + 0.5Y + 7 + 6 − 3 = 16 + 0.5Y. Setting it equal to Y gives 16 = 0.5Y, so Y = 32. GDP rises by only 2 instead of 4, because only the $1 trillion spent on U.S.-made equipment and construction adds to demand for U.S. output.

Full credit: NX falls by 1, intercept 16, Y = 32, because imported equipment raises I and lowers NX equally.

Story 5: A funding lapse cuts government spending

12 points Shut down, multiply out. A record 43-day government shutdown ended in mid-November 2025 (Military.com). For fiscal year 2027, a stopgap bill funds the government only through December 11, 2026, four days before our final (Legis1). Now a made-up model in trillions of dollars, with net taxes T (taxes minus transfers):

C = 5 + 0.8 × (Y − T), T = 5, I = 3, G = 4, NX = −1.

Suppose a long lapse in funding cuts G by $1 trillion at an annual rate. That is far larger than any real shutdown; it is chosen so the graph is readable.

(a) (2 points) Combine the terms in PAE, find the vertical intercept, and solve for equilibrium output before the lapse. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) G falls from 4 to 3. Find the new intercept and the new equilibrium output. Show your work.

(c) (4 points) Graph both PAE lines and the 45-degree line on axes from 0 to 40 with equal scales. Label intercepts and equilibria.

(d) (2 points) G fell by $1 trillion but GDP fell by more. By how much did consumption fall, and why? Explain fully.

(e) (2 points) Suppose Congress instead keeps G at 3 and cuts net taxes to restore the original output. How big a tax cut is needed? Why is it bigger than the $1 trillion cut in G? Explain fully.

Show answer

(a) C = 5 + 0.8Y − 0.8 × 5 = 1 + 0.8Y. PAE = 1 + 0.8Y + 3 + 4 − 1 = 7 + 0.8Y, so the intercept is 7. Setting 7 + 0.8Y = Y gives 7 = 0.2Y, so Y = 35.

Full credit: PAE = 7 + 0.8Y, intercept 7, Y = 35.

(b) PAE' = 1 + 0.8Y + 3 + 3 − 1 = 6 + 0.8Y, intercept 6. Setting 6 + 0.8Y = Y gives 6 = 0.2Y, so Y = 30.

Full credit: intercept 6, Y = 30.

(c)

Full credit: two parallel PAE lines with intercepts 7 and 6 and slope 0.8, the 45-degree line, equilibria at 35 and 30 labeled.

(d) GDP fell by 5 = ΔG + ΔC, so consumption fell by $4 trillion. Check: ΔC = 0.8 × ΔY = 0.8 × (−5) = −4. Furloughed workers and unpaid contractors lose income, so they spend less; that lowers the income of the businesses they buy from, who spend less in turn. With mpc = 0.8 the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5, so the $1 trillion cut in G becomes a $5 trillion fall in output, and $4 trillion of that is lost consumption.

Full credit: ΔY = −5, ΔC = −4, explained by rounds of lost income and spending, multiplier 5.

(e) A tax cut raises PAE only through consumption: households get ΔT more after-tax income and spend mpc × ΔT of it. To shift PAE back up by 1, Congress needs 0.8 × ΔT = 1, so ΔT = −$1.25 trillion (T falls from 5 to 3.75). Check: C = 5 + 0.8 × (Y − 3.75) = 2 + 0.8Y, and PAE = 2 + 0.8Y + 3 + 3 − 1 = 7 + 0.8Y, so Y = 35 again. The tax cut must be bigger because households save 20% of it, while every dollar of G is spent.

Full credit: a $1.25 trillion cut in net taxes, because only mpc = 0.8 of each tax dollar is spent.

Story 6: Immigration enforcement and the farm labor market

12 points Fewer hands in the field. A September 2026 brief from the National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP), an advocacy group, reads the BLS household survey as showing about 1.4 million fewer foreign-born workers between January 2025 and August 2026. The brief notes the survey's large standard errors (NFAP). Edwards' own research studies what unauthorized workers contribute industry by industry, so this is a story he knows well. Now a made-up market for strawberry pickers. A picker's MPL is MPL = 12 − 0.2L crates per hour, where L is thousands of pickers. A crate sells for $2.50, and growers are price takers. Labor supply is w = 6 + 0.5L, with the wage w in dollars per hour.

(a) (2 points) Write the labor demand curve as MRPL = MR × MPL. What are MPL and MRPL when L = 24?

(b) (2 points) Find the equilibrium wage and employment. Show your work.

(c) (4 points) Enforcement removes 6 thousand pickers from the market at every wage, so supply becomes w = 9 + 0.5L. Find the new equilibrium and graph the market before and after, with all curves and equilibria labeled.

(d) (2 points) Six thousand workers left, but employment fell by less. Explain fully why.

(e) (2 points) U.S.-born truck drivers haul the crates the pickers fill. What happens to the demand for drivers, and to their wages and employment? Explain fully using MRPL = MR × MPL.

Show answer

(a) For a price taker, marginal revenue is the price, $2.50. So MRPL = 2.50 × (12 − 0.2L) = 30 − 0.5L. At L = 24: MPL = 12 − 0.2 × 24 = 7.2 crates per hour, and MRPL = 2.50 × 7.2 = $18 per hour.

Full credit: MRPL = 30 − 0.5L; MPL = 7.2, MRPL = $18 at L = 24.

(b) Set demand equal to supply: 30 − 0.5L = 6 + 0.5L, so 24 = L. Employment is 24 thousand and the wage is 30 − 12 = $18. That matches part (a): growers hire until the last picker's MRPL equals the wage.

Full credit: L = 24 thousand, w = $18.

(c) Set 30 − 0.5L = 9 + 0.5L, so 21 = L. Employment falls to 21 thousand and the wage rises to 30 − 10.5 = $19.50.

Full credit: MRPL unchanged, supply shifted left, new equilibrium w = $19.50 and L = 21 thousand, both equilibria labeled on both axes.

(d) The shortage of pickers pushes the wage up from $18 to $19.50. At the higher wage, more of the remaining workers are willing to pick: on the new supply curve, 18 thousand would work at $18, but 21 thousand work at $19.50. So 3 thousand of the 6 thousand lost workers are replaced, and employment falls by only 3 thousand. At the same time growers move up their demand curve and hire fewer workers, because each worker now costs more.

Full credit: the wage rises, which draws in more of the remaining workers along the new supply curve, so employment falls by 3 thousand, less than 6.

(e) With fewer pickers, fewer crates are filled, so each driver has less to haul and the marginal product of a driver falls. Since the drivers' labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, lower MPL shifts their whole labor demand curve in (down). Their wages and employment both fall. Workers whose jobs complement the pickers' work lose when enforcement removes the pickers, even though they were never in the pickers' labor market.

Full credit: drivers' MPL falls, so their MRPL (labor demand) shifts left, lowering their wage and employment.

Story 7: The yen above 160 per dollar

12 points Yen for a weaker currency. Japan spent about $73 billion buying yen after the exchange rate passed 160 yen per dollar in late April and early May 2026 (New Straits Times). A joint U.S.-Japan intervention at the end of July pulled the rate from about 163 toward 155 to 158 (Oxford Economics), but the yen was back above 160 per dollar by early September (BestExchangeRates, secondary). Meanwhile U.S. interest rates were rising (Story 1). From part (b) on, the numbers are made up.

(a) (2 points) The exchange rate rises from ¥150 per dollar to ¥160 per dollar. Which currency appreciated and which depreciated?

(b) (2 points) A Cal sweatshirt costs $50 and a bowl of ramen in Tokyo costs ¥1,200. Find the yen price of the sweatshirt and the dollar price of the ramen at ¥150 and at ¥160 per dollar. Show your work.

(c) (2 points) Using part (b), what happens to U.S. exports to Japan, imports from Japan, and net exports? Explain fully.

(d) (2 points) Explain fully why higher U.S. interest rates tend to push up the price of the dollar in yen.

(e) (4 points) In a made-up market for dollars, priced in yen, demand is e = 190 − 0.5Q and supply is e = 130 + 0.5Q, where e is yen per dollar and Q is billions of dollars traded per day. Japan's government sells an extra $20 billion a day from its reserves to buy yen. Find the exchange rate before and after, and graph the market.

Show answer

(a) The number is the price of one dollar in yen. It rose, so the dollar appreciated and the yen depreciated. This is the reverse of the Fall 2025 multiple choice question, where the rate fell from ¥160 to ¥150 and the yen appreciated.

Full credit: dollar appreciated, yen depreciated.

(b) Sweatshirt: 50 × 150 = ¥7,500, and 50 × 160 = ¥8,000. Ramen: 1,200 ÷ 150 = $8.00, and 1,200 ÷ 160 = $7.50. U.S. goods became more expensive for Japanese buyers, and Japanese goods became cheaper for Americans.

Full credit: ¥7,500 to ¥8,000 for the sweatshirt; $8.00 to $7.50 for the ramen.

(c) Japanese buyers face higher yen prices for U.S. goods, so U.S. exports to Japan fall. Americans face lower dollar prices for Japanese goods and trips to Japan, so U.S. imports from Japan rise. Net exports, exports minus imports, fall. In the Keynesian cross, lower NX shifts PAE down.

Full credit: exports fall, imports rise, NX falls, because the strong dollar makes U.S. goods relatively expensive.

(d) Investors look for the highest real return. When U.S. rates rise while Japan's stay low, Japanese savers want U.S. bonds, so they demand more dollars, and U.S. savers keep their money home, so they supply fewer dollars to buy yen. Demand for dollars shifts right and supply shifts left, and both shifts raise the price of the dollar in yen.

Full credit: higher U.S. r attracts capital, demand for dollars rises and supply falls, so the dollar appreciates.

(e) Before: 190 − 0.5Q = 130 + 0.5Q gives 60 = Q, and e = 190 − 30 = ¥160. Japan's sales add $20 billion to supply at every price, so supply becomes e = 130 + 0.5 × (Q − 20) = 120 + 0.5Q. After: 190 − 0.5Q = 120 + 0.5Q gives 70 = Q, and e = 190 − 35 = ¥155. The dollar depreciates and the yen appreciates, which is what the intervention is for.

Full credit: supply of dollars shifts right; equilibria at ¥160 and $60 billion, then ¥155 and $70 billion, labeled on both axes.

Story 8: Four-dollar gas, a cumulative micro story

12 points Fill 'er up. U.S. gasoline passed $4 a gallon in early April 2026, up from about $2.98 before the war with Iran (CBS News), and was back at $4.00 on July 20 (Axios). Now a made-up state gasoline market, with Q in millions of gallons per week. Demand, which is MB, is P = 6 − 0.1Q. Supply, which is MC, was P = 0.1Q before the war. The oil shock raises the marginal cost of every gallon by $2, so supply becomes P = 2 + 0.1Q.

(a) (2 points) Find the equilibrium price and quantity before and after the shock. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Marginal cost rose by $2 a gallon, but the price rose by only $1. Explain fully, using MB and MC.

(c) (4 points) Suppose the state caps the price of gas at $3, the old price. Graph the market after the shock with the ceiling. Find the quantity sold, the shortage, and the deadweight loss.

(d) (2 points) Burning gasoline imposes an external cost of $1 a gallon on other people. Without a ceiling, what quantity is efficient after the shock? Is the market quantity too high or too low? Show your work.

(e) (2 points) If one company controlled all gasoline supply in the state, would its price be above or below $4, and its quantity above or below 20 million gallons? Explain.

Show answer

(a) Before: 6 − 0.1Q = 0.1Q gives 6 = 0.2Q, so Q = 30 million gallons and P = $3. After: 6 − 0.1Q = 2 + 0.1Q gives 4 = 0.2Q, so Q = 20 and P = 6 − 2 = $4.

Full credit: (30, $3) before and (20, $4) after.

(b) At the old quantity of 30, the marginal cost of a gallon is now 2 + 3 = $5, but buyers value the 30th million gallon at only $3. Gallons whose MC exceeds their MB stop being bought, so quantity falls. As it falls, MB rises along the demand curve and MC falls along the new supply curve, until they meet at $4 and 20. Buyers absorb part of the cost increase by paying $1 more, and the rest shows up as less gas sold.

Full credit: at the old quantity MC > MB, so quantity falls until MB = MC; with downward-sloping demand the price rises by less than the cost increase.

(c) At $3, sellers supply 3 = 2 + 0.1Q, so QS = 10 million gallons, and that is all that gets sold. Buyers want 3 = 6 − 0.1Q, so QD = 30. The shortage is 20 million gallons a week. The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and supply from 10 to 20: at Q = 10 buyers value gas at $5 and it costs $3 to supply, so the triangle is ½ × (20 − 10) × (5 − 3) = $10 million a week. Lines and misallocation would make the real loss larger.

Full credit: ceiling drawn below the new equilibrium, Q sold = 10, shortage = 20, deadweight loss triangle shaded = $10 million.

(d) Social marginal cost (SMC) is private MC plus the external cost: SMC = 2 + 0.1Q + 1 = 3 + 0.1Q. Efficient quantity: 6 − 0.1Q = 3 + 0.1Q, so Q = 15 million gallons. The market sells 20, which is too much, because buyers and sellers ignore the $1 cost to others. The deadweight loss is ½ × (20 − 15) × 1 = $2.5 million a week.

Full credit: SMC = 3 + 0.1Q, efficient Q = 15, market quantity 20 is too high.

(e) A monopolist produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and its marginal revenue is below the price because it must cut the price on every gallon to sell one more. So it stops short of 20 million gallons and charges more than $4: higher price, lower quantity. That is the monopoly answer from the Fall 2025 multiple choice, with a deadweight loss because MB > MC at its quantity.

Full credit: price above $4 and quantity below 20, because the monopolist sets MR = MC with MR below price.

Story 9: Soybeans and the trade truce

10 points Oy, soybeans again. In late September 2026, the U.S. and China extended their trade truce from November 10, 2026 to January 10, 2027 (Korea Times). China's pledge to buy 25 million metric tons of U.S. soybeans a year runs through 2028, and China's duty on U.S. soybeans is 13% (DTN). Now a made-up U.S. soybean market, with Q in billions of bushels per year: demand is P = 14 − 0.5Q and supply is P = 2 + 0.5Q. Suppose that during the trade war the world price U.S. farmers could get was $9 a bushel, and that the truce raises it to $10.

(a) (2 points) Find the no-trade price and quantity. Then find quantity demanded, quantity supplied, and exports at $9. Show your work.

(b) (4 points) Graph the market and show what happens when the world price rises to $10. Find the new exports.

(c) (2 points) How much do U.S. producer surplus and U.S. consumer surplus change when the price rises from $9 to $10? What is the net change? Show your work.

(d) (2 points) U.S. soybean buyers, such as livestock feeders, are worse off after the truce. Explain fully why the country still gains.

Show answer

(a) No trade: 14 − 0.5Q = 2 + 0.5Q gives 12 = Q, and P = 14 − 6 = $8. At $9: demand gives 9 = 14 − 0.5Q, so QD = 10; supply gives 9 = 2 + 0.5Q, so QS = 14. Exports = 14 − 10 = 4 billion bushels. The world price is above the no-trade price, so the U.S. exports.

Full credit: no-trade (12, $8); at $9, QD = 10, QS = 14, exports 4.

(b) At $10: QD = 2 × (14 − 10) = 8 and QS = 2 × (10 − 2) = 16, so exports double to 8 billion bushels.

Full credit: D, S, flat lines at $9 and $10, quantities 8, 10, 14, 16 marked, exports 8.

(c) Producer surplus rises by the area between $9 and $10 left of supply: (14 + 16) ÷ 2 × 1 = +$15 billion. Consumer surplus falls by the area between $9 and $10 left of demand: (10 + 8) ÷ 2 × 1 = −$9 billion. Net change = 15 − 9 = +$6 billion. Check: the net gain is the area between the curves at the two prices, (4 + 8) ÷ 2 × 1 = 6.

Full credit: producers +$15 billion, consumers −$9 billion, net +$6 billion.

(d) The $9 billion that U.S. buyers lose is a transfer to U.S. farmers, who gain all of it plus another $6 billion from selling more soybeans abroad at the higher price. Farmers' gain exceeds buyers' loss, so total U.S. surplus rises. This is the export case from class: a higher world price helps the exporting country as a whole, even though domestic buyers lose.

Full credit: buyers' loss is smaller than farmers' gain (part of it is a transfer), so the net change is positive.

One-minute review

  • Every story starts with a news item and ends in a model from class. Name the model first: bond price, CPI real price, tariff graph, Keynesian cross, labor market, or exchange rate.
  • Higher interest rates lower the price of existing bonds and the value of new capital, so investment and PAE fall.
  • Real wage or real price: divide by the CPI ratio, or compare growth factors as in the Big Mac question.
  • An oil shock is an inflation shock. The Fed moves along its reaction function, raising r, which costs output.
  • A tariff raises the domestic price by the tariff. Consumers lose more than producers and the government gain, and the difference is two deadweight loss triangles.
  • In the Keynesian cross, a change in I or G shifts PAE by that amount, and GDP moves by 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) times as much. Spending on imports adds nothing to U.S. demand.
  • A tax cut must be bigger than the G change it replaces, because only mpc of each tax dollar is spent.
  • Losing workers shifts labor supply left: the wage rises and employment falls by less than the number who left. Workers in complementary jobs see their MRPL fall.
  • Yen per dollar going up means the dollar appreciated, so U.S. net exports fall. Japan selling dollars shifts the supply of dollars right and pushes the rate down.
