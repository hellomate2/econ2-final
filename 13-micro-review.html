Midterm material refresher

The micro ideas from the first half that the final still asks about, in one compact page. On the Fall 2025 final, three of the six multiple-choice items were midterm material (marginal benefit equals marginal cost, comparative advantage, monopoly), one short answer was a marginal cost argument, and the trade question leaned on supply and demand. For full depth on any topic, follow the link to the matching unit of the midterm site.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

The whole first half of the course is one rule used over and over: do one more of something only if it is worth more than it costs. Demand shows what buyers think one more unit is worth, supply shows what it costs to make, and a free market lands where the two are equal. For the final, remember that every policy or problem on this page (a tax, a price control, a monopoly, pollution) is judged by how far it pushes the quantity away from that point, and the triangle it leaves behind is the deadweight loss.

Abbreviations used on this page: MB is marginal benefit, MC is marginal cost, MR is marginal revenue, MU is marginal utility, CS is consumer surplus, PS is producer surplus, DWL is deadweight loss, PPC is the production possibilities curve, P is price, and Q is quantity.

1. Marginal benefit equals marginal cost

Every choice in this course is made at the margin, which means you look only at the next unit. You compare the benefit of one more unit with the cost of one more unit. Keep going while MB is greater than MC, and stop where they are equal. A market does the same thing for a whole society. The demand curve shows buyers' MB and the supply curve shows sellers' MC, so the equilibrium quantity is where MB = MC.

Costs here are opportunity costs: the value of the next best thing you give up. Some of that cost you pay in cash, and some you never see as a bill. The cost of a year of college includes tuition and also the earnings you give up by not working. Rent and food do not count, because you pay them whether or not you go to college. An hour of your time is worth roughly what you could earn in that hour. When a choice is between two things, divide: if a free afternoon gives you either 2 bookcases built or 3 shows watched, one show costs 2 ÷ 3 of a bookcase (Problem Set 1 Q1(a)). Depth: midterm unit 01.

Do more of something while MB > MC. Stop where MB = MC. In a market, demand is MB and supply is MC.

In plain words: keep doing something as long as the next one is worth more to you than it costs you. Stop when the next one is worth exactly what it costs. The cost always includes what you could have done instead, as well as the money you hand over.

2. Comparative advantage

You have a comparative advantage in a task when your opportunity cost of doing it is lower than someone else's. You have an absolute advantage when you can simply produce more of it per hour. Gains from trade come from comparative advantage, so even someone who is faster at everything should specialize in the task where their edge is biggest and trade for the rest.

The class example: per hour, Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut, and Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts. Chris is faster at both. But a coconut costs Chris 4 fish and costs Bill only 1 fish, so Bill should gather coconuts and Chris should fish. On the Fall 2025 midterm, a new fishhook doubled both people's fish per hour. A coconut then cost Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish. Both opportunity costs doubled, so Bill's is still the lower one and nobody switches tasks. The same holds if a tool doubles one person's output of both goods: that person's opportunity costs do not change at all. Depth: midterm unit 02.

Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. Specialize in the task you do best relative to your other options, not in every task you do better than others.

In plain words: whoever gives up the least to do a job should do that job, even if someone else is faster at it. Being faster at everything does not mean you should do everything.

Problem Set 1 Q2 added a third islander, Robinson, who catches 2 fish or gathers 2 coconuts per hour. A coconut costs him 2 ÷ 2 = 1 fish, the same as Bill. When two people have the same opportunity cost, they share the job: Bill and Robinson both gather coconuts and Chris fishes. To draw a joint PPC, first add up each person's full output to get the two intercepts. Then let the person with the lowest opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis switch first. Each switch adds a straight piece whose slope is minus that person's opportunity cost, and a kink appears wherever the next person starts switching.

3. Supply and demand shifts

A change in the good's own price moves you along a curve. Anything else that changes buyers' willingness to pay shifts demand, and anything else that changes sellers' costs shifts supply. A substitute is a good used instead of this one (tea for coffee). A complement is a good used together with it (gasoline for an SUV). Higher income, a pricier substitute, a cheaper complement, or a fashion boom shifts demand right. Better technology, cheaper inputs, or more sellers shift supply right. Taxes, costlier inputs, or a lost harvest shift supply left.

First decide which curve the story moves. A story about buyers moves only demand, and a story about sellers moves only supply. Depth: midterm unit 03.

In plain words: ask who the news is about. If it is about the people buying, move demand. If it is about the people making and selling, move supply. Then read the new price and quantity where the curves now cross.

When two things happen at once, both curves move. Work out each shift on its own, then combine them. The result both shifts agree on is certain, and the other one depends on which shift is bigger. If demand and supply both shift right, quantity surely rises, but the price can go either way. If demand shifts right and supply shifts left, the price surely rises, but the quantity can go either way.

Questions can also run backward: you see what happened to the price and the quantity, and you must say which curve moved. If price and quantity moved in the same direction, demand shifted. If they moved in opposite directions, supply shifted. The sizes tell you about the curve that stayed put: a big price change with a small quantity change means that curve is steep (inelastic). This is how the Fall 2025 midterm Q2(f) wanted you to read the egg price spikes as a supply shock.

4. Surplus and efficiency

Consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price. Each buyer pays the market price but would have paid up to their MB, and the gap is their gain. Producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price. Each seller receives the price but only needed to cover that unit's MC, and the gap is their gain. Total surplus is their sum. A competitive market with no market failure produces the quantity where MB = MC, which makes total surplus as large as it can be.

In plain words: consumer surplus is how much better off buyers are than if they had paid their top price. Producer surplus is how much better off sellers are than if they had been paid only their cost. A free market with no outside harm makes the total of the two as big as possible.

Deadweight loss is the total surplus that disappears because some trades worth making do not happen (or trades not worth making do). Any policy that pushes the quantity away from MB = MC creates one. On the graph it is a triangle between demand and supply that points toward the efficient quantity. Too little and too much output both create it.

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price, and a price floor is a legal minimum price. A ceiling matters only if it is below equilibrium, and a floor matters only if it is above. Neither one shifts a curve. Instead the quantity traded becomes the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied at the legal price, because nobody can be forced to buy or sell.

  • Ceiling below equilibrium: sellers offer less, so units sold = quantity supplied, and there is a shortage equal to quantity demanded minus quantity supplied. Producer surplus falls. Consumer surplus can rise (a lower price on the units still sold) or fall (fewer units), and his review slide 32 shows total surplus falling by the deadweight loss.
  • Floor above equilibrium: buyers take less, so units sold = quantity demanded, and there is an excess supply (a surplus of the good) equal to quantity supplied minus quantity demanded. Consumer surplus falls.

A shortage also means the good may go to the wrong buyers. The graph assumes the buyers who value it most get it, but with a waiting list that need not happen, so the true loss can be bigger than the triangle. Depth: midterm unit 04.

With a binding price control, units sold = the smaller of Qd and Qs. Ceiling: shortage Qd − Qs. Floor: excess supply Qs − Qd. Both create a deadweight loss.

In plain words: a law that holds the price too low leaves people who want the good but cannot find it. A law that holds the price too high leaves sellers with goods nobody buys. Either way, some trades that would have helped both sides never happen.

5. Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand measures how much quantity responds to price: elasticity = (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P). If its size is above 1, demand is elastic; below 1, inelastic. "Size" means the number without its minus sign. His midterm key used simple percent changes measured from the starting values. It kept the negative sign and said "relatively elastic" or "relatively inelastic" depending on whether the size was above or below 1, so write your answers the same way. Demand is inelastic when there are no close substitutes, when the good is a necessity, or in the short run. On a graph, inelastic means steep and elastic means flat.

When you are given a straight-line demand curve instead of two data points, use the version on his Class 6 slide 22 and Problem Set 2 Q3(b): elasticity = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q), where the slope is the change in P per one unit of Q and P and Q are the point you are at. Because P ÷ Q changes as you slide along the line, one straight demand curve is elastic near the top and inelastic near the bottom (Class 6 slide 24). Example: on P = 300 − 10Q the slope is −10. At Q = 10 and P = 200, elasticity = (1 ÷ −10) × (200 ÷ 10) = −2, elastic. At Q = 20 and P = 100, it is (1 ÷ −10) × (100 ÷ 20) = −0.5, inelastic.

Elasticity tells you what happens to spending, which is price times quantity. When demand is inelastic, a price increase raises total spending, because quantity falls by a smaller percent than price rises. His Class 7 slide 13 example: the price rises from $100 to $110 (10%) and quantity falls from 200 to 185 (7.5%), so spending rises from $20,000 to $20,350. When demand is elastic, a price increase lowers spending: on slide 15 the quantity falls to 170 (15%) and spending falls to $18,700. Supply can be inelastic too. Once a crop is planted, the short-run supply is close to vertical, which is the setup of the Fall 2025 soybean question. Depth: midterm unit 05.

Inelastic means steep. Elastic means flat. Elasticity = (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P), with Q on top. On a straight line, elasticity = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q).

In plain words: elasticity says how strongly buyers react when the price changes. If they barely react (they need it, or nothing replaces it), demand is inelastic, the curve is steep, and a higher price brings in more money. If they react a lot, demand is elastic, the curve is flat, and a higher price brings in less money.

6. Taxes

A per-unit tax is a fixed dollar amount charged on every unit sold. It drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. The wedge equals the tax and sits at the new, smaller quantity. Buyers pay the price read off demand; sellers keep the price read off supply. It does not matter which side sends the money to the government: the result is the same (Class 6 slide 44).

To find the new quantity with equations, set the buyers' price minus the sellers' price equal to the tax, which is (demand height) − (supply height) = tax. Tax revenue is the tax times the new quantity. The deadweight loss is the triangle between the two curves from the new quantity out to the old one, so it equals ½ × tax × (drop in quantity).

The side that is less elastic (steeper) bears more of the tax, because it cannot easily walk away. Two extreme cases come up in his problems. If supply is flat (perfectly elastic), as in Problem Set 2 Q3(c), buyers bear the whole tax. If demand is vertical (perfectly inelastic), buyers also bear all of it.

Elasticity also sets the size of the deadweight loss. When buyers or sellers react a lot to the price, the tax cuts the quantity a lot, so the triangle is big. His Class 6 slide 61 puts it as "Taxing inelastic goods is more efficient," meaning the same tax wastes less surplus on a good people keep buying.

A per-unit subsidy is a negative tax: the government pays an amount per unit. It pushes the quantity above the efficient level, so at the new quantity MB < MC, and it also creates a deadweight loss (Class 6 slides 61 and 63). The subsidy costs the government the subsidy times the new, larger quantity. Depth: midterm unit 06.

Tax: wedge = tax at the new quantity; revenue = tax × new Q; DWL = ½ × tax × drop in Q. The less elastic side pays more of it, and the more elastic the market, the bigger the DWL.

In plain words: a tax makes buyers pay more and sellers keep less, so fewer units get sold. Whoever has fewer other options ends up paying most of it. The units that no longer get sold are the waste, and that waste is bigger when people can easily switch away.

7. Households: the rational spending rule

A household gets the most out of its budget when the last dollar spent on each good brings the same marginal utility: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb. If good a gives more MU per dollar, buy more of a and less of b. Marginal utility falls as you consume more, so buying more of a lowers its MU until the two ratios match. Edwards put this rule on his Fall 2025 final review slides. Depth: midterm unit 07.

When a price changes, say which side of the equation moved, then change the quantity so that falling MU puts it back. If the price of clothing rises, MU ÷ P for clothing falls, so buy less clothing (its MU rises) and more of other goods (their MU falls). If something raises the MU of a good at every quantity, like a heatwave for electricity in Problem Set 2 Q2(c), demand for it shifts out and you buy more of it.

In plain words: spend each next dollar on whatever gives you the most enjoyment for that dollar. You are doing the best you can when no dollar could be moved from one good to another to make you happier.

8. Competitive firms

A competitive firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it wants at the market price, so its MR equals the price. It maximizes profit by producing where P = MC. That makes each firm's MC curve its supply curve, and the market supply curve is the sum of firms' MC curves. Anything that lowers every firm's MC, such as better technology or cheaper inputs, shifts market supply right. Depth: midterm unit 08.

Profit tells firms whether to enter or leave. Average total cost (atc) is total cost divided by quantity, so profit = (P − atc) × q (Class 9 slide 41). Costs here are economic costs, which include opportunity costs, so zero profit means the owners earn exactly what they could earn elsewhere. If firms make positive profits, new firms enter, market supply shifts right, and the price falls. If they make losses, firms exit and the price rises. When all firms are alike, this stops only at zero profit, where P = mc = atc (Class 9 slides 46 and 47).

Competitive firm: P = MR = MC. Profit = (P − atc) × q. Positive profit brings entry until P = atc and profit is zero.

In plain words: a small firm cannot change the market price, so it keeps making more until one more unit would cost more than the price. If firms in an industry are earning more than they could elsewhere, others join in, and the extra supply pushes the price down until that extra profit is gone.

9. Monopoly

A monopolist is the only seller, so it faces the whole market demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price on every unit, so its MR is below the price. For example, if it sells 8 units at $15 and must cut to $14 to sell 9, revenue goes from $120 to $126, so MR is only $6. The ninth unit brings in $14, but the $1 cut on the first 8 units costs $8. With a straight-line demand curve P = a − bQ, MR = a − 2bQ: it starts at the same intercept and is twice as steep (Class 10 slide 26). The monopolist picks the quantity where MR = MC, then charges the highest price buyers will pay for that quantity, read straight up on the demand curve.

Monopoly profit is total revenue minus total cost. When fixed costs are zero, total cost is the area under the MC curve up to the quantity sold. On the Fall 2025 midterm Q3(f) that area was a triangle, so profit was $2 billion − $500 million = $1.5 billion. His key also gave full credit for the rectangle (P − MC) × Q = $1 billion, which was his own first answer. A monopoly can keep a positive profit in the long run only because something keeps rivals out. His Class 10 slide 10 lists the barriers to entry: high fixed costs, patents and other legal protections, and licensing or other gatekeeping.

Monopoly: find Q where MR = MC, then go up to demand for P. Compared with competition, a monopolist raises price and reduces quantity, and at its quantity MB > MC.

In plain words: a seller with no competition makes fewer units than buyers want, because selling more would mean cutting the price for everyone. The result is a higher price, fewer sales, and some units that are worth more to buyers than they cost to make but never get made.

Depth: midterm unit 09.

10. Externalities and Pigouvian taxes

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside the market. With a negative externality such as pollution, social marginal cost (SMC) equals private marginal cost (PMC) plus external marginal cost (EMC), so SMC sits above the supply curve. The market produces where demand meets private cost, which is too much. The efficient quantity Q* is where social marginal benefit equals SMC.

A Pigouvian tax equal to the external marginal cost at Q* moves supply up to SMC, so buyers and sellers now face the full cost and the market lands on Q*. The efficient amount of pollution is usually not zero: it is wherever SMB = SMC, which is normally a positive quantity (Fall 2025 midterm Q1(g)).

A positive externality, such as a vaccine or a neighbor's beehive that pollinates gardens, works the other way. Social marginal benefit (SMB) equals private marginal benefit (PMB) plus external marginal benefit, so SMB sits above the demand curve (Class 11 slide 18). The market produces too little, and a per-unit subsidy equal to the external benefit fixes it (Class 11 slide 35).

Two problems can also cancel. In Problem Set 2 Q3(f) a monopolist that also polluted happened to produce exactly the efficient quantity, because cutting output to raise its price offset the overproduction the pollution would cause. His key called this a "lucky coincidence." Depth: midterm unit 10.

SMC = PMC + EMC. Negative externality: the market overproduces. Pigouvian tax per unit = EMC at the efficient quantity. Positive externality: SMB = PMB + external MB, the market underproduces, and a subsidy fixes it.

In plain words: when making or using something hurts people who are not part of the deal, the market makes too much of it, because nobody pays for the harm. A tax equal to the harm makes the seller count it. When something helps outsiders, the market makes too little, and paying people for that help fixes it.

11. The bridge to the second half: labor demand

The micro tools come back in the labor unit. A firm hires workers up to where the wage equals the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MPL (marginal product of labor) is the extra output from one more worker. For a competitive firm MR is the output price, so anything that lowers the price of the good, such as losing an export market, shifts labor demand in. That was Fall 2025 final Q4(c). Full treatment is in Labor and wages.

In plain words: a firm hires another worker only if what that worker produces sells for more than the wage. If the product sells for less, every worker is worth less to the firm, so it wants fewer of them at any wage.

Worked examples

Example: the rational spending rule from his review slides

Coffee costs $4 and frozen yogurt costs $6. The first coffee gives 10 units of MU and the second gives 8. The first frozen yogurt gives 12 and the second gives 8. You have $14 a day. What do you buy?

  1. Compute MU per dollar for each unit. Coffee: 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5, then 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Frozen yogurt: 12 ÷ 6 = 2, then 8 ÷ 6 = 1.33.
  2. Buy units in order of MU per dollar: first coffee (2.5) for $4.
  3. Next the second coffee (2) and the first frozen yogurt (2) tie. Buying both costs $4 + $6 = $10, for a total of $14, the whole budget.
  4. Check the rule: last coffee 8 ÷ $4 = 2 and last frozen yogurt 12 ÷ $6 = 2. The ratios match.

Answer: 2 coffees and 1 frozen yogurt, because that spends the budget and equalizes MU per dollar at 2. (The slide shows the bundle and the 8 ÷ $4 = 12 ÷ $6 check; the $14 budget is implied by it.)

Example: monopoly versus competition with Problem Set 2 numbers

Demand is P = 5 − Q and MC is constant at $1. Compare a competitive market with a monopoly.

  1. Competition: P = MC, so 5 − Q = 1, giving Q = 4 and P = $1.
  2. Monopoly: MR has the same intercept and twice the slope, so MR = 5 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2.
  3. Read the price off demand: P = 5 − 2 = $3.
  4. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and MC from Q = 2 to Q = 4, which is ½ × (4 − 2) × (3 − 1) = 2. Profit is (3 − 1) × 2 = 4.

Answer: the monopolist charges $3 instead of $1 and sells 2 instead of 4: it raises price and reduces quantity, with a deadweight loss of 2.

Example: elasticity and spending

A venue raises ticket prices from $50 to $60 and sales fall from 1,000 to 700.

  1. Percent change in price: (60 − 50) ÷ 50 = +20%.
  2. Percent change in quantity: (700 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = −30%.
  3. Elasticity: −30% ÷ 20% = −1.5. The size is above 1, so demand is elastic.
  4. Spending: $50 × 1,000 = $50,000 before and $60 × 700 = $42,000 after.

Answer: elasticity is −1.5 (elastic), so the price increase lowers total spending, from $50,000 to $42,000.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "Comparative advantage means I should do every task I am better at."

Correct: That is absolute advantage. Comparative advantage compares opportunity costs, and you specialize in the task where yours is lowest. This was the trap option in Fall 2025 final Q1(b).

Mistake: Reading the monopoly price at the point where MR crosses MC.

Correct: MR = MC gives the quantity. The price comes from going straight up to the demand curve at that quantity. In the Swift graph, MR = MC at $100, but the price is $200.

Mistake: Saying a price change shifts the demand curve.

Correct: A change in the good's own price is a movement along the curve. Only other factors, such as income or the price of a related good, shift it.

Mistake: Putting the price change on top of the elasticity fraction.

Correct: Quantity goes on top: (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P).

Mistake: Thinking the efficient amount of pollution is zero.

Correct: The efficient amount is where SMB = SMC, which is usually a positive amount. The Pigouvian tax gets you there; it does not ban the activity.

Mistake: Shifting both curves for a one-sided story.

Correct: A buyer story moves demand and a seller story moves supply. The new equilibrium then slides along the curve that did not move.

Mistake: With a price ceiling, counting the quantity demanded as the quantity sold.

Correct: Nobody can be made to sell, so units sold are the quantity supplied at the ceiling. With a floor it flips: nobody can be made to buy, so units sold are the quantity demanded.

Mistake: Taking the monopolist's MR to be the price of the extra unit.

Correct: MR is the change in total revenue. It equals the new price minus the price cut on every unit already being sold, so it is always below the price.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Write one line before drawing anything: "buyers, so demand" or "sellers, so supply." Most supply and demand questions are decided by that line.
  • With equations, solve the no-policy equilibrium first by setting demand equal to supply, even if the question does not ask. Every surplus, tax, price control, and deadweight loss number starts from that point.
  • For any area, write ½ × base × height with the base measured along the quantity axis. The deadweight loss triangle always points at the efficient quantity.
  • For monopoly, write the three steps in order: MR with the same intercept and twice the slope, MR = MC for Q, then demand for P.
  • In multiple choice, cross out options that describe absolute advantage, a curve shifting because of its own price, or a monopoly price read at MR = MC. If two survivors remain, check whether "all of the above" is really true.
  • If a word answer will not come, compare MB and MC for one more unit and say who gains and who pays. Most micro explanations on this page come down to that comparison, and a correct method can still earn partial credit.

Words that change the answer: "demand" versus "quantity demanded" (a shift versus a move along the curve). "Below equilibrium" for a ceiling and "above equilibrium" for a floor (otherwise the control does nothing). "Comparative" versus "absolute." "Marginal" versus "average" or "total." "Per-unit" tax versus a one-time fee. "Social" versus "private" cost. "Short run" (inelastic supply, no entry) versus "long run" (elastic, entry until zero profit). 2 points 1. A farmer can plant one more acre. The acre would add $500 of revenue and cost $420 in seed, labor, and water. The farmer should

(i) Not plant, because profit per acre is lower on the extra acre
(ii) Plant, because the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost
(iii) Plant only if the average cost of all acres is below $420
(iv) Not plant, because $420 is a large cost

Show answer

(ii). The decision is about the extra acre only. It adds $500 and costs $420, so MB > MC and planting raises profit by $80. Averages and totals do not matter for a marginal decision.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 2. The opportunity cost of a year of college includes

(i) Tuition only
(ii) Tuition and rent
(iii) Tuition and the earnings you give up by not working
(iv) Rent and food only

Show answer

(iii). Opportunity cost is what you give up. You give up the tuition money and the paycheck you could have earned. Rent and food are paid whether or not you go to college, so they cancel out.

Full credit: circle (iii).

2 points 3. In one hour, Amy can write 4 reports or code 2 programs. Ben can write 1 report or code 1 program. Which is true?

(i) Amy should do both tasks because she is faster at both
(ii) Amy should write reports and Ben should code
(iii) Ben should write reports and Amy should code
(iv) There are no gains from trade because Amy is better at both

Show answer

(ii). A program costs Amy 2 reports (4 ÷ 2) and costs Ben 1 report. Ben has the lower opportunity cost of programs, so he codes. A report costs Amy half a program and costs Ben 1 program, so Amy writes reports. Amy has the absolute advantage in both, which does not decide who does what.

Full credit: circle (ii).

(i) Ben has the comparative advantage in reports

(iii) Amy should do both tasks

Show answer

(ii). Ben now makes 2 reports or 2 programs per hour, so a program still costs him 1 report. His opportunity costs did not change, so comparative advantage did not change. 6 points 23. Demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = 2 + Q. The government puts a $2 per-unit tax on the good. Show your work.

The subsidy. Under the ceiling, neither curve moves, so only the quantity supplied at the ceiling rent gets rented, and there is a shortage. Renters' surplus is the area under demand and above the ceiling rent, but only out to that smaller quantity (and less if the units go to people who value them less). Under the subsidy, supply shifts down until the market rent equals the same low level, and the quantity rented rises to the quantity demanded at that rent. Renters pay the same rent on more units, so consumer surplus is larger by the surplus on the extra units.

Full credit: the subsidy; the ceiling limits the quantity to quantity supplied (a shortage), while the subsidy delivers more units at the same rent.

2 points 34. Maya's last coffee gives her 30 units of marginal utility and costs $3. Her last sandwich gives 40 and costs $5. Is she maximizing utility? If not, what should she change? Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2023 final MC 27 and Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q3

Show answer

No. MU per dollar is 30 ÷ 3 = 10 for coffee and 40 ÷ 5 = 8 for sandwiches, so the rational spending rule fails. She should move spending from sandwiches to coffee. Buying more coffee lowers its MU and buying fewer sandwiches raises theirs, until the two ratios are equal.

Full credit: 10 versus 8 per dollar; shift toward coffee until MU ÷ P is equal.

2 points 35. A coffee shop cuts its price by 10%, and the quantity it sells rises by 25%. Find the price elasticity of demand and the percent change in total revenue. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q1

Show answer

Full credit: −2.5 (elastic) and revenue up 12.5%.

2 points 37. A new chip design cuts the cost of making laptops. At the same time, students' incomes rise, and laptops are a good people buy more of when they get richer. In the laptop market,

(i) The price falls and the quantity rises
(ii) The quantity rises, and the price could rise, fall, or stay the same
(iii) The price rises, and the quantity could rise or fall
(iv) Both the price and the quantity rise

Show answer

(ii). Take the two events one at a time. The cheaper chip is a seller story, so supply shifts right: on its own that lowers the price and raises the quantity. Higher income is a buyer story, so demand shifts right: on its own that raises the price and raises the quantity. Both push quantity up, so quantity surely rises. They push the price in opposite directions, so the price depends on which shift is bigger.

A check with numbers: start with demand P = 10 − Q and supply P = 2 + Q, so Q = 4 and P = $6. If demand moves to P = 12 − Q and supply to P = Q, then 12 − Q = Q gives Q = 6 and P = $6, so the price did not change. If supply only moves to P = 1 + Q, then 12 − Q = 1 + Q gives Q = 5.5 and P = $6.50, so the price rose.

Option (i) is right only if the supply shift is bigger, and option (iv) only if the demand shift is bigger; the question does not say. Option (iii) has it backward: the quantity is the certain part and the price is the uncertain part.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 38. Over one year, the price of cocoa beans doubles while the quantity of cocoa sold falls only slightly. Which event best explains this?

(i) Chocolate becomes more fashionable, so the demand for cocoa rises
(ii) A plant disease destroys part of the crop, and the demand for cocoa is inelastic
(iii) A plant disease destroys part of the crop, and the demand for cocoa is elastic
(iv) A cheap cocoa substitute appears, so the demand for cocoa falls

Show answer

(ii). Work backward from the outcome. The price rose and the quantity fell, so they moved in opposite directions, which means supply shifted, and here it shifted left. The price change is big and the quantity change is small, so the curve the market slid along, demand, must be steep, which means inelastic.

Option (i) is a demand increase, which would raise both the price and the quantity, but the quantity fell. Option (iii) has the right shift, but with elastic (flat) demand a supply cut shows up mostly as a fall in quantity, with only a small price rise. Option (iv) is a demand decrease, which would lower the price. This is the same reasoning as reading the egg price spikes as a supply shock on the Fall 2025 midterm Q2(f).

Full credit: circle (ii).

6 points 39. Demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = 2 + Q, the market in the graphs above. With no policy, Q = 4, P = $6, CS = 8, and PS = 8. Show your work.

(a) (2 points) The city sets a price ceiling of $4. How many units are sold, and how big is the shortage?
(b) (2 points) Find CS, PS, and the deadweight loss under the ceiling. Who gains and who loses compared with no ceiling? Describe the graph you would draw.
(c) (2 points) Suppose instead the city sets a price floor of $8. How many units are sold, how big is the excess supply, and what is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

(a) At $4, quantity demanded is 10 − 4 = 6 and quantity supplied is 4 − 2 = 2. Sellers cannot be forced to sell more than they want, so 2 units are sold. The shortage is 6 − 2 = 4.

(b) Consumer surplus is the area under demand and above $4, out to Q = 2. At Q = 2 demand is 10 − 2 = 8. Split the area into a rectangle, 2 × (8 − 4) = 8, and a triangle on top, ½ × 2 × (10 − 8) = 2, so CS = 10. Producer surplus is the triangle above supply and below $4: ½ × 2 × (4 − 2) = 2. At Q = 2 supply is 2 + 2 = 4, so the deadweight loss is the triangle from Q = 2 to Q = 4 between demand and supply: ½ × (4 − 2) × (8 − 4) = 4. Check: 10 + 2 + 4 = 16, the old total of 8 + 8. Buyers who still find a unit gain (CS rises from 8 to 10), sellers lose (PS falls from 8 to 2), and society loses 4. Graph: a horizontal line at $4 below the crossing, Qs = 2 and Qd = 6 marked with dotted lines, the shortage bracketed between them, and the deadweight loss triangle between the curves from 2 to 4, pointing at the old equilibrium (4, $6).

(c) At $8, quantity demanded is 10 − 8 = 2 and quantity supplied is 8 − 2 = 6. Buyers cannot be forced to buy, so 2 units are sold, and the excess supply is 6 − 2 = 4. CS = ½ × 2 × (10 − 8) = 2. PS is a rectangle, 2 × (8 − 4) = 8, plus a triangle, ½ × 2 × (4 − 2) = 2, so PS = 10. The deadweight loss is again ½ × (4 − 2) × (8 − 4) = 4, and 2 + 10 + 4 = 16 checks. This time sellers who still sell gain and buyers lose.

Full credit: (a) 2 sold, shortage 4; (b) CS 10, PS 2, DWL 4, with buyers gaining and sellers losing; (c) 2 sold, excess supply 4, DWL 4.

2 points 40. The government needs to raise money with a per-unit tax and wants the smallest possible deadweight loss. It should tax a good

(i) With many close substitutes, because buyers can easily switch away
(ii) With few substitutes, which people keep buying when its price rises
(iii) With the highest price, because the deadweight loss is the same for every good
(iv) With very elastic demand and very elastic supply

Show answer

(ii). The deadweight loss is the surplus on the units that stop being traded because of the tax. If people keep buying almost the same amount, few trades are lost, so the triangle is thin. His Class 6 slide 61 says it directly: "Taxing inelastic goods is more efficient." Options (i) and (iv) describe elastic markets, where the tax cuts the quantity a lot and the triangle is big. Option (iii) is wrong because the deadweight loss depends on how much the quantity falls, not on how high the price is.

Full credit: circle (ii).

4 points 41. Demand for concert tickets is P = 300 − 10Q, with Q in millions of tickets. Use elasticity = (1 ÷ slope) × (P ÷ Q).

(a) (2 points) Find the price elasticity of demand at Q = 10. Is demand elastic or inelastic there?
(b) (2 points) Find it at Q = 20. Explain why one straight line gives two different answers.

Show answer

(a) The slope is −10: the price falls $10 for each extra million tickets. At Q = 10, P = 300 − 10 × 10 = $200. Elasticity = (1 ÷ −10) × (200 ÷ 10) = −0.1 × 20 = −2. Its size is above 1, so demand is relatively elastic there. A check with percent changes: a 1% higher price, $202, gives Q = (300 − 202) ÷ 10 = 9.8, which is 2% fewer tickets, and −2% ÷ 1% = −2.

(b) At Q = 20, P = 300 − 200 = $100. Elasticity = (1 ÷ −10) × (100 ÷ 20) = −0.1 × 5 = −0.5, so demand is relatively inelastic there. The slope is the same everywhere on the line, but P ÷ Q is not: it is 20 at the first point and 5 at the second. Near the top of the line the price is high and the quantity small, so a $1 change is a small percent of the price but a large percent of the quantity. His Class 6 slide 24 notes that elasticity is generally not constant along a demand curve. (On this line it equals exactly −1 at the middle, Q = 15 and P = $150.)

Full credit: (a) −2, elastic, with the slope and P ÷ Q shown; (b) −0.5, inelastic, because P ÷ Q changes along the line even though the slope does not.

2 points 42. A bakery is the only seller of a famous pie. It sells 8 pies a day at $15 each. To sell a ninth pie each day, it must lower its price to $14 on every pie. The marginal revenue of the ninth pie is

(i) $14, the price of the ninth pie
(ii) $6
(iii) $15
(iv) $126

Show answer

(ii). Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue. Before: 8 × $15 = $120. After: 9 × $14 = $126. MR = $126 − $120 = $6. Another way to see it: the ninth pie brings in $14, but the first 8 pies now sell for $1 less, which costs 8 × $1 = $8, and $14 − $8 = $6. Option (i) is what a price taker would get, because it never has to cut its price; it ignores the cut on the first 8 pies. Option (iii) is the old price. Option (iv) is the new total revenue, not the change in it.

Full credit: circle (ii).

8 points 43. A firm holds the only patent on a farm pesticide. Demand is P = 120 − 2Q, with Q in thousands of gallons per year and P in dollars per gallon. Marginal cost is MC = 2Q, a line from the origin, and fixed costs are zero. Show your work.

(a) (2 points) Find the monopoly quantity and price.
(b) (2 points) Find the monopolist's profit.
(c) (2 points) Find the competitive quantity and price, and the deadweight loss of monopoly.
(d) (2 points) Pesticide runoff also does $40 of damage to neighbors for every gallon used. Is the monopoly quantity too high, too low, or efficient? Explain.

Show answer

(a) MR has the same intercept and twice the slope: MR = 120 − 4Q. Set MR = MC: 120 − 4Q = 2Q, so 6Q = 120 and Q = 20. Price from demand: P = 120 − 2 × 20 = $80.

(b) Total revenue = $80 × 20 = 1,600. With zero fixed costs, total cost is the area under MC from 0 to 20. MC at Q = 20 is 2 × 20 = $40, so that area is a triangle: ½ × 20 × 40 = 400. Profit = 1,600 − 400 = 1,200, which is $1.2 million because Q is in thousands. The rectangle (80 − 40) × 20 = 800 is the shortcut the Fall 2025 midterm key also accepted on Q3(f), but it charges every unit the $40 cost of the last one, so it overstates cost by the triangle of 400.

(c) Competition produces where demand meets MC: 120 − 2Q = 2Q, so 4Q = 120, Q = 30, and P = 120 − 60 = $60. DWL = ½ × (30 − 20) × (80 − 40) = ½ × 10 × 40 = 200, or $200,000. These are the units buyers value above their cost that the monopolist does not sell.

(d) Efficient. Social marginal cost is SMC = 2Q + 40. The efficient quantity is where demand meets SMC: 120 − 2Q = 2Q + 40, so 4Q = 80 and Q* = 20. That is exactly the monopoly quantity. A competitive market would make 30 and overproduce, but the monopolist's cut in output happens to offset the pollution. Problem Set 2 Q3(f) had the same "lucky coincidence." The price is still high, which moves surplus from buyers to the firm, but total surplus is as large as it can be.

Full credit: (a) Q = 20, P = $80 from MR = MC; (b) profit 1,200 (thousand dollars) from TR − area under MC; (c) Q = 30, P = $60, DWL 200; (d) efficient, because demand meets SMC = 2Q + 40 at Q = 20.

2 points 44. A farmer keeps bees for honey. The bees also pollinate the neighbors' fruit trees, and nobody pays the farmer for that. Without any policy, the market for honey

(i) Produces too much honey, so a per-unit tax equal to the neighbors' benefit fixes it
(ii) Produces too little honey, because social marginal benefit is above private marginal benefit, so a per-unit subsidy equal to the neighbors' benefit fixes it
(iii) Produces the efficient amount, because the farmer sells the honey at the market price
(iv) Produces too little honey, so a price ceiling on honey fixes it

Show answer

(ii). The pollination is a benefit to people outside the market, so it is a positive externality. Social marginal benefit equals private marginal benefit plus that external benefit, so SMB lies above demand, and the efficient quantity, where SMB meets SMC, is larger than the market quantity. A per-unit subsidy equal to the external benefit makes the farmer count it (Class 11 slide 35). Option (i) describes a negative externality. Option (iii) ignores the neighbors, who are not part of the honey sale. Option (iv) gets the direction right but the fix wrong: a ceiling lowers the price sellers receive, so they make even less.

Full credit: circle (ii).

4 points 45. Robinson joins Bill and Chris on the island. Per hour, Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut, Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts, and Robinson catches 2 fish or gathers 2 coconuts. Each works 10 hours a day. Adapted from Problem Set 1 Q2

(a) (2 points) Following comparative advantage, who gathers coconuts and who fishes? Explain.
(b) (2 points) With fish on the vertical axis and coconuts on the horizontal axis, describe their joint PPC with specialization: both intercepts, the kink, and the slope of each piece. How does it compare with the PPC if everyone splits time the same way?

Show answer

(a) The opportunity cost of one coconut is fish per hour ÷ coconuts per hour. Bill: 1 ÷ 1 = 1 fish. Chris: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 fish. Robinson: 2 ÷ 2 = 1 fish. Bill and Robinson tie for the lowest cost, so both gather coconuts and Chris fishes. Robinson is better than Bill at both tasks, but that is absolute advantage and does not matter here.

(b) Fish intercept (everyone fishes): 10 × 1 + 10 × 8 + 10 × 2 = 110 fish. Coconut intercept (everyone gathers): 10 × 1 + 10 × 2 + 10 × 2 = 50 coconuts. Starting at 110 fish, Bill and Robinson switch first. Each coconut costs 1 fish, so the first piece has slope −1 and runs until they gather 10 + 20 = 30 coconuts, with fish down to 110 − 30 = 80. That is the kink, at (30 coconuts, 80 fish). Then Chris switches: each coconut costs 4 fish, so the second piece has slope −4 and runs from (30, 80) to (50, 0), and 80 ÷ 20 = 4 checks. If everyone splits time the same way, the PPC is the straight line from 110 to 50 with slope −110 ÷ 50 = −2.2. The specialized PPC shares both intercepts and bows out above that line everywhere in between.

Full credit: (a) Bill and Robinson gather coconuts and Chris fishes, because Bill's and Robinson's opportunity cost of a coconut (1 fish) is below Chris's (4 fish); (b) intercepts 110 and 50, one kink at (30, 80), slopes −1 then −4, outside the straight line of slope −2.2.

4 points 46. Two markets, two long runs.

(a) (2 points) Coffee carts near campus are a competitive industry with identical carts. At today's price of $5 a cup, each cart's average total cost is $4 a cup at the amount it sells. What happens in the long run to the number of carts, market supply, the price, and each cart's profit?
(b) (2 points) A drug company with a patent earns large profits for years, and no rival enters. Why not? Name two other barriers to entry from his slides.

Show answer

(a) Profit per cart = (P − atc) × q = ($5 − $4) × q, which is positive. Because costs here include opportunity costs, this is profit beyond what the owners could earn elsewhere, so new carts enter. Entry shifts market supply right, and the price falls. Entry continues until the price equals average total cost and each cart's profit is zero, at P = mc = atc (Class 9 slides 46 and 47).

(b) The patent is a legal barrier to entry: rivals are not allowed to make the same drug, so entry cannot push its price down. His Class 10 slide 10 lists the main barriers as high fixed costs (such as building a railway), patents and other legal protections, and licensing or other gatekeeping. Slide 41 adds that a monopolist "can make positive economic profits in the long run" only because such barriers exist.

Full credit: (a) carts enter, supply shifts right, the price falls to atc, and profit goes to zero; (b) the patent blocks entry; the other two barriers are high fixed costs and licensing or gatekeeping.

One-minute review

Quick check

A rent ceiling is set above the equilibrium rent. What happens?

Nothing, because a ceiling only binds when it is below the price the market would reach anyway.

The price rises 10% and the quantity demanded falls 5%. Elasticity, and what happens to spending?

−5% ÷ 10% = −0.5, inelastic, so spending rises (1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045).

MR = MC at 10 million tickets, where MC is $100 and demand is $200. What price does the monopolist charge?

$200, because the price is read off demand at the chosen quantity, not off MC.

Each unit causes $3 of harm to neighbors. What per-unit Pigouvian tax gets the market to the efficient quantity?

$3, equal to the external marginal cost, so supply rises to social marginal cost.

Your last apple gives 4 units of MU per dollar and your last banana gives 6. What do you change?

Buy more bananas and fewer apples, until diminishing MU makes the two ratios equal.

A crop is planted and cannot be stored, and then export demand disappears. What happens to price and quantity?

The quantity stays the same and the price falls a lot, because short-run supply is vertical.

  • Choose where MB = MC. In a market, demand is MB and supply is MC.
  • Comparative advantage means a lower opportunity cost. Specialize in what you do best relative to your other options.
  • Buyer stories shift demand; seller stories shift supply; the good's own price only moves you along a curve.
  • CS is below demand and above price; PS is above supply and below price; competition maximizes their sum.
  • Inelastic means steep. With inelastic demand, a price rise raises spending.
  • Binding ceiling: sold = Qs, shortage. Binding floor: sold = Qd, excess supply. Both leave a DWL.
  • A tax is a wedge; the less elastic side bears more of it, and the DWL is smaller when the market is inelastic.
  • MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb.
  • Competitive firm: P = MC, and entry pushes long-run profit to zero. Monopoly: MR = MC, price off demand, so it raises price and reduces quantity; barriers to entry protect its profit.
  • SMC = PMC + EMC. Negative externality means overproduction; a Pigouvian tax equal to EMC fixes it.
  • Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, so a lower output price shifts labor demand in.
