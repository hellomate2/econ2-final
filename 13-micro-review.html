Midterm material refresher

The micro ideas from the first half that the final still asks about, in one compact page. On the Fall 2025 final, three of the six multiple-choice items were midterm material (marginal benefit equals marginal cost, comparative advantage, monopoly), one short answer was a marginal cost argument, and the trade question leaned on supply and demand. For full depth on any topic, follow the link to the matching unit of the midterm site.

The ideas, explained

Abbreviations used on this page: MB is marginal benefit, MC is marginal cost, MR is marginal revenue, MU is marginal utility, CS is consumer surplus, PS is producer surplus, DWL is deadweight loss, PPC is the production possibilities curve, P is price, and Q is quantity.

1. Marginal benefit equals marginal cost

Every choice in this course is made at the margin: you compare the benefit of one more unit with the cost of one more unit. Keep going while MB is greater than MC, and stop where they are equal. A market does the same thing for a whole society, because the demand curve shows buyers' MB and the supply curve shows sellers' MC, so the equilibrium quantity is where MB = MC.

Costs here are opportunity costs: the value of the next best thing you give up. The cost of a year of college includes the earnings you give up by not working, and an hour of your time is worth roughly what you could earn in that hour. Depth: midterm unit 01.

Do more of something while MB > MC. Stop where MB = MC. In a market, demand is MB and supply is MC.

2. Comparative advantage

You have a comparative advantage in a task when your opportunity cost of doing it is lower than someone else's. You have an absolute advantage when you can simply produce more of it per hour. Gains from trade come from comparative advantage, so even someone who is faster at everything should specialize in the task where their edge is biggest and trade for the rest.

The class example: per hour, Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut, and Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts. Chris is faster at both. But a coconut costs Chris 4 fish and costs Bill only 1 fish, so Bill should gather coconuts and Chris should fish. On the Fall 2025 midterm, a new fishhook doubled both people's fish per hour. A coconut then cost Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish. Both opportunity costs doubled, so Bill's is still the lower one and nobody switches tasks. The same holds if a tool doubles one person's output of both goods: that person's opportunity costs do not change at all. Depth: midterm unit 02.

Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. Specialize in the task you do best relative to your other options, not in every task you do better than others.

3. Supply and demand shifts

A change in the good's own price moves you along a curve. Anything else that changes buyers' willingness to pay shifts demand, and anything else that changes sellers' costs shifts supply. Higher income, a pricier substitute, a cheaper complement, or a fashion boom shifts demand right. Better technology, cheaper inputs, or more sellers shift supply right. Taxes, costlier inputs, or a lost harvest shift supply left.

First decide which curve the story moves. A story about buyers moves only demand, and a story about sellers moves only supply. Depth: midterm unit 03.

4. Surplus and efficiency

Consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price, because each buyer pays the market price but would have paid up to their MB. Producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price, because each seller receives the price but only needed to cover that unit's MC. Total surplus is their sum. A competitive market with no market failure produces the quantity where MB = MC, which makes total surplus as large as it can be.

Any policy that pushes the quantity away from MB = MC creates a deadweight loss, a triangle that points toward the efficient quantity. A price ceiling below equilibrium is the classic case: neither curve shifts, the quantity traded falls to the quantity supplied, and there is a shortage equal to quantity demanded minus quantity supplied. Depth: midterm unit 04.

5. Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand measures how much quantity responds to price: elasticity = (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P). If its size is above 1, demand is elastic; below 1, inelastic. His midterm key used simple percent changes measured from the starting values, kept the negative sign, and said "relatively elastic" or "relatively inelastic" depending on whether the size was above or below 1, so write your answers the same way. Demand is inelastic when there are no close substitutes, when the good is a necessity, or in the short run. On a graph, inelastic means steep and elastic means flat.

Elasticity tells you what happens to spending. When demand is inelastic, a price increase raises total spending, because quantity falls by a smaller percent than price rises. When demand is elastic, a price increase lowers spending. Supply can be inelastic too: once a crop is planted, the short-run supply is close to vertical, which is the setup of the Fall 2025 soybean question. Depth: midterm unit 05.

Inelastic means steep. Elastic means flat. Elasticity = (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P), with Q on top.

6. Taxes

A per-unit tax drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. The wedge equals the tax and sits at the new, smaller quantity. Buyers pay the price read off demand; sellers keep the price read off supply. It does not matter which side sends the money to the government: the result is the same.

The side that is less elastic (steeper) bears more of the tax, because it cannot easily walk away. Depth: midterm unit 06.

7. Households: the rational spending rule

A household gets the most out of its budget when the last dollar spent on each good brings the same marginal utility: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb. If good a gives more MU per dollar, buy more of a and less of b. Marginal utility falls as you consume more, so buying more of a lowers its MU until the two ratios match. Edwards put this rule on his Fall 2025 final review slides. Depth: midterm unit 07.

8. Competitive firms

A competitive firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it wants at the market price, so its MR equals the price. It maximizes profit by producing where P = MC. That makes each firm's MC curve its supply curve, and the market supply curve is the sum of firms' MC curves. Anything that lowers every firm's MC, such as better technology or cheaper inputs, shifts market supply right. Depth: midterm unit 08.

9. Monopoly

A monopolist is the only seller, so it faces the whole market demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price on every unit, so its MR is below the price. With a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same intercept and is twice as steep. The monopolist picks the quantity where MR = MC, then charges the highest price buyers will pay for that quantity, read straight up on the demand curve.

Monopoly: find Q where MR = MC, then go up to demand for P. Compared with competition, a monopolist raises price and reduces quantity, and at its quantity MB > MC.

Depth: midterm unit 09.

10. Externalities and Pigouvian taxes

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside the market. With a negative externality such as pollution, social marginal cost (SMC) equals private marginal cost (PMC) plus external marginal cost (EMC), so SMC sits above the supply curve. The market produces where demand meets private cost, which is too much. The efficient quantity Q* is where social marginal benefit equals SMC.

A Pigouvian tax equal to the external marginal cost at Q* moves supply up to SMC, so buyers and sellers now face the full cost and the market lands on Q*. A positive externality, such as a vaccine, works the other way: the market produces too little, and a subsidy fixes it. Depth: midterm unit 10.

SMC = PMC + EMC. Negative externality: the market overproduces. Pigouvian tax per unit = EMC at the efficient quantity.

11. The bridge to the second half: labor demand

The micro tools come back in the labor unit. A firm hires workers up to where the wage equals the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MPL (marginal product of labor) is the extra output from one more worker. For a competitive firm MR is the output price, so anything that lowers the price of the good, such as losing an export market, shifts labor demand in. That was Fall 2025 final Q4(c). Full treatment is in Labor and wages.

Worked examples

Example: the rational spending rule from his review slides

Coffee costs $4 and frozen yogurt costs $6. The first coffee gives 10 units of MU and the second gives 8. The first frozen yogurt gives 12 and the second gives 8. You have $14 a day. What do you buy?

  1. Compute MU per dollar for each unit. Coffee: 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5, then 8 ÷ 4 = 2. Frozen yogurt: 12 ÷ 6 = 2, then 8 ÷ 6 = 1.33.
  2. Buy units in order of MU per dollar: first coffee (2.5) for $4.
  3. Next the second coffee (2) and the first frozen yogurt (2) tie. Buying both costs $4 + $6 = $10, for a total of $14, the whole budget.
  4. Check the rule: last coffee 8 ÷ $4 = 2 and last frozen yogurt 12 ÷ $6 = 2. The ratios match.

Answer: 2 coffees and 1 frozen yogurt, because that spends the budget and equalizes MU per dollar at 2. (The slide shows the bundle and the 8 ÷ $4 = 12 ÷ $6 check; the $14 budget is implied by it.)

Example: monopoly versus competition with Problem Set 2 numbers

Demand is P = 5 − Q and MC is constant at $1. Compare a competitive market with a monopoly.

  1. Competition: P = MC, so 5 − Q = 1, giving Q = 4 and P = $1.
  2. Monopoly: MR has the same intercept and twice the slope, so MR = 5 − 2Q. Set MR = MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2.
  3. Read the price off demand: P = 5 − 2 = $3.
  4. Deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and MC from Q = 2 to Q = 4, which is ½ × (4 − 2) × (3 − 1) = 2. Profit is (3 − 1) × 2 = 4.

Answer: the monopolist charges $3 instead of $1 and sells 2 instead of 4: it raises price and reduces quantity, with a deadweight loss of 2.

Example: elasticity and spending

A venue raises ticket prices from $50 to $60 and sales fall from 1,000 to 700.

  1. Percent change in price: (60 − 50) ÷ 50 = +20%.
  2. Percent change in quantity: (700 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = −30%.
  3. Elasticity: −30% ÷ 20% = −1.5. The size is above 1, so demand is elastic.
  4. Spending: $50 × 1,000 = $50,000 before and $60 × 700 = $42,000 after.

Answer: elasticity is −1.5 (elastic), so the price increase lowers total spending, from $50,000 to $42,000.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "Comparative advantage means I should do every task I am better at."

Correct: That is absolute advantage. Comparative advantage compares opportunity costs, and you specialize in the task where yours is lowest. This was the trap option in Fall 2025 final Q1(b).

Mistake: Reading the monopoly price at the point where MR crosses MC.

Correct: MR = MC gives the quantity. The price comes from going straight up to the demand curve at that quantity. In the Swift graph, MR = MC at $100, but the price is $200.

Mistake: Saying a price change shifts the demand curve.

Correct: A change in the good's own price is a movement along the curve. Only other factors, such as income or the price of a related good, shift it.

Mistake: Putting the price change on top of the elasticity fraction.

Correct: Quantity goes on top: (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P).

Mistake: Thinking the efficient amount of pollution is zero.

Correct: The efficient amount is where SMB = SMC, which is usually a positive amount. The Pigouvian tax gets you there; it does not ban the activity.

Mistake: Shifting both curves for a one-sided story.

Correct: A buyer story moves demand and a seller story moves supply. The new equilibrium then slides along the curve that did not move.

Practice questions

Questions 1 to 20 are multiple choice in the style of Fall 2025 final Q1: circle the one correct answer, 2 points each. Questions 21 to 28 are short answers in the style of Q2, 2 points per part.

2 points 1. A farmer can plant one more acre. The acre would add $500 of revenue and cost $420 in seed, labor, and water. The farmer should

(i) Not plant, because profit per acre is lower on the extra acre
(ii) Plant, because the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost
(iii) Plant only if the average cost of all acres is below $420
(iv) Not plant, because $420 is a large cost

Show answer

(ii). The decision is about the extra acre only. It adds $500 and costs $420, so MB > MC and planting raises profit by $80. Averages and totals do not matter for a marginal decision.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 2. The opportunity cost of a year of college includes

(i) Tuition only
(ii) Tuition and rent
(iii) Tuition and the earnings you give up by not working
(iv) Rent and food only

Show answer

(iii). Opportunity cost is what you give up. You give up the tuition money and the paycheck you could have earned. Rent and food are paid whether or not you go to college, so they cancel out.

Full credit: circle (iii).

2 points 3. In one hour, Amy can write 4 reports or code 2 programs. Ben can write 1 report or code 1 program. Which is true?

(i) Amy should do both tasks because she is faster at both
(ii) Amy should write reports and Ben should code
(iii) Ben should write reports and Amy should code
(iv) There are no gains from trade because Amy is better at both

Show answer

(ii). A program costs Amy 2 reports (4 ÷ 2) and costs Ben 1 report. Ben has the lower opportunity cost of programs, so he codes. A report costs Amy half a program and costs Ben 1 program, so Amy writes reports. Amy has the absolute advantage in both, which does not decide who does what.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 4. Continuing question 3, a new laptop doubles Ben's output of both reports and programs. Now

(i) Ben has the comparative advantage in reports
(ii) Who should do which task does not change
(iii) Amy should do both tasks
(iv) The gains from trade disappear

Show answer

(ii). Ben now makes 2 reports or 2 programs per hour, so a program still costs him 1 report. His opportunity costs did not change, so comparative advantage did not change. The fishhook on the Fall 2025 midterm taught the same lesson from a different angle: it doubled both people's fish, both opportunity costs doubled, and Bill still had the lower one, so nobody switched.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 5. A frost destroys a third of Florida's orange crop. In the market for orange juice,

(i) Demand falls and the price falls
(ii) Supply falls, the price rises, and the quantity falls
(iii) Supply falls, the price rises, and the quantity rises
(iv) Both curves shift and the price does not change

Show answer

(ii). The frost hits sellers, so supply shifts left. Moving up along the unchanged demand curve, the price rises and the quantity falls.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 6. The price of coffee rises and people buy less coffee. This is

(i) A leftward shift of the demand for coffee
(ii) A movement along the demand curve for coffee
(iii) A rightward shift of the supply of coffee
(iv) A decrease in demand caused by the price

Show answer

(ii). A change in the good's own price moves you along its demand curve. Calling it a "decrease in demand" is the classic wording trap: the quantity demanded fell, not demand.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 7. Gasoline prices rise sharply. The demand for large, fuel-hungry SUVs

(i) Shifts right, because SUVs become more valuable
(ii) Shifts left, because gasoline is a complement to SUVs
(iii) Does not change, because SUVs are a different good
(iv) Moves along the curve to a lower price

Show answer

(ii). Gasoline and SUVs are used together, so they are complements. When a complement gets more expensive, owning an SUV costs more, so buyers' willingness to pay for SUVs falls and SUV demand shifts left.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 8. Producer surplus is the area

(i) Below the demand curve and above the price
(ii) Above the supply curve and below the price
(iii) Between demand and supply to the right of equilibrium
(iv) Under the supply curve

Show answer

(ii). Sellers receive the price on every unit but only need to cover that unit's MC, which is the height of supply. The gaps add up to the area above supply and below the price. Option (i) is consumer surplus.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 9. In a competitive market with no externalities, the equilibrium quantity

(i) Maximizes producer surplus
(ii) Maximizes consumer surplus
(iii) Maximizes total surplus
(iv) Creates a deadweight loss

Show answer

(iii). At equilibrium MB = MC. Every unit worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller gets made, and no unit that costs more than it is worth gets made, so total surplus is as large as possible. A monopoly would raise producer surplus by lowering total surplus.

Full credit: circle (iii).

2 points 10. A city sets a rent ceiling below the equilibrium rent. The result is

(i) Supply shifts left until the market clears
(ii) A shortage equal to quantity demanded minus quantity supplied, with neither curve shifting
(iii) A surplus of apartments
(iv) No effect, because ceilings only matter above equilibrium

Show answer

(ii). A ceiling below equilibrium is binding. At the low rent, more people want apartments than landlords offer, so there is a shortage. The curves do not move; the price is just not allowed to reach the crossing point.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 11. The price of a good rises 20% and the quantity demanded falls 10%. The price elasticity of demand is

(i) −2, elastic, so total spending falls
(ii) −0.5, inelastic, so total spending rises
(iii) −0.5, inelastic, so total spending falls
(iv) −2, elastic, so total spending rises

Show answer

(ii). Elasticity = −10% ÷ 20% = −0.5, which is below 1 in size, so demand is inelastic. The price rose by more than the quantity fell, so spending rises: 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08, an 8% increase.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 12. A per-unit tax is placed on a good with very inelastic demand and very elastic supply. Who bears most of the tax?

(i) Sellers, because they send the money to the government
(ii) Buyers, because they are the less elastic side
(iii) It is always split evenly
(iv) Nobody, because the price adjusts

Show answer

(ii). The side that cannot easily walk away bears more of the tax. Buyers with steep demand keep buying even at a higher price, so the price they pay rises by almost the whole tax. Who writes the check does not matter.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 13. A crop has been harvested, cannot be stored, and must be sold this season. Its short-run supply curve is

(i) Horizontal
(ii) Vertical
(iii) The same as its demand curve
(iv) Downward sloping

Show answer

(ii). The quantity is fixed no matter what the price is, so supply is a vertical line. Any fall in demand then shows up entirely in the price, which is what happened to soybeans in Fall 2025 final Q4(b).

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 14. A price-taking firm maximizes profit by producing where

(i) The price equals marginal cost
(ii) The price equals average cost
(iii) Total revenue is as large as possible
(iv) Marginal cost is as low as possible

Show answer

(i). For a price taker, MR is the market price. Profit is largest where MR = MC, so where P = MC.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points 15. A monopolist chooses its quantity where

(i) Price equals marginal cost
(ii) Marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and then charges the price on the demand curve at that quantity
(iii) Marginal revenue equals zero
(iv) Demand equals marginal cost

Show answer

(ii). Every firm maximizes profit where MR = MC. The monopolist then charges the most buyers will pay for that quantity, read off demand. Options (i) and (iv) describe the competitive outcome.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 16. At the quantity a monopolist chooses,

(i) MB = MC
(ii) MB > MC
(iii) MB < MC
(iv) MB = 0

Show answer

(ii). The monopolist stops short of the efficient quantity, so buyers would still pay more for one more unit than it costs to make. That unexploited gap is the deadweight loss. His review slide caption says exactly "At Q1, MB > MC."

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 17. A factory's smoke harms the people who live nearby. Without any policy, the market for the factory's product produces

(i) More than the efficient quantity, because social marginal cost is above private marginal cost
(ii) Less than the efficient quantity, because social marginal cost is above private marginal cost
(iii) The efficient quantity, because the market maximizes surplus
(iv) Zero, because pollution is banned

Show answer

(i). The factory pays only its private cost and ignores the harm to neighbors. SMC lies above supply, so the efficient quantity, where demand meets SMC, is smaller than the market quantity. The market overproduces.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points 18. The Pigouvian tax that gets this market to the efficient quantity is a per-unit tax equal to

(i) The firm's total profit
(ii) The external marginal cost at the efficient quantity
(iii) The market price
(iv) The private marginal cost

Show answer

(ii). The tax shifts supply up by the size of the external cost, so the supply curve the market faces becomes SMC. Producers then make the efficient quantity on their own.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points 19. You notice that MUpizza ÷ Ppizza is greater than MUtacos ÷ Ptacos. To raise your total utility with the same budget, you should

(i) Buy more pizza and fewer tacos
(ii) Buy fewer pizzas and more tacos
(iii) Ask for a lower taco price
(iv) Keep the same bundle, because you are already spending the whole budget

Show answer

(i). A dollar moved from tacos to pizza buys more utility. As you buy more pizza, its MU falls, and as you buy fewer tacos, their MU rises, until the two ratios are equal.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points 20. Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL. If the price farmers receive for almonds falls, the demand for almond farm workers

(i) Shifts right
(ii) Shifts left
(iii) Does not change, because workers are just as productive
(iv) Moves along the curve to a higher wage

Show answer

(ii). MPL did not change, but MR fell, so the value of each worker's output (MRPL) is lower at every level of employment. Labor demand shifts left, and the wage and employment both fall. Same logic as the soybean workers in Fall 2025 final Q4(c).

Full credit: circle (ii).

6 points 21. A virus kills a large share of egg-laying hens. Eggs have no close substitute in most recipes.

(a) (2 points) Which curve shifts, and which way? Explain.
(b) (2 points) Does the price of eggs rise by a lot or a little compared with the drop in quantity? Explain using elasticity.
(c) (2 points) Does total spending on eggs rise or fall? Explain.

Show answer

(a) Supply shifts left (up). The virus raises the cost of producing eggs and removes hens, which is a change on the seller side. Demand does not move, because buyers' willingness to pay did not change.

(b) The price rises a lot and the quantity falls only a little. With no close substitute, demand is inelastic, which means steep. When supply shifts along a steep demand curve, most of the adjustment shows up in the price.

(c) Spending rises. With inelastic demand, the percent increase in price is bigger than the percent drop in quantity, so price times quantity goes up.

Full credit: (a) supply shifts left, with a seller-side reason; (b) "inelastic" or "steep" demand, so a large price increase; (c) spending rises because demand is inelastic.

4 points 22. A battery factory installs robots, and its marginal cost per battery falls. A barbershop down the street cannot automate haircuts.

(a) (2 points) What happens to the market supply of batteries, and to the price and quantity of batteries? Explain.
(b) (2 points) Over many years, which would you expect to get more expensive relative to other goods, batteries or haircuts? Explain using marginal cost.

Show answer

(a) Supply shifts right, because market supply is the sum of firms' MC curves and MC fell. Moving down the demand curve, the price of batteries falls and the quantity rises.

(b) Haircuts. A haircut needs a barber's time and a shop, so its MC is tied to wages and rent, which rise over time. Batteries get cheaper as machines raise output per worker. This is the same reasoning as the Big Mac in Fall 2025 final Q2(b).

Full credit: (a) supply right, price down, quantity up, because MC fell; (b) haircuts, because the work cannot be automated, so MC does not fall.

6 points 23. Demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = 2 + Q. The government puts a $2 per-unit tax on the good. Show your work.

(a) (2 points) Find the quantity, the price buyers pay, and the price sellers keep after the tax.
(b) (2 points) Find the tax revenue and the deadweight loss.
(c) (2 points) Who bears more of the tax, and why?

Show answer

(a) Without the tax: 10 − Q = 2 + Q, so Q = 4 and P = $6. With the tax, the buyers' price minus the sellers' price must equal 2: (10 − Q) − (2 + Q) = 2, so 8 − 2Q = 2 and Q = 3. Buyers pay 10 − 3 = $7. Sellers keep 2 + 3 = $5.

(b) Revenue = $2 × 3 = $6. Deadweight loss = ½ × 2 × (4 − 3) = 1.

(c) They split it evenly: buyers pay $1 more than before ($7 instead of $6) and sellers keep $1 less ($5 instead of $6). The two curves are equally steep, so neither side is less elastic than the other.

Full credit: Q = 3, $7 and $5; revenue 6 and DWL 1; an even split because the slopes are equal in size.

6 points 24. A town has one water company. Demand for water is P = 5 − Q, and the marginal cost is a constant $1. Show your work.

(a) (2 points) Find the monopoly quantity and price.
(b) (2 points) Find the quantity and price if the market were competitive.
(c) (2 points) Find the deadweight loss of monopoly and explain where it comes from.

Show answer

(a) MR = 5 − 2Q (same intercept, twice as steep). MR = MC: 5 − 2Q = 1, so Q = 2. Price from demand: P = 5 − 2 = $3.

(b) Competition produces where P = MC: 5 − Q = 1, so Q = 4 and P = $1.

(c) DWL = ½ × (4 − 2) × (3 − 1) = 2. Units 3 and 4 are worth more to buyers than the $1 they cost to supply, but the monopolist does not sell them because selling more would mean cutting its price on every unit.

Full credit: Q = 2 and P = $3 from MR = MC; Q = 4 and P = $1 from P = MC; DWL = 2 with the reason that MB > MC on the units not produced.

6 points 25. Same demand, P = 5 − Q, but now the market is competitive with private marginal cost $1, and each unit produced causes $2 of pollution damage to neighbors.

(a) (2 points) Find the market quantity and the efficient quantity.
(b) (2 points) Find the deadweight loss without any policy.
(c) (2 points) What Pigouvian tax fixes it, and what quantity results?

Show answer

(a) The market uses private cost: 5 − Q = 1, so Q = 4. The efficient quantity uses SMC = 1 + 2 = 3: 5 − Q = 3, so Q* = 2.

(b) DWL = ½ × (4 − 2) × (3 − 1) = 2, the triangle between SMC and demand from Q = 2 to Q = 4.

(c) A $2 per-unit tax, equal to the external marginal cost. Supply rises from $1 to $3, the same as SMC, so the market produces Q = 2, the efficient quantity.

Full credit: 4 and 2; DWL 2; a $2 tax per unit that brings the quantity to 2.

4 points 26. Use Bill and Chris: per hour, Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut, and Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts.

(a) (2 points) Who should fish and who should gather coconuts? Explain using opportunity cost.
(b) (2 points) Working 10 hours each and fully specialized, how many fish and coconuts do they produce together?

Show answer

(a) Chris fishes and Bill gathers coconuts. A coconut costs Bill 1 fish and costs Chris 4 fish (8 ÷ 2), so Bill has the lower opportunity cost of coconuts. Chris has the absolute advantage in both, which does not decide it.

(b) Bill: 10 hours × 1 = 10 coconuts. Chris: 10 hours × 8 = 80 fish. Together, 80 fish and 10 coconuts, the kink of their joint PPC. If they each split their time evenly instead, they would get 5 + 40 = 45 fish and 5 + 10 = 15 coconuts, which is on the straight no-specialization line.

Full credit: (a) Chris fishes, Bill gathers, because Bill's opportunity cost of a coconut (1 fish) is lower than Chris's (4 fish); (b) 80 fish and 10 coconuts.

4 points 27. A concert venue raises ticket prices from $50 to $60, and ticket sales fall from 1,000 to 700.

(a) (2 points) Compute the price elasticity of demand. Is demand elastic or inelastic? Show your work.
(b) (2 points) Did the venue's ticket revenue rise or fall? Explain how this follows from (a).

Show answer

(a) % change in P = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 = 20%. % change in Q = (700 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = −30%. Elasticity = −30% ÷ 20% = −1.5. Its size is above 1, so demand is elastic.

(b) Revenue fell, from $50 × 1,000 = $50,000 to $60 × 700 = $42,000. With elastic demand, quantity falls by a bigger percent than price rises, so spending falls.

Full credit: −1.5 with the percent changes shown, "elastic"; revenue falls from $50,000 to $42,000 because demand is elastic.

4 points 28. Coffee costs $4 and frozen yogurt costs $6. Your first and second coffees give 10 and 8 units of marginal utility. Your first and second frozen yogurts give 12 and 8. You spend $14 a day.

(a) (2 points) What bundle maximizes your utility? Show the rule you used.
(b) (2 points) Suppose instead that you buy several frozen yogurts a week and their price rises. Without computing, which way should your frozen yogurt purchases move, and what happens to the marginal utility of the last frozen yogurt you buy?

Show answer

(a) 2 coffees and 1 frozen yogurt, which costs $8 + $6 = $14. The rational spending rule holds: 8 ÷ $4 = 2 for the last coffee and 12 ÷ $6 = 2 for the frozen yogurt.

(b) Buy fewer frozen yogurts (and more of other things). The higher price lowers frozen yogurt's MU per dollar below coffee's, so the last dollar spent on frozen yogurt now buys less utility than a dollar spent on coffee. Buying fewer raises the marginal utility of the last frozen yogurt you buy, because earlier units bring more MU than later ones, and you keep adjusting until the two ratios are equal again.

Full credit: (a) 2 coffees and 1 frozen yogurt with MU ÷ P equal at 2; (b) buy less, so its MU rises.

8 points 29. Demand for gas-powered leaf blowers is P = 100 − Q. The private marginal cost (PMC) of making them is P = 20 + Q. Each blower causes $20 of noise and pollution damage to neighbors. Show your work. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2024 final Q4a and Fall 2023 final Q9a

(a) (2 points) Find the market quantity and price.
(b) (2 points) Find the socially optimal quantity.
(c) (2 points) Draw the graph and find the deadweight loss of the free market.
(d) (2 points) What per-unit tax fixes it? Find the tax revenue, the price buyers pay, and the price sellers keep.

Show answer

(a) 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so 2Q = 80, Q = 40, and P = 100 − 40 = $60.

(b) Social marginal cost is SMC = PMC + EMC = 20 + Q + 20 = 40 + Q. Set it equal to demand: 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so Q* = 30 (where demand gives $70).

(c) Every unit between Q = 30 and Q = 40 costs society more than they are worth to buyers. At Q = 40 the gap between SMC ($80) and demand ($60) is $20, and at Q = 30 it is zero, so DWL = ½ × (40 − 30) × 20 = $100.

(d) A $20 per-unit Pigouvian tax, equal to the external marginal cost. It lifts supply to SMC, so Q = 30. Revenue = $20 × 30 = $600. Buyers pay $70 (read off demand) and sellers keep $70 − $20 = $50 (read off PMC: 20 + 30).

Full credit: (a) Q = 40, P = $60; (b) Q* = 30; (c) graph with D, PMC, SMC and DWL = 100; (d) $20 tax, revenue $600, $70 and $50.

2 points 30. The government gives a per-unit subsidy to producers of a good that has a negative externality and is sold in a competitive market. Then Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2024 final MC 4

(i) Producer surplus rises and total social surplus falls
(ii) Producer surplus falls and total social surplus rises
(iii) Producer surplus and total social surplus both rise
(iv) We cannot tell

Show answer

(i). The market already produces too much, because sellers ignore the external cost. The subsidy shifts supply down and pushes output even further past the efficient quantity, so the deadweight loss grows and total surplus falls, even though producers (and buyers) gain.

Full credit: circle (i).

6 points 31. A monopolist faces demand P = 100 − 2Q and has a constant marginal cost of $20. Show your work. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q2a

(a) (2 points) Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price.
(b) (2 points) Find the competitive quantity and price.
(c) (2 points) Find the deadweight loss of monopoly.

Show answer

(a) MR has the same intercept and twice the slope: MR = 100 − 4Q. MR = MC gives 100 − 4Q = 20, so Q = 20. Price from demand: P = 100 − 40 = $60.

(b) P = MC: 100 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 40 and P = $20.

(c) DWL = ½ × (40 − 20) × (60 − 20) = ½ × 20 × 40 = $400, the triangle between demand and MC from Q = 20 to Q = 40, where MB > MC on every unit not sold.

Full credit: Q = 20 and P = $60 from MR = MC; Q = 40 and P = $20; DWL = $400.

4 points 32. Berkeley wants lower rents. Option 1 is a rent ceiling below the equilibrium rent. Option 2 is a per-unit subsidy to landlords, sized so that the market rent falls to the same level as the ceiling. Under which option is renters' consumer surplus larger? Explain using a supply and demand graph. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q4

Show answer

The subsidy. Under the ceiling, neither curve moves, so only the quantity supplied at the ceiling rent gets rented, and there is a shortage. Renters' surplus is the area under demand and above the ceiling rent, but only out to that smaller quantity (and less if the units go to people who value them less). Under the subsidy, supply shifts down until the market rent equals the same low level, and the quantity rented rises to the quantity demanded at that rent. Renters pay the same rent on more units, so consumer surplus is larger by the surplus on the extra units.

Full credit: the subsidy; the ceiling limits the quantity to quantity supplied (a shortage), while the subsidy delivers more units at the same rent.

2 points 33. A per-unit tax is collected from sellers in a market where demand is perfectly inelastic (vertical) and supply slopes up. The tax is paid Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2024 final MC 2

(i) Entirely by buyers
(ii) Entirely by sellers
(iii) Split between buyers and sellers
(iv) By nobody, because the market shuts down

Show answer

(i). Buyers take the same quantity at any price, so the quantity does not change and the price buyers pay rises by the full tax. Sellers keep the same price as before. The less elastic side bears the tax, and here buyers cannot respond at all.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points 34. Maya's last coffee gives her 30 units of marginal utility and costs $3. Her last sandwich gives 40 and costs $5. Is she maximizing utility? If not, what should she change? Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2023 final MC 27 and Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q3

Show answer

No. MU per dollar is 30 ÷ 3 = 10 for coffee and 40 ÷ 5 = 8 for sandwiches, so the rational spending rule fails. She should move spending from sandwiches to coffee. Buying more coffee lowers its MU and buying fewer sandwiches raises theirs, until the two ratios are equal.

Full credit: 10 versus 8 per dollar; shift toward coffee until MU ÷ P is equal.

2 points 35. A coffee shop cuts its price by 10%, and the quantity it sells rises by 25%. Find the price elasticity of demand and the percent change in total revenue. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Midterm 1 Fall 2024 Q1

Show answer

Elasticity = (% change in Q) ÷ (% change in P) = 25% ÷ (−10%) = −2.5. Its size is above 1, so demand is relatively elastic. Revenue changes by a factor of 0.90 × 1.25 = 1.125, a 12.5% increase: with elastic demand, a price cut raises spending.

Full credit: −2.5 (elastic) and revenue up 12.5%.

2 points 36. The supply of a good is very price elastic (a flat supply curve). An increase in demand causes Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2023 final MC 29

(i) A large rise in quantity and a small rise in price
(ii) A small rise in quantity and a large rise in price
(iii) A fall in quantity
(iv) No change in quantity

Show answer

(i). Demand slides out along a flat supply curve, so most of the adjustment shows up in quantity and very little in price. Elastic means flat.

Full credit: circle (i).

One-minute review

  • Choose where MB = MC. In a market, demand is MB and supply is MC.
  • Comparative advantage means a lower opportunity cost. Specialize in what you do best relative to your other options.
  • Buyer stories shift demand; seller stories shift supply; the good's own price only moves you along a curve.
  • CS is below demand and above price; PS is above supply and below price; competition maximizes their sum.
  • Inelastic means steep. With inelastic demand, a price rise raises spending.
  • A tax is a wedge; the less elastic side bears more of it.
  • MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb.
  • Competitive firm: P = MC. Monopoly: MR = MC, price off demand, so it raises price and reduces quantity.
  • SMC = PMC + EMC. Negative externality means overproduction; a Pigouvian tax equal to EMC fixes it.
  • Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, so a lower output price shifts labor demand in.
