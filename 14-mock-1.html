Practice final A

A complete 60-point final in the exact shape of Edwards' Fall 2025 final: 2 free points, six multiple-choice questions, five short answers, a 24-point Keynesian cross problem, and a 12-point trade problem. Give yourself 90 minutes, one page of notes (front and back), and a calculator, the same rules as the real exam. Do the whole thing on paper first, and open the answers only after time is up.

The exam

Clear your desk except for your cheat sheet, a calculator, a pen, a ruler, and blank paper. For each graph part, copy the printed axes onto your paper with the same tick marks, because on exam day the axes are already printed and your job is to draw on them. Start a 90-minute timer and do not stop it.

The exam is worth 60 points in 90 minutes, so a point is worth about a minute and a half. A sensible budget is about 18 minutes for Question 1, 15 minutes for Question 2, 36 minutes for Question 3, and 18 minutes for Question 4. His cover page says "Less is more." A 2-point answer is the right answer plus one or two sentences of reason in course words.

After time is called, grade one part at a time. Open "Show answer," compare your work to the model answer and the "Full credit" line, and give yourself full, half, or zero credit honestly. Then use the table in "Score it" to decide what to reread.

Question 1 (12 points total): Circle back to the whole semester

For each of these questions, circle the one correct answer. Each part is worth 2 points.

2 points 1(a). A wheat farmer who sells in a perfectly competitive market maximizes profit by producing the quantity at which

(i) Price equals average total cost
(ii) Total revenue is as large as possible
(iii) Marginal revenue equals marginal cost
(iv) Marginal cost is as low as possible

Show answer

(iii). A firm keeps producing as long as one more unit adds more to revenue than it adds to cost, so profit is largest where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. For a price taker, marginal revenue is the market price, so the farmer produces where price equals marginal cost. His final review slide on profit maximization says exactly this: profits are largest at the quantity where mr = mc.

Full credit: (iii) circled.

2 points 1(b). A cement plant releases dust that harms the health of families living nearby, and nobody makes the plant pay for that harm. Compared with the efficient quantity, the unregulated market for cement produces

(i) Too much cement, because social marginal cost is above private marginal cost
(ii) Too little cement, because social marginal benefit is above private marginal benefit
(iii) The efficient quantity, because the market sets marginal benefit equal to private marginal cost
(iv) Too little cement, because the plant's profits are too low

Show answer

(i). This is a negative externality. The plant's supply curve reflects only its private marginal cost, but each ton of cement also imposes an external cost on neighbors, so social marginal cost is higher. The market stops where demand meets private marginal cost, which is past the efficient quantity where marginal benefit equals social marginal cost. The units in between cost society more than they are worth, which is a deadweight loss. Choice (ii) describes a positive externality, which is a demand-side story.

Full credit: (i) circled.

2 points 1(c). A Berkeley professor can grade exams a little faster than any of her graduate student instructors (GSIs), and she can write research papers much faster than any of them. The principle of comparative advantage implies that

(i) She should grade the exams herself, because she is the fastest grader
(ii) She should do both tasks herself, because she is faster at both
(iii) The GSIs should write the research papers, because that task is respected most
(iv) She should specialize in research and let the GSIs grade, because her opportunity cost of grading is higher than theirs

Show answer

(iv). Being faster at both tasks is an absolute advantage, and it does not decide who should do what. What matters is opportunity cost: every hour the professor spends grading is an hour of research she gives up, and because she is much faster at research, that hour is worth a lot. She is only a little faster at grading, so the GSIs give up much less research to grade an exam, and they have the comparative advantage in grading. This is his "Lesson 2" from the final review: even if you clean your own house faster than a housekeeper, you should hire the housekeeper if you are relatively more productive at paid work.

Full credit: (iv) circled.

2 points 1(d). Your savings account pays a nominal interest rate of 5% per year, and the inflation rate is 3% per year. Your real interest rate is

(i) 8%
(ii) 2%
(iii) 15%
(iv) −2%

Show answer

(ii). The real interest rate is the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate: r = i − π = 5% − 3% = 2%. Your balance grows 5% in dollars, but prices grow 3%, so what your savings can buy grows only about 2% per year.

Full credit: (ii) circled.

2 points 1(e). The economy is in a recession, with output below potential output. To fight the recession, the Federal Reserve would typically

(i) Raise the real interest rate to make saving more attractive
(ii) Raise government purchases G to shift planned aggregate expenditure up
(iii) Lower the real interest rate, which raises interest-sensitive spending such as investment and new car purchases
(iv) Do nothing, because prices and wages adjust right away

Show answer

(iii). The Fed's main tool is the real interest rate. Cutting it makes borrowing cheaper, so firms invest more and households buy more interest-sensitive goods like cars and houses. The lower rate also tends to depreciate the dollar, which raises net exports. All of these raise planned aggregate expenditure (PAE, total planned spending C + I + G + NX) at every level of income, so the PAE line shifts up and output rises. Choice (ii) is fiscal policy, which Congress controls, not the Fed. Choice (iv) is wrong because of inflation inertia: prices and wages adjust slowly, which is why output can sit below potential in the first place.

Full credit: (iii) circled.

2 points 1(f). Which of these counts in this year's U.S. gross domestic product (GDP)?

(i) Your neighbor buys your used 2019 car
(ii) You buy shares of stock in a technology company
(iii) A bakery buys flour that it uses to bake the bread it sells
(iv) A household buys a new air conditioner that was built in Ohio this year

Show answer

(iv). GDP counts final goods and services, newly produced, within the country, in a period of time. The air conditioner is all four. The used car was produced and counted in 2019, so reselling it is not new production. Buying stock is a financial transaction that moves ownership of an existing asset. The flour is an intermediate good: its value is already included in the price of the bread, so counting it separately would count it twice.

Full credit: (iv) circled.

Question 2 (10 points total): Short answers, long-run payoffs

Each part is worth 2 points. Show your work where there is arithmetic, and briefly explain.

2 points 2(a). Suppose a movie ticket cost $8.00 in 2000 and $20.00 in 2025, and suppose the consumer price index (CPI, with 1982 to 1984 = 100) was 160 in 2000 and 320 in 2025. These are round numbers made up for this question. Did the price of a movie ticket rise, fall, or stay the same in inflation-adjusted (real) terms? That is, if your income rose at the rate of inflation, could you buy more, fewer, or the same number of tickets in 2025 as in 2000? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Convert the 2000 price into 2025 dollars by multiplying by the ratio of the price levels: $8.00 × (320 ÷ 160) = $8.00 × 2 = $16.00.
  2. Compare: the actual 2025 price is $20.00, which is above $16.00.
  3. Same answer with ratios: the ticket price rose by a factor of 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5, while the CPI rose by a factor of 320 ÷ 160 = 2.

The real price of a ticket rose, by $4.00 in 2025 dollars, or 25%. If your income only kept up with inflation, you could buy fewer tickets in 2025 than in 2000.

Full credit: "increased," with the comparison shown, either $16.00 in 2025 dollars versus the actual $20.00, or the ticket price rising 2.5 times while the CPI rose only 2 times. This is the same skill as the Big Mac question on the Fall 2025 final.

2 points 2(b). A bond promises to pay $630 one year from now and $661.50 two years from now, and nothing else. If the interest rate is 5% per year, what is the most a buyer would pay for this bond today? If the Fed then raises interest rates and the market interest rate on this kind of bond rises to 8%, does the price of the bond rise or fall? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. The price of a bond is the present value (PV) of its payments: PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2.
  2. At i = 5%: $630 ÷ 1.05 = $600 and $661.50 ÷ 1.1025 = $600, so the price is $1,200.
  3. At i = 8%: $630 ÷ 1.08 = $583.33 and $661.50 ÷ 1.1664 = $567.13, so the price falls to about $1,150.46.

The price falls. The bond's payments are fixed, and a higher interest rate makes every future dollar worth less today, because the buyer could instead earn 8% on the money elsewhere. The denominators get bigger, so the present value gets smaller.

Full credit: $1,200 with the two discounted payments shown, and "the price falls" because the fixed payments are discounted at a higher rate. The $1,150.46 figure is not required; the direction with the reason is.

2 points 2(c). Two countries start with the same real GDP per person, $10,000. In Country A it grows 2% per year, and in Country B it grows 1% per year. Use the rule of 70 to find about how many years it takes each country's real GDP per person to double. About how much is real GDP per person in each country after 70 years?

Show answer
  1. The rule of 70 from the textbook: the number of years for a quantity to double is about 70 divided by its growth rate in percent.
  2. Country A: 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years to double. Country B: 70 ÷ 1 = 70 years to double.
  3. After 70 years, A has doubled twice: $10,000 to $20,000 to $40,000. B has doubled once: $20,000.

A 1-percentage-point difference in growth leaves Country A about twice as rich as Country B after 70 years. His Class 18 slides make the same point: a country growing 1 point faster is twice as high after 70 years, because 1.0170 is about 2.

Full credit: 35 years and 70 years, then about $40,000 and $20,000, with the doubling logic shown.

2 points 2(d). Suppose the Fed raises U.S. interest rates and the exchange rate moves from €0.80 per U.S. dollar to €0.90 per U.S. dollar. Has the dollar appreciated or depreciated? What happens to the price in euros of a $50 pair of jeans made in the U.S., and what happens to U.S. net exports?

Show answer

The dollar has appreciated, because one dollar now buys more euros (the euro has depreciated). The jeans used to cost a European buyer $50 × €0.80 = €40, and now cost $50 × €0.90 = €45. U.S. exports become more expensive for foreigners, so they buy fewer, and foreign goods become cheaper for Americans, so Americans import more. Both effects lower U.S. net exports. This is the Class 23 chain: a higher U.S. real interest rate draws in foreign savers, the dollar appreciates, and net exports fall.

Full credit: appreciated, €40 rises to €45, and net exports fall because exports cost more abroad and imports cost less at home.

2 points 2(e). A small town has 4,000 adults. Of them, 2,280 have jobs, 120 have no job but have actively looked for work in the past four weeks, and the other 1,600 are retired, in school full time, or not looking for work. What is the town's unemployment rate? Now suppose 25 of the job seekers become discouraged and stop looking. What is the new unemployment rate, and is this good news for the town? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. The labor force is everyone who has a job or is actively looking: 2,280 + 120 = 2,400. The 1,600 others are not in the labor force.
  2. Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force = 120 ÷ 2,400 = 5%.
  3. After 25 people stop looking, they leave the labor force: unemployed = 95, labor force = 2,375. New rate = 95 ÷ 2,375 = 4%.

The measured unemployment rate falls, but this is not good news. Nobody found a job: employment is still 2,280. The rate fell only because 25 discouraged workers stopped counting as unemployed.

Full credit: 5% from 120 ÷ 2,400, then 4% from 95 ÷ 2,375, and the point that the drop reflects people leaving the labor force, not new jobs.

Question 3 (24 points total): Data centers go dark, and we Keynesian Cross our fingers

Investment in AI data centers, the huge buildings full of computer chips and power equipment that train and run AI models, has helped fuel recent growth in U.S. GDP. Suppose U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion, and suppose the aggregate marginal propensity to consume out of disposable income is mpc = ½. The following system models planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) in trillions of dollars, where T is net taxes:

ConsumptionC = 8 + ½(Y − T)PAEPAE = C + I + G + NX
Net taxesT = 6InvestmentI = 6
Government purchasesG = 6Net exportsNX = −2

2 points 3(a). Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find the level of PAE when Y = 0, which is the vertical intercept in the Keynesian cross diagram. Show your work.

Show answer

Substitute the consumption function and the numbers:

PAE = 8 + ½(Y − 6) + 6 + 6 − 2 = 8 − 3 + 6 + 6 − 2 + ½Y = 15 + ½Y

When Y = 0, PAE = 15, and that is the vertical intercept. The 15 is autonomous spending, the part of spending that does not depend on income: C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX.

Full credit: PAE = 15 + ½Y with the substitution shown, including −½ × 6 = −3 from taxes, and the intercept 15. Forgetting the tax term gives 18, which loses the point.

2 points 3(b). Using your answer to part (a), set PAE = Y and solve for the short-run equilibrium level of Y, which is the horizontal coordinate of the spot where PAE crosses the 45° line. Show your work.

Show answer

Set 15 + ½Y = Y. Subtract ½Y from both sides: 15 = ½Y, so Y = 30 ($30 trillion). Check: PAE at Y = 30 is 15 + 15 = 30, which equals Y.

Full credit: Y = 30 with the algebra shown.

4 points 3(c). Graph the PAE line and the 45-degree line on the axes below, and show the equilibrium level of output in the short run. Label intercepts, slopes, and the equilibrium.

Show answer
  1. Draw the 45° line from the origin through (10, 10), (20, 20), and so on. Every point on it has PAE = Y.
  2. Draw PAE starting at 15 on the vertical axis with slope ½: it rises 5 for every 10 to the right, so it passes through (10, 20), (20, 25), and (30, 30).
  3. Mark the crossing at (30, 30) with dotted lines to both axes and label it as the equilibrium.

Full credit: 45° line from the origin, PAE with intercept 15 and a slope visibly flatter than the 45° line, both labeled, and the equilibrium at Y = 30 (and PAE = 30) marked on the axes.

In 2027, suppose several large technology companies announce that they will pause new data-center projects, because they now expect AI services to earn less in the future than they had hoped. Stock prices of AI-related companies fall, and households who own those stocks feel less wealthy. Suppose these events produce this situation:

ConsumptionC' = 7.5 + ½(Y − T)PAE'PAE' = C' + I' + G + NX
Net taxesT = 6InvestmentI' = 4.5
Government purchasesG = 6Net exportsNX = −2

2 points 3(d). Combine the expenditure terms in PAE' and find the level of PAE' when Y = 0, the new vertical intercept. Show your work.

Show answer

PAE' = 7.5 + ½(Y − 6) + 4.5 + 6 − 2 = 7.5 − 3 + 4.5 + 6 − 2 + ½Y = 13 + ½Y

When Y = 0, PAE' = 13, and that is the new vertical intercept.

Full credit: PAE' = 13 + ½Y with the substitution shown, and the intercept 13.

2 points 3(e). Using your answer to part (d), set PAE' = Y and solve for the new short-run equilibrium level of Y. Show your work.

Show answer

Set 13 + ½Y = Y, so 13 = ½Y and Y = 26 ($26 trillion). Check: 13 + 13 = 26.

Full credit: Y = 26 with the algebra shown.

4 points 3(f). On the axes below, graph the original PAE line from part (a), the new PAE' line from part (d), and the 45-degree line. Show both equilibria and label intercepts.

Show answer
  1. Redraw the 45° line and PAE = 15 + ½Y from part (c), with the equilibrium at 30.
  2. Draw PAE' = 13 + ½Y: it starts at 13 and is parallel to PAE, because the marginal propensity to consume did not change. It sits 2 below PAE at every level of Y.
  3. Mark the new crossing at (26, 26) with dotted lines to both axes. The equilibrium moves down and to the left along the 45° line.

Full credit: both PAE lines parallel with intercepts 15 and 13 labeled, the 45° line, and both equilibria (30 and 26) marked on the axes. A PAE' line with a different slope loses points, because nothing changed the mpc.

2 points 3(g). By how much did planned aggregate expenditure change between (a) and (d)? Express your answer in trillions of dollars and describe it as an increase, a decrease, or no change. Explain fully, saying which spending components changed and why, and describe how the change appears in the graph.

Show answer

PAE decreased by $2 trillion at every level of income (the intercept fell from 15 to 13). Two components changed:

  1. Planned investment fell by $1.5 trillion (from 6 to 4.5). A firm buys capital as long as the present value of its future marginal revenue products is at least its price. When companies expect AI to earn less, the expected future marginal revenue product of a data center falls, so its present value falls and they build fewer. This is the "Pessimism about Future MRPK" shift in his Class 14 slides, where MRPK is the marginal revenue product of capital.
  2. Autonomous consumption fell by $0.5 trillion (from 8 to 7.5). Lower stock prices reduce household wealth, and a fall in wealth reduces consumption at every level of income.

Together, −1.5 − 0.5 = −2. In the graph this is a parallel vertical drop of the PAE line by 2.

Full credit: decrease of $2 trillion, broken into investment (−1.5) and autonomous consumption (−0.5) with a reason for each, shown as a parallel downward shift of PAE.

2 points 3(h). By how much did GDP change between (b) and (e)? Is that change the same size as your answer to (g), or different? If different, say which is larger and explain why, referencing the model.

Show answer

GDP decreased by $4 trillion, from 30 to 26. That is larger than the $2 trillion drop in PAE. The reason is the multiplier. Because consumption depends on income with mpc = ½, the first $2 trillion of lost spending is also $2 trillion of lost income, and households then cut their own spending by ½ × 2 = $1 trillion, which cuts someone else's income by $1 trillion, and so on: 2 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + ... = 4. The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ (1 − ½) = 2, and 2 × (−2) = −4. In the graph, the PAE line is flatter than the 45° line, so a vertical drop of 2 moves the crossing 4 to the left.

Full credit: decrease of $4 trillion, larger than the change in PAE, because a nonzero mpc creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 2.

2 points 3(i). Suppose Congress could quickly pass a temporary spending bill that changes government purchases G by enough to bring GDP back to $30 trillion. How big a change in G is that? Is it an increase or a decrease? Explain fully.

Show answer

Congress should raise G by $2 trillion, from 6 to 8. The shock lowered PAE by $2 trillion at every income level. An extra $2 trillion of government purchases raises PAE by exactly $2 trillion at every income level, so the line shifts back up to 15 + ½Y, and with the mpc unchanged, the multiplier of 2 brings output back from 26 to 30. Congress does not need to raise G by the full $4 trillion drop in GDP, because the increase in G gets multiplied too.

Full credit: increase G by $2 trillion, matching the $2 trillion fall in PAE (not the $4 trillion fall in GDP), because the same multiplier applies to G.

2 points 3(j). Instead of raising G, suppose Congress cuts net taxes T. How big a tax cut would bring GDP back to $30 trillion? Is it larger or smaller than your answer to (i)? Explain fully, and say which policy adds more to the government budget deficit.

Show answer

A tax cut raises disposable income one for one, but households spend only the mpc share of it and save the rest. So a tax cut of ΔT raises PAE by only mpc × ΔT = ½ × ΔT. To raise PAE by $2 trillion, Congress must cut taxes by $4 trillion, from 6 to 2. Check: autonomous spending becomes 7.5 − ½ × 2 + 4.5 + 6 − 2 = 15, the original intercept.

The tax cut is larger than the $2 trillion increase in G, because government purchases enter PAE directly while half of a tax cut is saved. His Class 20 slides make the same comparison for the 2009 stimulus: $100 billion more G raises PAE by $100 billion, while $100 billion of tax cuts raises PAE by mpc × $100 billion. The government's budget surplus is T − G, which starts at 6 − 6 = 0. Raising G creates a deficit of $2 trillion, and cutting T creates a deficit of $4 trillion, so the tax cut adds more to the deficit.

Full credit: cut T by $4 trillion, larger than the $2 trillion in G, because a tax cut raises PAE by only mpc × ΔT (households save half), so it adds twice as much to the deficit.

Question 4 (12 points total): Spin cycle: washing machines, tariffs, and jobs

Washing machines are a familiar tariff story in this class. Suppose the U.S. market for washing machines has domestic demand P = 1000 − 50Q and domestic supply P = 100 + 50Q, where P is dollars per machine and Q is millions of machines per year. The U.S. is a price taker on the world market: it can buy as many machines as it wants at the world price of $300.

4 points 4(a). The diagram below shows domestic supply and demand. Find the price and quantity if the U.S. did not trade. Then adapt the diagram to show free trade at the world price of $300: show the world price, the domestic quantity supplied, the domestic quantity demanded, and the quantity of imports, with values. Briefly explain what you drew.

Show answer
  1. No trade: set demand equal to supply, 1000 − 50Q = 100 + 50Q, so 900 = 100Q, Q = 9 million machines, and P = 1000 − 450 = $550.
  2. Draw a horizontal world price line at $300, below the no-trade price, which is why this is an import good.
  3. Domestic quantity supplied at $300: 300 = 100 + 50Q, so QS = 4 million.
  4. Domestic quantity demanded at $300: 300 = 1000 − 50Q, so QD = 14 million.
  5. Imports = 14 − 4 = 10 million machines per year, the horizontal gap between demand and supply at the world price.

Full credit: no-trade point at 9 million and $550, a world price line at $300 below it, QS = 4 and QD = 14 marked on the quantity axis, and imports labeled as the 10 million gap between them.

2 points 4(b). Now the U.S. puts a tariff of $100 per machine on imported washing machines. What is the new price in the U.S.? What are the new domestic quantity supplied, domestic quantity demanded, and imports? Show the tariff on your graph from (a).

Show answer

The tariff is a tax on imports, so imported machines now cost $300 + $100 = $400 in the U.S., and domestic sellers can charge $400 too. Draw a second horizontal line at $400.

  1. Quantity supplied: 400 = 100 + 50Q, so QS = 6 million (up from 4).
  2. Quantity demanded: 400 = 1000 − 50Q, so QD = 12 million (down from 14).
  3. Imports = 12 − 6 = 6 million (down from 10).

Full credit: price $400, QS = 6 million, QD = 12 million, imports 6 million, with the $400 line drawn above the world price line.

2 points 4(c). Calculate the tariff revenue the government collects and the total deadweight loss of the tariff. Shade and label the deadweight loss areas on your graph, and briefly explain why each one is a loss.

Show answer
  1. Tariff revenue = tariff × imports = $100 × 6 million = $600 million (area e, the rectangle between $300 and $400 from 6 million to 12 million).
  2. Deadweight loss triangle d, on the supply side: ½ × (6 − 4) million × $100 = $100 million.
  3. Deadweight loss triangle f, on the demand side: ½ × (14 − 12) million × $100 = $100 million.
  4. Total deadweight loss = $200 million per year.

Triangle d is a loss because the machines from 4 million to 6 million are now built in the U.S. at a marginal cost above $300, when they could have been bought abroad for $300. Triangle f is a loss because buyers of the machines from 12 million to 14 million valued them above $300, the cost of getting them, but no longer buy them at $400. As a check on the whole table: consumer surplus falls by $1,300 million, producer surplus rises by $500 million, and the government gains $600 million, so 1,300 − 500 − 600 = $200 million is lost.

Full credit: revenue $600 million, deadweight loss $200 million as two triangles of $100 million each, with both triangles shaded in the right places.

Now consider the labor market for workers in U.S. washing machine factories. There is a labor supply curve, reflecting workers' marginal cost of supplying their labor, and a labor demand curve equal to the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MPL is the marginal product of labor (the extra machines one more worker produces) and MR is the marginal revenue from selling one more machine.

4 points 4(d). U.S. washing machine makers are price takers, so their marginal revenue is the U.S. price of a machine. Before the tariff, the last worker hired adds 150 machines per year (MPL = 150). What is that worker's MRPL before the tariff, and what is it after the tariff, at the same level of employment? Illustrate the labor market before and after the tariff, showing all demand and supply curves and the equilibrium wages and employment. Does the tariff raise total U.S. employment? Briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. Before the tariff, MR = $300, so MRPL = $300 × 150 = $45,000 per year. Firms hire until MRPL equals the wage, so the market wage W1 is $45,000.
  2. After the tariff, MR = $400, so at the same employment MRPL = $400 × 150 = $60,000 per year.
  3. Because the price rose, MRPL rises at every level of employment, so the whole labor demand curve shifts up (out). Labor supply does not move.
  4. The new equilibrium is up and to the right along labor supply: the wage rises from W1 to W2 and employment rises from L1 to L2. W2 lands between $45,000 and $60,000, because as firms hire more workers, MPL falls.

Total employment probably does not rise. His Class 16 slides say a tariff likely raises employment in the protected industry but probably does not raise total employment. Buyers pay $100 more per machine and have less to spend on other goods, other countries may retaliate with tariffs on U.S. exports, the dollar may appreciate, and industries that use the protected good can be hurt. Jobs get rearranged more than created.

Full credit: $45,000 before and $60,000 after; a graph with labor supply, D1 and D2 with D2 above D1, and W1, L1, W2, L2 marked with W2 > W1 and L2 > L1; and the point that jobs gained in the protected industry are likely offset elsewhere.
