Labor market and its shiftsClass 12

  1. W up, L across. D = MRPL slopes down; S slopes up. Mark W1 and L1.
  2. Shift only the curve the story touches; mark W2, L2, and an arrow.
  • MR or MPL falls (trade war, cheaper imports, machines replace these workers): D shifts in, so W and L fall. A rise shifts D out.
  • Immigration shifts S out: W falls, L rises. Removals shift S in, and partners of those removed lose MPL, so their D shifts in.
  • Reverse: W and L moved the same way means D shifted; opposite ways means S shifted.

Wage floor and the EITCClasses 12, 13

  1. Floor: a flat line at WN above W1. Firms hire LD (on D); LS want work (on S).
  2. EITC: S shifts down by the credit. Employers pay W2; workers get W2 + EITC.
  • Unemployment = LS − LD. D: W = 30 − 2L, S: W = 6 + 2L, WN = 22 give LD = 4, LS = 8, so 4 are unemployed.
  • A floor below W1 does nothing. The EITC raises L, and W2 < W1 < W2 + EITC. A subsidy paid to employers shifts D up instead.

Two labor markets: inequalityClass 13

  1. Low-skill panel on the left, high-skill on the right, WH1 above WL1. Shift only the curves the story names; inequality is the gap WH − WL.
  • Globalization (shown): low-skill goods get cheaper, so DL shifts in; high-skill export prices rise, so DH shifts out. The gap grows.
  • Skill-biased technology: only DH shifts out. More college graduates: SH shifts out and SL shifts in, so the gap shrinks.

Capital: rental price and bond priceClass 14

  1. Rental market: D = MRPK = MR × MPK; S is vertical (a fixed stock). A higher output price raises only R. More machines lower R and raise K.
  2. Bond: the price falls as i rises. At i = 0 it is F1 + F2 + F3.
  • $100 a year for 3 years is worth $300 at i = 0 and $248.69 at i = 10%.
  • Lower future MPK or MR lowers each MRPK, so the price of capital falls.

Exports, then a trade warClass 16

  1. Mark P1 and Q1, then a flat Pw above P1. Read QD on D and QS on S; exports = QS − QD.
  2. Trade war with a fixed harvest that cannot be stored: go straight down from QS to D. That height is Ptw, below P1. Next season P drifts back toward P1.
  • D: P = 12 − Q, S: P = Q, Pw = 8 give QD = 4, QS = 8, exports 4. Trade war: Ptw = 12 − 8 = 4 (P1 was 6).
  • If other buyers pay more than Ptw, their price is the floor. Reverse: exports of 6 need Pw − (12 − Pw) = 6, so Pw = 9.

Imports with a tariff or a quotaClass 16

  1. Flat Pw below P1; imports = QD1 − QS1.
  2. Draw Pw + t; read QS2 on S and QD2 on D.
  3. Letter the strip between the lines c, d, e, f (g is producer surplus before).
  • Buyers lose c + d + e + f. Producers gain c. Revenue e = t × (QD2 − QS2). DWL = d + f.
  • D: P = 20 − Q, S: P = Q, Pw = 4, t = 2 give c = 10, d = 2, e = 16, f = 2.
  • Quota: draw "S + quota" (S shifted right by the quota above Pw). Equal imports give the tariff's price; e becomes quota rent.

PPC and CPC: gains from tradeClass 15

  1. Straight PPC: the intercepts are one worker's output (U.S. 2 tons or 2 WM; China 1 ton or 2 WM). The CPC starts at the export good with slope −ToT.
  2. Bowed-out PPC (rising OC): make at A, where a line with slope −ToT touches it; use at B. The country makes both goods.
  • ToT = export price ÷ import price = $400 ÷ $300 = 1⅓ WM per ton, between the OCs 1 and 2. China's ToT is the reciprocal, ¾.
  • A better ToT pivots the CPC out. Reverse: ToT = CPC vertical intercept ÷ horizontal intercept.
  • Gain at 1 ton: CPC 1⅓ − PPC 1 = ⅓ WM more.

Monopoly and negative externalitymicro

  1. Monopoly: D = MB. MR starts with D and is twice as steep. Qm is where MR = MC; go up to D for Pm.
  2. Externality: SMC = PMC + EMC. The market makes Qmkt (D meets PMC); the efficient Q* is where D meets SMC. A tax equal to EMC moves it to Q*.
  • A monopolist raises P and cuts Q. Example: P = 300 − 10Q, MC = 10Q: 10 units at $200 against 15 at $150.

Keynesian cross: base and shockClasses 19 to 21

  1. Same scale on both axes; draw the 45° line Y = PAE.
  2. PAE = C + I + G + NX starts at A and rises with slope mpc.
  3. The crossing is Y = A ÷ (1 − mpc). Mark it on both axes.
  4. Shock: PAE' is parallel, moved by ΔPAE. Mark its intercept and its crossing.
  • 12 + 0.5Y gives Y = 12 ÷ 0.5 = 24; 10 + 0.5Y gives 20.
  • ΔPAE = −2 is the vertical shift. ΔY = 2 × (−2) = −4 is the horizontal move.
  • Right of the crossing Y > PAE, so unsold goods pile up and firms cut output.

Output gaps and the policy fixClasses 20 to 22

  1. Draw a dashed line at Y*. Recessionary gap (Y1 < Y*): raise G, cut T, or the Fed cuts r. PAE shifts up until it crosses at Y*.
  2. Expansionary gap (Y2 > Y*): inflation creeps up, so the Fed raises r. PAE shifts down to PAELR (long run) at Y*.
  • Needed ΔPAE = (Y* − Y) × (1 − mpc). Example: Y = 20, Y* = 24, mpc = 0.5: raise G by 2, or cut T by 2 ÷ 0.5 = 4.

Fed reaction function, long-run r*Classes 22, 23

  1. RF slopes up: higher π, higher r. At r*, read the π target.
  2. Lower long-run inflation: shift RF up to RF2. After a short recession, π settles at the lower target (2% to 1% here).
  3. Long run: I slopes down, S + NKI slopes up. Monetary policy moves neither, so r* stays put.
  • RF: r = 3 + 0.5 × (π − 2). At π = 4%, r = 4% and i = r + π = 8%.
  • Bigger budget deficits shift S + NKI left: r* rises, I falls, NX falls. A permanent rise in spending also raises r*.

Market for dollarsClass 23

  1. D is foreigners buying U.S. goods and assets. S is Americans buying foreign ones.
  2. Shift, then mark e2. If e2 is above e1, the dollar appreciated.
  • U.S. real rate rises (shown): D right, S left, so the dollar appreciates and NX falls.
  • Foreign rate rises: D left, S right, the dollar depreciates. Safe-haven rush: D right. U.S. tariffs: S left.
ECON 2 final · Side 2 · Rules and formulas for every question typeGraphs and symbol key are on side 1

How to answer (his grading)

  • Answer first with the direction word and the number ("decreased by $2 trillion"), then the reason in one to three sentences. Less is more.
  • On a graph, label both axes, every curve and intercept, and both coordinates of each equilibrium. On a shift, name the curve, the direction, and the reason through a formula.

Keynesian cross graphs 9, 10

  • PAE = A + mpc × Y, where A = C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX (the intercept) and C̄ is autonomous consumption. Y = A ÷ (1 − mpc).
  • Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc); ΔY = multiplier × ΔPAE; gap = Y − Y*.
  • Short run: prices are sticky, so output follows planned spending. Long run: output returns to Y*, set by labor, capital, and technology.
  • Multipliers: mpc 1/3 gives 1.5; 0.5 gives 2; 0.6 gives 2.5; 2/3 gives 3; 0.75 gives 4; 0.8 gives 5; 0.9 gives 10.
If askedDo this
ΔPAE, then ΔGDP.ΔPAE adds the changes in the pieces not multiplied by Y, with signs (NX from −1 to 0 is +1); it is the vertical shift. ΔY = multiplier × ΔPAE, the slide along the 45° line. ΔY is larger whenever mpc > 0, because spending becomes income and part of it is spent again.
Offset a shock, or close a gap.Offset: ΔG = −ΔPAE of the shock, which returns Y to where it was. Close a gap: ΔG = (Y* − Y) × (1 − mpc), which moves Y to Y*. They match only if Y started at Y*.
Use taxes or transfers.ΔT = −needed ΔPAE ÷ mpc, so needed 2 with mpc 0.5 means cut T by 4. A transfer is a cut in T. Tax changes must be bigger because part is saved.
Equal ΔG and ΔT.ΔPAE = (1 − mpc) × ΔG, so ΔY = ΔG exactly.
Fed move that closes a gap.Points of r = needed ΔPAE ÷ spending change per point. Gap 2 with mpc 0.6 needs 0.8; at 0.4 per point, cut r by 2 points. Then i = r + π.
r that puts Y at Y*.Set the intercept (a − b × r) equal to Y* × (1 − mpc) and solve for r.
Find the mpc.mpc = ΔC ÷ Δ(Y − T), never C ÷ Y. From two outcomes, mpc = 1 − ΔPAE ÷ ΔY. From a graph, 1 − mpc = intercept ÷ Y, so 12 and 30 give 0.6.
Missing I or G.The intercept that gives Y* is Y* × (1 − mpc). Subtract the pieces you know.
A table of Y and PAE.Equilibrium is the row where PAE = Y. mpc = ΔPAE ÷ ΔY between two rows.
Find ΔC.ΔC = ΔC̄ + mpc × ΔY. If only I moved, ΔC = mpc × ΔY.
New mpc.The line tilts around the same intercept; recompute A ÷ (1 − new mpc).
Imported spending.Only the part spent on domestic goods raises PAE.
  • Trap Offset a shock with −ΔPAE, never −ΔGDP. With taxes inside C, subtract mpc × T, not T: 6 + 0.5(Y − 2) = 5 + 0.5Y.
  • Trap A change in Y moves along PAE. C̄, I, G, NX, T, and r shift it in parallel; the 45° line never moves.

Fiscal policy words Class 20

  • Surplus = T − G. A deficit is one year's shortfall; the debt is all past deficits minus surpluses. Expansionary policy lowers T − G; countercyclical policy fights the current gap.
  • Tax multiplier = −mpc ÷ (1 − mpc), one smaller in size than the G multiplier. A tax cut for high-mpc households moves PAE more.
  • Debt brings higher interest rates, the risk of a debt crisis, and a burden on future generations. Run surpluses in good times.
  • A balanced-budget rule cuts G in a recession and deepens it. Stimulus can be too small, too big, or too late; the Recovery Act offset part of the fall (Y2 < Y3 < Y*).
  • G rose but Y did not change: other spending fell by as much.
  • Automatic stabilizers: taxes fall and transfers rise in a recession with no new law. The structural deficit is the deficit at Y*.

Long-run growth Class 18

  • Yt = Y0 × (1 + g)t. Average yearly growth = (Yt ÷ Y0)1/t − 1, so 100 to 121 in 2 years is 10%.
  • Rule of 70: years to double ≈ 70 ÷ g%, and g ≈ 70 ÷ years. Eightfold is 3 doublings. 1.0170 ≈ 2 and 1.01200 ≈ 7.3.
  • Growth per person ≈ GDP growth − population growth. Catching up from half takes about 70 ÷ (gB − gA) years.
  • Y/POP = (Y/N) × (N/POP), with N workers and POP population. $30,000 per person and $75,000 per worker mean N/POP = 0.4.
  • Over time, technology explains most growth (about 2/3). Across countries, capital and technology explain about half each; the technology gap is mostly institutions.
  • Roads are physical capital, schooling is human capital, and methods and institutions are technology. Social infrastructure: rule of law, property rights, protection from government.
  • Inventions help others, so markets make too few. Fixes: education, R&D subsidies, secure property rights.
  • Growth rising from 1% to 2% is a rise of 1 percentage point, or 100%.

The Fed, inflation, banks graphs 10, 11

  • The Fed sets i (monetary policy); Congress sets G and T (fiscal policy). r = i − π, and π moves slowly, so r moves with i.
  • Unexpected inflation lowers the real rate a loan pays, so it helps borrowers and hurts lenders.
If askedDo this
The Fed raises r.I, C (new cars), and NX (the dollar rises) fall, so PAE shifts down and Y falls by the multiplier.
π up, i fixed.r falls, so policy got looser with no Fed move.
Open-market purchase.The Fed creates money and buys bonds: reserves rise, the fed funds rate falls, bond prices rise. It is not G. Higher interest on reserves raises the fed funds rate.
Y is not at Y*.Right away π does not move (menu costs, contracts, limited information); at Y* it stays at expected inflation. Over time π rises if Y > Y* and falls if Y < Y*, and the Fed moves r until Y = Y*. Reverse: π falling for years means Y < Y*.
His rule r = 3 + 0.5(π − 2).π = 4 gives r = 4 and i = 8. In nominal terms i = 2 + 1.5π, so π up 2 points raises i 3 points, and i = 9.5 means π = 5.
Deflation.π = −2 needs r = 1, so i = −1. Rates cannot go far below 0 (cash pays 0), so the Fed uses forward guidance and quantitative easing.
Long run.Y = Y*, r = r*, and π is where the RF crosses r*, not necessarily 2%. RF r = 1 + 0.5π and r* = 3 give π = 4%.
Along the RF, or a shift?π moved and the Fed followed its rule: a movement. A new rule is a shift, the only way to change long-run π. A steeper rule deepens the short-run recession.
Oil price jump.π rises at once (an inflation shock). The Fed raises r along its RF, so Y dips below Y*, then returns.
Why did the Fed move?To offset another force (2008, COVID), to cut inflation, or by mistake (the Depression, when tight money raised r).
Bank solvency.Equity = loans + reserves + bonds − deposits − borrowing; negative is insolvent. Rate rises hit long-term bonds hardest.
Runs, contagion.A run forces fire sales that can sink a sound bank. Channels: confidence, linkage (a loan between banks), fire sale, macroeconomic.
Financial crisis.Wider credit spreads (risky rate − safe rate), tighter lending, and lower confidence shift PAE down. Prevent with capital requirements and deposit insurance.
  • Trap "Inflation falls" means prices rise more slowly, not that prices fall.

Exchange rates, capital flows graph 12

  • Ask whose price the number is: ¥ per $1 prices a dollar, $ per €1 prices a euro. A higher price means that currency appreciated.
  • Percent changes use each currency's own old price: ¥160 to ¥150 per $1 is −6.25% for the dollar and +6.67% for the yen.
  • Foreign price = dollar price × rate, so $50 at ¥150 is ¥7,500. Dollar price = foreign price ÷ rate. Flip a quote with 1 ÷ rate.
  • A foreign recession shifts D for dollars left, so the dollar depreciates. When D and S shift in opposite directions, the price change is certain but the quantity is not. A strong dollar helps importers and Americans abroad and hurts exporters.
  • Long run: NX = −NKI and S + NKI = I, so NKI = I − S; r* is the real rate where they balance. A trade deficit means saving is below investment.
  • Tariffs move the dollar but not NKI, so NX stays the same. A higher U.S. rate raises NKI and lowers NX. Bad U.S. goods or a taste for imports would weaken the dollar, not cause a deficit.
  • Balance of payments: EX + KI = IM + KO (KI, KO are capital inflows, outflows). EX 3, IM 4, KO 1 gives KI = 2.
  • Law of one price: e = foreign price ÷ U.S. price. It holds for traded goods like gold, not for Big Macs (local labor and rent). A fixed rate above the market rate forces the central bank to buy its currency with reserves.

Capital and present value graph 4

  • PV = F ÷ (1 + i)t with i as a decimal; add the PV of each payment. Price of capital = the PV of future MRPKs.
  • When rates rise, bond, stock, and machine prices fall, long-term bonds most. Bond prices fell means rates rose.
  • Implied rate: 1 + i = F ÷ price, so $1,050 next year for $1,000 is 5%. Paid forever, PV = F ÷ i: $50 a year at 5% is $1,000.
  • Buy a machine if the PV of its MRPKs is at least its price: $1,000 a year for 3 years at 5% is $2,723.25.
  • Investment demand: a change in i is a move along it. A tax credit shifts it right; a higher machine price, lower MPK, or pessimism shifts it left.
  • Lower rates raise stock prices two ways: a higher PV of dividends and higher expected dividends.
  • "Capital" covers machines, financial assets, and savings that cross borders. Investment counts only new physical capital.

GDP, prices, jobs Class 17

  • Y = C + I + G + NX. I is new capital, new housing, and inventory change. G excludes transfers.
  • Count final goods newly made here this period, or add each stage's value added. Used goods, stocks, and transfers are out. An import raises C and IM equally, so Y is unchanged. Paying a housekeeper adds to GDP.
  • CPI = cost of the base-year basket now ÷ its base-year cost × 100. π = change in the index ÷ last year's index.
  • $X of year A in year-B dollars = X × CPIB ÷ CPIA. Real price change = (new ÷ old price) ÷ (new ÷ old CPI); above 1, it rose.
  • Real GDP = nominal ÷ deflator × 100; the two are equal in the base year. Real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation.
  • The CPI includes imports and the deflator does not. The CPI overstates living costs (quality, substitution, new goods).
  • Core CPI drops food and energy. GDP per person beats the typical wage because it counts capital income. PPP compares countries at one set of prices.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force (employed + unemployed). Participation = labor force ÷ adults. The unemployed looked for work in the last 4 weeks; they are frictional, structural, or cyclical.
  • Discouraged workers leave the labor force: 8 ÷ 160 = 5% becomes 6 ÷ 158 = 3.8% with no new jobs.

Trade and trade policy graphs 5 to 7

  • Opportunity cost of 1 X = Y given up ÷ X made. From hours per unit, it is hours for X ÷ hours for Y. The lower OC exports X.
  • Absolute advantage in both goods still leaves gains if OCs differ; equal OCs mean no gain. Scaling both goods equally changes nothing. Sources: factor abundance and an early start.
  • Exporters and buyers of imports win; import-competing firms and their workers lose; the country gains. A tariff may add jobs in the protected industry but probably not in total.
  • Tariff DWL = ½ × t × (QS2 − QS1) + ½ × t × (QD1 − QD2). Revenue given: set t × (imports at Pw + t) equal to it and solve for t.
  • Pw + t at or above P1 ends imports. A quota above free-trade imports, or a tariff on a good the country exports, does nothing.
  • A production subsidy causes only loss d, since buyers still pay Pw. Flatter curves mean imports fall more. Convert first: €320 at €0.80 per $1 is $400.

Micro for the multiple choice graph 8

  • Markets match MB to MC. A monopolist raises P and cuts Q. Comparative advantage means the lowest OC.
  • The opportunity cost of college is tuition plus forgone earnings.
  • Buyer stories shift D, seller stories shift S, and the own price moves along. With two shifts, only one of P and Q is certain.
  • A binding ceiling sells Qs (shortage Qd − Qs); a binding floor sells Qd.
  • Elasticity = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP; above 1 in size is elastic. If inelastic, P and spending move together.
  • The less elastic side bears more of a tax, whoever pays it in. Taxing inelastic goods gives a smaller DWL.
  • Competitive firm: P = MR = MC; profits bring entry until P = ATC. Monopoly MR is the change in total revenue. With no fixed cost, profit = revenue − the area under MC.
  • Positive externality: too little, so subsidize. A polluting monopolist: compare Qm with where D meets SMC.
  • Rational spending: MUa ÷ Pa = MUb ÷ Pb. Services that need people cannot be automated much, so their MC tracks wages.
