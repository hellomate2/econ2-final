Class 13 · Inequality

Inequality

Rising U.S. income inequality since the 1970s comes mostly from rising inequality in what people earn from work. So the tool is the labor market from Class 12, drawn twice: one market for low-skilled workers and one for high-skilled workers. Skill-biased technology and globalization shift those two demand curves apart; education, the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), and the minimum wage are the policy responses. The Fall 2025 final did not ask an inequality question directly, but its Q4 used the same shift in the marginal revenue product of labor (MRPL = MR × MPL), and Q4(d) asked about winners and losers from a trade shock, which is the globalization story in this deck.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

Since the 1970s, people with a lot of education have pulled further ahead in pay than people with little education, and that growing pay gap is the main reason U.S. income inequality rose. To study it, draw the labor market from Class 12 twice: one market for low-skill workers and one for high-skill workers. On the final, every cause and every policy is a shift of one curve in one of those two markets, so always ask which curve moves and why: technology and trade move demand through MRPL = MPL × MR, while education, immigration, and the EITC move supply.

Three kinds of inequality, and the one he focuses on

Slide 15 lists three types of economic inequality:

  • Opportunity inequality: differences in the chances people have, such as access to good schools.
  • Wealth inequality: differences in what people own (savings, houses, stocks).
  • Income inequality: differences in what people receive each year.

He focuses on labor income inequality, inequality in earnings from working (slide 16), for three reasons. We have a tool for it, the labor supply and labor demand diagram. Rising labor income inequality is the main source of rising overall income inequality in the U.S. And it is an important source of rising wealth inequality, since people save out of earnings.

In plain words: inequality can mean unequal chances in life, unequal savings and property, or unequal pay each year. This class is about the last one, and mostly about pay from jobs, because that is what grew most unequal.

Why care

Slide 17 gives the reasons: income inequality and poverty are often correlated; both are damaging to people and to the economy; and rising inequality may be bad for social cohesion and democracy. Slide 18 shows Case and Deaton's "deaths of despair" (deaths from drugs, alcohol, or suicide) for people aged 50 to 54. For U.S. white non-Hispanics the rate more than doubled from 1990 to 2014. In Germany and France it fell, and in Sweden, Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom it changed far less than in the U.S.

Measuring inequality: income shares and fractiles

The measure in his slides is the income share of a group at the top: the fraction of all income received by, say, the top 10% of households. If the top 10% receive 45% of all income, the top decile income share is 45%.

A fractile is "a slice of a distribution defined by percentiles" (slide 21). Examples: the 99th percentile and above (the top 1%), or the group between the 90th and 95th percentiles (written P90-95). A percentile is a cutoff: the 90th percentile income is the income that 90% of households are below.

Top share = (income of the group) ÷ (total income of everyone). Shares of non-overlapping slices add up: share of P90-100 = share of P90-95 + share of P95-99 + share of P99-100.

In plain words: add up what the richest group earned and divide by what everyone earned. Slices that do not overlap can be added together, and a middle slice is the bigger group minus the smaller group inside it.

Where the numbers come from: Piketty and Saez (2003) used U.S. income tax returns going back to the start of the federal income tax in 1913 (slide 19). Surveys say little about the very top, because few very rich households are sampled and they often underreport, but tax returns cover them. Their method (slide 20):

  1. Total income for the whole country comes from other sources (national accounts).
  2. The total number of households (tax units) comes from the Census.
  3. Tax data show how many taxpayers earn different amounts and how much those groups earn.
  4. So they can compute what fraction of total income goes to the top 1%, the top 10%, and so on.

In plain words: they took the country's total income and its number of households from other sources. Tax records told them how much the richest households earned. Dividing one by the other gives the share of income going to the top.

Other measures you may meet elsewhere, the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, are not in his Class 13 slides. On this exam, think in top shares and in the wage gap between the high-skill and low-skill markets.

What the data show

Slide 14 summarizes the history: measured by the share of total income earned by the top, U.S. income inequality "Was high prior to World War II," was "Low up to the 1970s," and "Is rising strongly since then." His charts (all from Piketty and Saez, read from the graphs):

  • Top 10% share (slides 22 and 23): in the mid-40s percent in the late 1920s and 1930s, a sharp drop during World War II to about a third, roughly flat at a third into the 1970s, then a steady climb to over 50% in the 2010s, reaching about 55% in 2021.
  • Splitting the top 10% (slide 24): most of the rise is the top 1%. The legend gives 2021 cutoffs: the top 1% had incomes above $630,000; the next 4% (P95-99), $233,000 to $630,000; and the next 5% (P90-95), $153,000 to $233,000. The P90-95 share stays near 10 to 12% the whole time, while the top 1% share falls to about 10% by the 1970s and climbs past 20% after 2000.
  • Top 0.01% share (slide 25): about 1% or less from the 1950s to the 1970s, and roughly 3 to 7% in recent years, depending on whether capital gains are counted. The very top rose fastest.
  • Wage income only (slide 26): the top 1% share of wages fell to about 5% in the 1950s and 1960s and rose to about 12% by 2007. So the rise is in earnings from work, not only in income from capital.

His key findings (slide 27): labor income inequality started rising in the early 1970s; the share going to the very top rose even faster; and current trends toward rising inequality are due in large part to rising inequality of labor income.

In plain words: the top took a big slice before World War II, a smaller slice from the war until about 1970, and a growing slice ever since. Most of the recent growth comes from pay for work at the very top.

The framework: two labor markets

To explain rising labor income inequality, draw the Class 12 labor market twice (slides 31 and 32). Low-skill jobs are those requiring relatively little education (home healthcare aides, janitors, food-service workers). High-skill jobs require high levels of education (doctors, engineers, accountants, computer programmers) (slide 29). Each market has:

  • a supply curve from households' utility maximization (each person choosing how much to work to make themselves as well off as possible), whose position depends on tastes and on the number of workers with those skills, and
  • a demand curve from firms' profit maximization, the MRPL curve for that type of labor, with MRPL = MPL × MR. MPL (marginal product of labor) is the extra output from one more worker, and MR (marginal revenue) is the extra revenue from selling one more unit of output, which equals the price P for a competitive firm.

In plain words: each kind of worker has its own market with its own wage. What a firm will pay for one more worker equals the extra output that worker makes times the money each unit of output brings in. So anything that changes a worker's output or the price of what they make moves that market's demand curve, and anything that changes how many people want those jobs moves its supply curve.

The high-skill wage starts above the low-skill wage. Inequality rises when the gap between WH and WL grows. Autor's chart (slide 30) shows the gap growing. Real wages of full-time male workers with more than a bachelor's degree rose to roughly 1.9 times their mid-1960s level by 2012. Real wages of workers without a high school diploma ended slightly below their mid-1960s level (both read from the chart). He notes that changes in the relative wages of low-skill and high-skill workers parallel the trends in income inequality (slide 29). A relative wage is one wage divided by the other, WH ÷ WL.

He looks at two explanations inside a well-functioning labor market (skill-biased technological change and globalization) and then at explanations based on imperfect labor markets (slide 28).

Cause 1: skill-biased technological change

Technological change in recent decades, such as computers, has made high-skilled workers more productive (slide 33). Their MPL rises, so their MRPL rises, so labor demand in the high-skill market shifts out. The high-skill wage and employment rise. Most low-skilled workers' jobs do not use the new technology, so the low-skill market does not change. The gap widens, which is his slide 38 caption: skill-biased technological change "doesn't affect the wage of most low-skilled workers, and so raises inequality."

In plain words: new tools like computers let educated workers get more done, so firms want more of them and bid up their pay. Most workers without degrees do jobs the tools do not change, so their pay stays put, and the gap grows.

Why the MR part matters: tax preparers (slides 36 and 37). Computers (tax software) made tax preparers more productive, which shifted out the supply of tax preparation services and lowered their price. MRPL = MPL × MR has one piece going up and one going down. His slide 37: the data suggest MPL rose faster than prices fell, so MRPL rose and demand for tax preparers shifted out. With made-up numbers: if a preparer used to finish 2 returns an hour at $100 each, MRPL was $200 an hour. With software she finishes 3 returns an hour, and the price falls to $80: MRPL = 3 × $80 = $240, up 20%.

The other side: workers who cannot switch (slides 39 and 40). Now take workers producing the same product the old way, without the new technology. The product's price falls because the new technology expanded supply, but their MPL does not rise. So their MRPL falls and their labor demand "falls hard." In the made-up numbers: 2 returns an hour at the new $80 price gives MRPL = $160, down 20% from $200.

Technology helps workers it makes more productive (their MPL rises more than the price falls) and hurts workers producing the same thing who cannot use it (their MPL is unchanged while the price falls). Always check both pieces of MRPL = MPL × MR.

In plain words: a new machine is good for you if it raises how much you make by more than it lowers the price of what you make. It is bad for you if you cannot use it, because the price of your product still falls and your output does not rise.

The top 1%: superstars (slide 58). Modern technology may also raise the rewards to the very top-skilled people: "Computers and easy mass communication make it possible for top entertainers, artists, inventors, and entrepreneurs to reap huge rewards." One performer or one app can now reach a whole country or the world, so the best in a field earn far more than the second best.

In plain words: when one person's work can be sold to millions of people at almost no extra cost, buyers flock to the very best, and the best collects a huge share of the money.

The race between education and technology

Skill-biased technological change tends to increase inequality; increases in education tend to decrease it (slide 41). Education does this through supply, in both markets at once. More college graduates shift the high-skill supply curve out, which pushes down the high-skill wage. Every new graduate is also one fewer person without a degree, so the low-skill supply curve shifts in, which pushes up the low-skill wage. In the 1950s and 1960s the two forces roughly balanced. Starting around 1970, increases in education slowed, so skill-biased technological change dominated. His slide 42 chart (Economic Report of the President 2010) shows mean years of schooling, by the year a cohort turned 21 (a cohort is everyone born in the same year), rising steadily from under 8 years around 1900 to about 13 years by the mid-1970s, then rising much more slowly, to about 14 by 2000.

In plain words: technology keeps raising the demand for educated workers, and schooling keeps adding more of them. When schooling grows fast enough, the two wages stay about the same distance apart. After 1970 schooling grew more slowly, so technology won and the gap grew.

Cause 2: globalization

The U.S. tends to have a comparative advantage in goods that use high-skilled labor and a comparative disadvantage in goods that use low-skilled labor (slide 44). As the U.S. opened up to trade over the past four decades, it specialized in and exported high-skill goods and imported low-skill goods. His FRED chart (slide 43) shows both exports and imports rising from around 5% of GDP in the 1960s to well over 10% in recent decades, with imports above exports.

The mechanism runs through prices, and so through MR. The price of high-skill goods rises (foreign buyers now want them), so MRPL rises for high-skilled workers. The price of low-skill goods falls (cheaper imports compete), so MRPL falls for low-skilled workers. In his words, movements in prices "are what bring this specialization about."

In plain words: trade raises the price of what the U.S. sells abroad and lowers the price of what it buys from abroad. Workers who make the exports become worth more to their employers. Workers who make goods that compete with imports become worth less. One group's pay rises while the other's falls.

Globalization and skill-biased technology both widen the gap, but they look different on the graph. Technology shifts only the high-skill demand curve. Globalization shifts both: high-skill demand out and low-skill demand in, because export prices rise and import-competing prices fall. A trade war or tariff runs the price channel in reverse for whichever goods it hits; the soybean question is a case where a U.S. export price collapsed and those workers' MRPL fell.

Cause 3: imperfect labor markets and changing power

Labor markets may be imperfect (slide 47). Employers may not face much competition for workers, so they can use their market power to pay less than MRPL. This is called monopsony: one buyer, or a few buyers, of labor. He leaves the details for ECON 151. Unions can bargain for higher pay, but U.S. union power is declining. His evidence:

  • Private sector union membership (slide 48): rose to roughly a third of workers in the 1950s and fell steadily to well under 10% by 2019.
  • The federal minimum wage adjusted for inflation (slide 49): highest in the late 1960s and drifting down since, with jumps each time Congress raised it and declines in between as inflation eroded it.
  • Changing social norms? (slide 50, Economic Policy Institute): the CEO-to-worker compensation ratio, counting stock options when they are cashed in ("realized"), was 19.9 in 1965, peaked at 368.1 in 2000, and was 278.1 in 2018. Counting options when they are granted instead, the same three numbers are 15.5, 386.4, and 221.0. The chart's headline: "CEOs make 278 times more than typical workers."

In plain words: when employers have few rivals for workers, or when unions and the real minimum wage get weaker, workers at the bottom have less power to push their pay up, while pay at the very top keeps climbing.

What to do about it: three categories of remedies

Slide 51 gives three broad categories.

1. Education and job training (slides 52 and 53). Education can be a great equalizer, and it is not only college: preschool, K-12, and job training all matter. Nobelist James Heckman and others argue that early childhood education has the highest returns. His chart shows the rate of return on investments in disadvantaged children is highest for preschool programs, lower for schooling, and lowest for job training later in life, falling below the opportunity cost of funds at older ages. The catch: "These investments won't change inequality quickly."

2. Policies for specific groups of workers hurt by technology or trade (slide 54). Trade Adjustment Assistance helps workers who lose jobs because of trade with job training, job search help, extra unemployment benefits, and a temporary wage subsidy after reemployment. His verdict: "It's tiny and not used much." Place-based policies give tax benefits, subsidies, and other incentives for employers to come into a troubled area, and fund job training suited to local employers.

3. Policies to raise incomes at the bottom directly (slide 55): the minimum wage, the Earned Income Tax Credit, and direct cash payments such as a universal basic income (a fixed cash payment to everyone, whether or not they work).

In plain words: there are three ways to help. Give people more skills, which works but slowly. Help the particular workers who lost out to trade or technology. Or add money at the bottom directly.

The EITC is a wage subsidy

The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) pays low-wage workers extra money for working, on top of their wage (slides 56 and 57). Because workers now receive W + EITC for each hour, they are willing to supply each quantity of labor at a wage that is lower by the amount of the credit. For example, with a $4 credit, a worker who needed $18 an hour from an employer before will now take $14 from the employer, because $14 + $4 = $18. So the labor supply curve shifts down (out) by the size of the credit. The new equilibrium has more employment, a lower wage paid by employers (W2), and a higher total received by workers (W2 + EITC, above the old W1).

EITC: supply shifts down by the credit. Employment rises, the wage employers pay falls, and what workers take home (W2 + EITC) rises. Minimum wage above equilibrium: the wage rises for those who keep jobs, but employment falls and unemployment appears.

In plain words: the EITC adds government money to every hour a low-wage person works, so more people want those jobs. Employers can then pay a little less, workers still take home more, and more people end up working. A minimum wage raises pay by law instead, so each worker costs the employer more and firms hire fewer people.

Back to immigration: the Mariel boatlift

He opened Class 13 by returning to Card's Mariel study from Class 12 (slides 5 to 13). Low-skilled immigration shifts labor supply out, so theory says the wage falls. Comparing cities is misleading because immigrants are "not randomly applied treatments": they may go where labor demand is growing. In 1980, 125,000 Cubans arrived, almost all in Miami, for political reasons, so it was a natural experiment. A natural experiment is an event that changes one thing for reasons unrelated to what you are studying, so it works like a controlled test. Card found "No obvious effects of the Marielitos on average earnings" (slide 10) and "no obvious effect on inequality in wages among non-Cubans in Miami" (slide 11). For example, log real hourly earnings of Black workers in Miami went from 1.59 in 1979 to 1.60 in 1985, while in the comparison cities they fell from 1.74 to 1.65. (A small change in a log is roughly a percent change, so +0.01 is about +1%.) Card's explanations (slide 12): some migration to Miami that otherwise would have happened did not, and labor demand "may have been quite elastic," because Miami had industries that used low-skilled workers and could expand easily.

In plain words: a sudden wave of low-skill workers should push low-skill pay down. In Miami it barely did, possibly because local businesses could easily grow and hire the newcomers without cutting pay much.

The 2025 Nobel Prize (opening of the Fall 2025 class)

Slide 4: the 2025 prize went to Joel Mokyr (50%), Philippe Aghion (25%), and Peter Howitt (25%), for work on economic growth, following the 2024 prize (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson) and the 2018 prize (Nordhaus and Romer). Mokyr, an economic historian, showed how scientific innovation must connect to practical and commercial knowledge and to social acceptance of change. Aghion and Howitt modeled growth as creative destruction: innovations are profitable because patents give temporary monopolies, and skilled labor is devoted either to research and development or to manufacturing.

In plain words: new products and methods keep replacing old ones. Firms keep inventing because a patent lets them be the only seller for a while and earn extra profit. This prize is about growth, which is why it ties to the growth unit more than to inequality.

Worked examples

Example 1: Computing top shares

A made-up country has 1,000 households with total income of $80 million. The top 100 households earn $36 million, and of those, the top 10 earn $12 million and the next 40 earn $14 million.

  1. Top 10% (top 100 of 1,000): $36 million ÷ $80 million = 45%.
  2. Top 1% (top 10): $12 million ÷ $80 million = 15%.
  3. P95-99 (the next 40 households): $14 million ÷ $80 million = 17.5%.
  4. P90-95 (the remaining 50 of the top 100): $36 − $12 − $14 = $10 million, so 10 ÷ 80 = 12.5%. Check: 15 + 17.5 + 12.5 = 45.
  5. Bottom 90%: $80 − $36 = $44 million, or 55%.

Answer: top 10% share 45%, top 1% 15%, P95-99 17.5%, P90-95 12.5%, bottom 90% 55%.

Example 2: The Piketty and Saez calculation

Suppose national accounts say total personal income is $20 trillion, the Census says there are 150 million tax units, and tax returns show the richest 1.5 million returns reported $4 trillion.

  1. The top 1% of 150 million tax units is 1.5 million units, which is exactly the group the tax data describe.
  2. Their share is $4 trillion ÷ $20 trillion = 20%.
  3. A survey would not give this, because it reaches few households at the very top.

Answer: a top 1% income share of 20%. Each piece of data comes from a different source: total income from the national accounts, the count of tax units from the Census, the top group's income from tax returns.

Example 3: Skill-biased technological change in numbers

Low-skill market: demand W = 24 − 2L, supply W = 3L − 6. High-skill market: demand W = 40 − 2L, supply W = 3L − 10 (W in dollars per hour, L in millions). New technology shifts high-skill demand to W = 50 − 2L.

  1. Low-skill: 24 − 2L = 3L − 6, so 30 = 5L, L = 6, W = 24 − 12 = $12.
  2. High-skill before: 40 − 2L = 3L − 10, so 50 = 5L, L = 10, W = 40 − 20 = $20. Gap $8; ratio 20 ÷ 12 ≈ 1.67.
  3. High-skill after: 50 − 2L = 3L − 10, so 60 = 5L, L = 12, W = 50 − 24 = $26.
  4. Low-skill is unchanged at $12. Gap $14; ratio 26 ÷ 12 ≈ 2.17.

Answer: the high-skill wage rises from $20 to $26, the low-skill wage stays at $12, and the gap grows from $8 to $14.

Example 4: Education closes the gap

Continue from Example 3. More people finish college: high-skill supply shifts to W = 3L − 25, and low-skill supply shifts to W = 3L − 1.

  1. High-skill: 50 − 2L = 3L − 25, so 75 = 5L, L = 15, W = 50 − 30 = $20.
  2. Low-skill: 24 − 2L = 3L − 1, so 25 = 5L, L = 5, W = 24 − 10 = $14.
  3. Gap: 20 − 14 = $6, down from $14. Ratio 20 ÷ 14 ≈ 1.43.

Answer: education pushes the high-skill wage down to $20 and the low-skill wage up to $14, shrinking the gap to $6. That is the "race": when education keeps pace with technology, inequality does not rise.

Example 5: Globalization in numbers

Start from Example 3's original markets. Trade lowers the price of low-skill goods, so low-skill demand becomes W = 19 − 2L, and raises the price of high-skill goods, so high-skill demand becomes W = 45 − 2L.

  1. Low-skill: 19 − 2L = 3L − 6, so 25 = 5L, L = 5, W = 19 − 10 = $9.
  2. High-skill: 45 − 2L = 3L − 10, so 55 = 5L, L = 11, W = 45 − 22 = $23.
  3. Gap: 23 − 9 = $14, up from $8.

Answer: the low-skill wage falls from $12 to $9 and the high-skill wage rises from $20 to $23. Both markets move, in opposite directions.

Example 6: Tax software and two kinds of preparers

Before software, each preparer finishes 2 returns an hour, and a return sells for $100. Software lets those who adopt it finish 3 an hour, and the extra supply drives the price to $80.

  1. Before: MRPL = MPL × MR = 2 × $100 = $200 per hour.
  2. Adopters after: 3 × $80 = $240. Up 20% (1.5 × 0.8 = 1.2). Their labor demand shifts out.
  3. Non-adopters after: 2 × $80 = $160. Down 20% (1 × 0.8). Their labor demand shifts in.
  4. Break-even price for adopters: MRPL stays at $200 if 3 × P = 200, so P ≈ $66.67, a fall of one third.

Answer: the same technology raises adopters' MRPL to $240 and cuts non-adopters' MRPL to $160. Adopters would only lose if the price fell by more than a third.

Example 7: EITC versus minimum wage

Low-wage labor market: demand W = 30 − 2L, supply W = 6 + 2L (L in thousands). Compare a $4 per hour EITC with a $22 minimum wage.

  1. No policy: 30 − 2L = 6 + 2L, L = 6, W = $18.
  2. EITC: workers accept $4 less from employers, so supply becomes W = 2 + 2L. 30 − 2L = 2 + 2L gives L = 7 and W = $16 paid by employers. Workers receive $16 + $4 = $20. Government cost: $4 × 7 thousand = $28 thousand per hour.
  3. Minimum wage of $22: firms hire where 30 − 2L = 22, L = 4. Workers who want jobs: 6 + 2L = 22, L = 8. Unemployment 4 thousand.

Answer: the EITC raises employment (6 to 7 thousand) and raises take-home pay ($18 to $20), paid for by taxpayers. The minimum wage raises pay for those employed ($18 to $22) but cuts employment to 4 thousand. Note the EITC's $4 is shared: workers gain $2 and employers pay $2 less.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Drawing skill-biased technological change as a shift in high-skill supply.

Correct: Technology raises high-skill workers' MPL, so it shifts high-skill demand (MRPL) out. Supply shifts come from changes in the number of workers with the skill, such as education or immigration.

Mistake: Shifting the low-skill demand curve for skill-biased technological change.

Correct: His slide 38: it "doesn't affect the wage of most low-skilled workers." Only the high-skill market changes. (The exception is workers producing the same product who cannot switch, slides 39 and 40.)

Mistake: Explaining globalization's effect on low-skilled workers as "foreign workers take the jobs" without the mechanism.

Correct: Say it through prices: imports lower the price of low-skill goods, so MR falls, so MRPL = MPL × MR falls and low-skill labor demand shifts in. For high-skill goods the price rises and demand shifts out.

Mistake: Shifting labor demand for the EITC, or showing the EITC lowering employment.

Correct: The EITC is paid to workers, so it shifts labor supply down by the credit. Employment rises; the wage employers pay falls; workers' total pay rises.

Mistake: Saying education reduces inequality by shifting demand.

Correct: Education changes the number of workers with each skill, so it shifts supply: high-skill supply out (wage down) and low-skill supply in (wage up).

Mistake: Thinking top income shares come from surveys.

Correct: Piketty and Saez used tax returns, because surveys tell us little about incomes at the very top.

Mistake: Assuming new technology always helps every worker in the industry.

Correct: Check MPL × MR. If the price falls and a group's MPL does not rise, their MRPL falls (non-adopting tax preparers: $200 to $160).

Mistake: Reading a Lorenz curve backwards, as if a curve that bows far below the 45-degree line meant more equality.

Correct: The 45-degree line is perfect equality. The further the Lorenz curve bows away from it, the more unequal the distribution and the higher the Gini.

Mistake: Putting the area under the Lorenz curve (B) on top of the Gini fraction.

Correct: Gini = A ÷ (A + B), where A is the gap between the 45-degree line and the curve. Because A + B = 0.5, Gini = 1 − 2B. If B = 0.29, the Gini is 0.42, not 0.58.

Mistake: Ignoring the hidden tax in benefit phase-outs.

Correct: When a benefit shrinks as earnings rise, each extra dollar earned loses part of the benefit. A 40-cent cut per dollar is a 40% implicit marginal tax rate, and a dollar-for-dollar cut is 100%, which leaves no reward for earning more in that range.

Mistake: Treating a minimum wage and the EITC as two versions of the same policy.

Correct: The EITC is paid to workers by taxpayers, so it raises take-home pay without raising what employers pay, and employment rises. A binding minimum wage raises employers' cost of labor, so employment falls.

Mistake: Mixing up income inequality with wealth inequality, or one year's income with lifetime income.

Correct: Income is a flow received each year; wealth is a stock of what people own. Wealth is usually more unequal than income. And one year's income can look unequal partly because people are at different ages and stages of their careers.

Mistake: Treating a subsidy paid to employers for each low-skilled worker as the same graph as the EITC.

Correct: A subsidy paid to employers makes each worker worth more to the firm, so it shifts labor demand up by the subsidy. The EITC is paid to workers, so it shifts labor supply down. With the same dollar amount in the same market, both end with the same employment, the same take-home pay, and the same cost to employers; only the curve you draw differs.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Draw two side-by-side labor markets first, labeled "Low-skill" and "High-skill," with WL and WH marked. Most inequality questions are answered by deciding which of the four curves moves.
  • Write MRPL = MPL × MR in the margin and mark each piece up, down, or unchanged. A change in productivity or in the price of the product moves demand. A change in how many people have the skill, or a payment made to workers, moves supply.
  • Ask who receives the money in a policy story. Paid to workers (EITC): supply shifts down. Paid to employers: demand shifts up. Set by law above equilibrium (minimum wage, union wage): draw a horizontal line and read employment off demand.
  • For numbers, set demand equal to supply, solve for L, and plug L back into either curve for W. Then compute both the gap (WH − WL) and the ratio (WH ÷ WL) and say which one the question asked for.
  • End every inequality answer with one sentence such as "WH rises relative to WL, so inequality rises," and name the cause in MRPL terms (MPL rose, or MR fell).

How it shows up on the final

The Fall 2025 final had no question labeled "inequality." But the machinery of this class was all over question 4: a trade shock changed the price of an exported good, which changed MR, which shifted labor demand (MRPL = MR × MPL) for the workers who make it, and part (d) asked who won and who lost. That is the globalization slide (slide 46) run in reverse for one industry. Expect the final to use these tools in a story such as:

  • AI or other new technology raising some workers' MPL (skill-biased change), with a two-market graph and a question about the wage gap.
  • Tariffs or a trade war changing the price of goods made by low-skilled or high-skilled labor, and what that does to each group's MRPL and wage.
  • A policy comparison: minimum wage versus EITC, drawn on one labor market, with who gains and who pays.
  • Immigration (or immigration enforcement) as a labor supply shift, with Card's elastic-demand point.
  • Multiple choice on facts: what a fractile is, why tax data, the three remedies, Heckman's early childhood finding, the 2025 Nobel.

When a question says "inequality," draw two side-by-side labor markets (low-skill and high-skill), shift whichever curves the story moves, and finish with one sentence comparing the two new wages: "WH rises relative to WL, so inequality rises." Name the cause in MRPL terms (MPL rose, or MR fell).

More practice: other Berkeley exams and the textbook

These come from Emmanuel Saez's ECON 2 exams, Martha Olney's ECON 1 problem sets, and the textbook by Frank, Bernanke, Antonovics, and Heffetz, rewritten in Edwards' notation (W for the wage, L for workers, MRPL = MPL × MR). Several use the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, which Saez tests but Edwards' Class 13 slides do not cover, so give them lower priority. The two definitions you need:

  • The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of people, poorest first, on the horizontal axis against the cumulative share of income they receive on the vertical axis. If everyone earned the same, it would be the 45-degree line. The further it bows below that line, the more unequal the distribution.
  • The Gini coefficient is A ÷ (A + B), where A is the area between the 45-degree line and the Lorenz curve and B is the area under the Lorenz curve. Since A + B = 0.5, Gini = 1 − 2B. It runs from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one person has everything). With only two groups, a shortcut works: Gini = the top group's income share minus the top group's share of the population.

(a) (2 points) What is the income share of the bottom 90%? Sketch the Lorenz curve and find the Gini coefficient.

Gini = 1 − 2 × 0.425 = 0.15. The two-group shortcut agrees: 25% − 10% = 0.15.

Full credit: 75%, the kinked Lorenz curve through (90%, 75%), and Gini = 0.15.

(b) Wages fall to 0.8 × 75 = 60 per firm, so workers get 60% and owners 40%. B = 0.5 × 0.9 × 0.6 + 0.1 × (0.6 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0.27 + 0.08 = 0.35. Gini = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30 (shortcut: 40% − 10%). The Lorenz curve bows further from the 45-degree line.

Full credit: 60% and Gini = 0.30.

(c) Each worker now gets 95 ÷ 9 ≈ 10.56 and each owner gets 5, so the owners are now the bottom 10%, with 5 × 5 = 25 out of 500, a 5% share. The Lorenz curve runs from (0, 0) to (10%, 5%), then to (100%, 100%). B = 0.5 × 0.1 × 0.05 + 0.9 × (0.05 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0.0025 + 0.4725 = 0.475. Gini = 1 − 0.95 = 0.05 (shortcut: the top 90% has 95%, and 95% − 90% = 0.05).

Full credit: owners are the bottom 10%, 5% share, Gini = 0.05.

(d) Labor supply must be perfectly inelastic (vertical): workers keep working the same amount at the lower wage. If supply slopes up, a wage cut makes some workers stop working, so employment and output fall. (Firms that can set the wage like this have market power over workers, which is monopsony. Edwards leaves monopsony to ECON 151.)

Full credit: perfectly inelastic (vertical) labor supply.

(a) technology that raises the productivity of high-skilled workers; (b) on-the-job training that raises the productivity only of workers who already have college degrees; (c) a rise in the world price of goods made by low-skilled U.S. workers. (ii) (a) and (c)
(iii) all three

(i). (a) and (b) raise the MPL of high-skilled workers, so their MRPL = MPL × MR rises, high-skill labor demand shifts out, and their wage pulls away. (c) raises MR for low-skilled workers, so their MRPL and wage rise, which narrows the gap. 4 points The top 10% income share in the U.S. was high before World War II, low from the 1940s to the 1970s, and has risen since. Give two labor-market explanations for the rise since the 1970s, and explain each with labor supply and demand. Adapted from Olney ECON 1 Fall 2019 problem set 3, Q3, and Edwards' Class 13 slides

Show answer

Any two of these, each with its curve:

Skill-biased technological change. Computers and new technology raised the MPL of high-skilled workers, so their MRPL rose and high-skill labor demand shifted out. Their wage rose relative to low-skilled workers, whose market did not change.

Globalization. More imports of goods made with low-skilled labor lowered the price, so the MR, of what U.S. low-skilled workers make. Their MRPL fell and their labor demand shifted in. Exports of goods made with high-skilled labor grew, so high-skill demand shifted out.

Education slowed. Growth in the number of college graduates slowed around 1970, so high-skill supply did not shift out fast enough to keep up with demand, and the high-skill wage kept rising.

Imperfect labor markets. Falling union membership and a falling real minimum wage removed forces that had held up pay at the middle and bottom, and employers with market power can pay less than MRPL.

Full credit: two distinct causes, each naming the market and the curve that shifts (or the wage floor that eroded) and the effect on the wage gap.

2 points Explain why wealth inequality is usually larger than income inequality, and why rising labor income inequality can feed rising wealth inequality. Built from Edwards' Class 13 slides (three kinds of inequality)

Show answer

Wealth is accumulated saving plus the returns on it. High earners can save a large part of their income, while low earners save little or nothing, so income gaps pile up year after year into much bigger wealth gaps, and wealth then earns more income from capital. When top labor incomes grow faster, the top saves more and the wealth gap widens over time. Edwards' slide 16 makes the same link: rising labor income inequality is an important source of rising wealth inequality.

Full credit: wealth is a stock built from saving, the rich save more, and higher top earnings mean more saving at the top.

2 points Over a decade, the high-skill wage and high-skill employment both rose, while the low-skill wage and low-skill employment both fell. Which single change best explains all four facts?

(i) Skill-biased technological change
(ii) Greater openness to international trade
(iii) More people finishing college
(iv) More low-skilled immigration

Show answer

(ii). When wage and employment move in the same direction, the demand curve shifted. Here both markets' demand curves moved, in opposite directions. That is globalization (slides 44 to 46): the price of high-skill goods rises, so high-skill demand shifts out, and the price of low-skill goods falls, so low-skill demand shifts in. (i) is wrong because skill-biased technological change moves only the high-skill market; the low-skill wage would stay the same. (iii) is wrong because more graduates would lower the high-skill wage and raise the low-skill wage. (iv) is wrong because a low-skill supply shift lowers the low-skill wage but raises low-skill employment, and it leaves the high-skill market alone.

Full credit: circle (ii).

6 points Paying the boss instead. A low-wage labor market has demand W = 30 − 2L and supply W = 6 + 2L (L in thousands, W in dollars per hour). Without policy, L = 6 thousand and W = $18. The government pays employers $4 for every hour of low-skilled labor they hire. Adapted from his Fall 2026 Problem Set 3, Q2(b)

(a) (2 points) Which curve shifts, and by how much? Find the new employment, the wage workers receive, and what employers pay after the subsidy.

(b) (2 points) Compare your answer with a $4 EITC in the same market (Example 7). What is the same and what is different?

(c) (2 points) What happens to income inequality, and who pays for the policy?

Show answer

(a) The money goes to employers, so each worker is worth $4 more an hour to the firm: the firm's willingness to pay is MRPL + $4. Labor demand shifts up by $4, from W = 30 − 2L to W = 34 − 2L. Set demand equal to supply: 34 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 28 = 4L and L = 7 thousand. The wage is W = 6 + 2 × 7 = $20, and workers receive all of it. Employers pay $20 but get $4 back, so their cost is $20 − $4 = $16 an hour.

Full credit: demand shifts up by $4; 7 thousand employed; workers get $20; employers' net cost $16.

(b) The outcomes are identical: with a $4 EITC, employment is 7 thousand, employers pay $16, and workers receive $16 + $4 = $20. The government spends $4 × 7 thousand = $28 thousand an hour either way. What differs is the graph and the market wage you label. With the EITC, supply shifts down by $4 and the market wage is the $16 employers pay. With the employer subsidy, demand shifts up by $4 and the market wage is the $20 workers receive. Who gets the check does not decide who gains.

Full credit: same employment, take-home pay, and employer cost; EITC shifts supply, employer subsidy shifts demand.

(c) Low-skilled workers' pay rises from $18 to $20 and their employment rises from 6 to 7 thousand, while nothing changes in the high-skill market. WL rises relative to WH, so inequality falls. Taxpayers pay, $28 thousand an hour.

Full credit: low-skill wage and employment rise, inequality falls, taxpayers pay.

In the soybean industry, the marginal product of labor is MPL = 6 − 0.2L bushels per hour when L thousand workers are employed (each added worker adds a little less). Labor supply is W = 6 + L. (b) Before: 30 − L = 6 + L, so 24 = 2L, L = 12 thousand, and W = 6 + 12 = $18. After: 24 − 0.8L = 6 + L, so 18 = 1.8L, L = 10 thousand, and W = 6 + 10 = $16. Check with demand: at L = 10, MPL = 6 − 2 = 4 bushels, and 4 × $4 = $16.

(c) Wage on the vertical axis, employment on the horizontal. One upward-sloping supply curve. MRPL1 and, below it, MRPL2, with an arrow pointing down and to the left. (d) The wage fell from $18 to $16, which is $2 ÷ $18 ≈ 11.1%. Two things keep it above a 20% fall. At the old employment of 12 thousand, MRPL fell 20%, from $18 to 4 × 3.6 = $14.40. One-minute review

Quick check

New software makes programmers more productive. Which curve shifts?

High-skill labor demand shifts out, because their MPL rises, so MRPL = MPL × MR rises.

More people finish college. Which curves shift?

Supply in both markets: high-skill supply out and low-skill supply in, so the wage gap shrinks.

Imports cut the price of goods made by low-skilled workers. What happens to their labor demand?

It shifts in, because MR falls, so MRPL = MPL × MR falls at every level of employment.

Does the EITC shift labor supply or labor demand?

Supply, down by the credit, because the money is paid to workers; a subsidy paid to employers would shift demand instead.

What is a fractile?

A slice of a distribution defined by percentiles, such as P90-95 or the 99th percentile and above (slide 21).

Why did the Mariel boatlift barely lower wages in Miami?

Labor demand may have been quite elastic, and some migration that would otherwise have come did not (slide 12).

  • Three types: opportunity, wealth, income. He focuses on labor income inequality, the main source of rising U.S. income inequality.
  • Measure: top income shares by fractile (a slice defined by percentiles). Piketty and Saez used tax returns from 1913 on, because surveys miss the top.
  • History: high before World War II, low up to the 1970s, rising strongly since; labor income inequality started rising in the early 1970s, and the very top rose fastest.
  • Tool: two labor markets, low-skill and high-skill, each with demand = MRPL = MPL × MR. Inequality rises when WH pulls away from WL.
  • Skill-biased technological change raises high-skill MPL, so only high-skill demand shifts out.
  • Technology also lowers product prices: adopters gain if MPL rises faster than the price falls (tax preparers); workers who cannot switch lose.
  • Race between education and technology: education shifts high-skill supply out and low-skill supply in. Education slowed around 1970, so technology won.
  • Globalization: export prices of high-skill goods rise and prices of low-skill goods fall, so high-skill demand shifts out and low-skill demand shifts in.
  • Imperfect markets: employer market power, falling union membership, a falling real minimum wage, and CEO pay 278 times typical workers' in 2018.
  • Remedies: education (Heckman: early childhood has the highest returns, but slow); targeted help (Trade Adjustment Assistance, "tiny"; place-based policies); direct help (minimum wage, EITC, cash).
  • EITC shifts labor supply down by the credit: more jobs, lower wage paid by employers, higher take-home pay. A binding minimum wage raises pay but cuts jobs.
  • Card's Mariel study: no obvious effect on wages or wage inequality in Miami, probably because labor demand was very elastic.
