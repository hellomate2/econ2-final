Class 17 · Macroeconomic Measurement

GDP, prices, and unemployment

This unit is the measuring kit for all of macroeconomics: what gross domestic product (GDP) counts, how spending splits into Y = C + I + G + NX, how a price index removes inflation so you can compare dollars across years, and how the unemployment rate is built. On the Fall 2025 final it was worth a direct 2 points in question 2(a), the Big Mac versus consumer price index (CPI) real-price question, and it was the foundation of the 24-point Keynesian cross question, which started from U.S. GDP of about $30 trillion and built planned aggregate expenditure (PAE, total planned spending) as PAE = C + I + G + NX.

The ideas, explained

What macroeconomics measures

Macroeconomics is the study of the aggregate economy, the whole economy added up. Edwards paraphrases David Romer on why we need models at all: reality is already a complicated model, so we need a simpler one. The big things macro tracks are total output, the overall price level and inflation, the unemployment rate, and the overall level of interest rates, the exchange rate, and total exports and imports (Class 17, slide 17). This unit defines the first three.

GDP: the definition, word by word

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the market value of the final goods and services newly produced in a country during some period of time. Edwards highlights four key points (slide 23), and almost every "does this count?" question is one of them:

  • Final goods and services. Count only goods sold to their final user. The flour a bakery buys is an intermediate good; its value is already inside the price of the bread, so counting both would count the flour twice.
  • Newly produced. Count only things made in this period. A used car, an existing house, or a 1990 painting resold this year adds nothing new, so its sale price is not counted. (The dealer's or realtor's fee is a new service, so that part counts.)
  • Within the country. GDP is about where production happens, not who owns the firm. A Toyota built in Kentucky counts in U.S. GDP; a U.S.-owned factory's output in Mexico counts in Mexico's.
  • In some period of time, usually a quarter or a year. A car built in 2026 counts in 2026 GDP even if it is sold in 2027; while it sits unsold, it counts as inventory investment.

"Market value" means we use market prices to add apples and haircuts into one number. That is why GDP leaves out things with no market price: cleaning your own house, cooking your own dinner, leisure, and (by the textbook's account) illegal activity that is never reported. Edwards' final review used this example for trade-offs: cleaning your own house costs you the time you could have "traded in a labor market." The cleaning is real value, but it is not in GDP unless you hire a housekeeper.

Three ways to measure GDP

GDP measures total income, and total income also equals total spending and total production of new goods and services (slide 15). Each gives a way to measure it (slide 24):

  • Expenditure approach: add up spending on final goods at market prices, split into consumption (C), investment (I), government purchases (G), and net exports (NX).
  • Production (value added) approach: follow goods through each stage of production and add the value each stage adds, which is its sales minus what it paid for intermediate goods.
  • Income approach: add up the income earned from producing new goods and services in the country, split into labor income (wages and benefits) and capital income (profits, rent, interest).

Why income equals spending: every dollar a buyer spends on a new product is a dollar of income to someone, the workers, owners, and lenders who made it. Edwards uses the same fact in his argument about inflation: "your expenditure is somebody's income."

Example: value added adds up to the final price

A farmer grows wheat and sells it to a miller for $1.00. The miller sells flour to a baker for $1.50. The baker sells the bread to you for $3.00. Assume the farmer bought no inputs.

StageSalesPaid for inputsValue added
Farmer (wheat)$1.00$0.00$1.00
Miller (flour)$1.50$1.00$0.50
Baker (bread)$3.00$1.50$1.50
Total$5.50$3.00

Answer: GDP rises by $3.00, the price of the final good, which equals the sum of value added. Adding all sales ($5.50) would count the wheat three times and the flour twice.

The expenditure identity: Y = C + I + G + NX

Edwards' slide 15: "Viewed as total spending, GDP is Y = C + I + G + EX – IM." Here Y is GDP, and:

  • C, consumption: household spending on goods and services (food, rent, phones, haircuts, tuition).
  • I, investment: spending on new capital. That means business equipment and structures (a new data center, a delivery truck), new houses, and the change in firms' inventories. Buying stocks or bonds is not investment in GDP; it only moves ownership of existing assets.
  • G, government purchases: government spending on goods and services, including the salaries of teachers and soldiers and the cost of roads. Transfer payments such as Social Security, unemployment benefits, and SNAP food benefits are not in G, because the government buys nothing in return. They show up in C when the people who receive them spend them.
  • NX, net exports = exports (EX) minus imports (IM). Exports are U.S.-made goods bought by foreigners, so they count. Imports are subtracted because C, I, and G already include spending on imported goods, which were not produced here.

Y = C + I + G + NX, with NX = EX − IM. It holds as an identity: every final good produced is bought by one of these four groups, or counted as inventory investment if it is not sold. Buying an imported TV raises C and IM by the same amount, so GDP does not change.

Size: U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion for about 340 million people, which is about $88,000 per person (slide 15). GDP per person is average income. The Fall 2025 final's question 3 began from the same "$30 trillion" and modeled planned spending as PAE = C + I + G + NX with NX = −1 trillion, a trade deficit.

Nominal versus real

Economists use "nominal" to mean measured in dollars and "real" to mean adjusted for changes in prices, which is the same as measured in goods and services rather than dollars (slide 19). "Adjusting for" means removing.

  • Nominal GDP values each year's output at that year's prices: Nominal GDP2022 = Σi Pi,2022 × Qi,2022, where i runs over every good and Σ means "add up."
  • Real GDP values each year's output at the prices of one fixed base year: with base year 2012, Real GDP2022 = Σi Pi,2012 × Qi,2022. It answers: how much would everything made in 2022 have been worth at 2012 prices?

Nominal GDP can rise because we made more stuff or because prices rose. Real GDP holds prices fixed, so it rises only when quantities rise. Slide 16 puts it compactly: nominal GDP is total cash spending, YN = P × Q, and real GDP is "basically just Q."

The growth rate of any variable X from period t − 1 to period t is (Xt − Xt−1) ÷ Xt−1 × 100. Real GDP growth uses real GDP in that formula.

The textbook also defines the GDP deflator, a price index for everything in GDP: GDP deflator = (nominal GDP ÷ real GDP) × 100. In the base year nominal and real GDP are equal, so the deflator is 100.

Example: nominal GDP, real GDP, and the deflator

An economy makes pizzas and haircuts. Base year 2024.

YearPizzasPrice of pizzaHaircutsPrice of haircut
2024100$1050$20
2025110$1250$22
  1. Nominal 2024 = 100 × $10 + 50 × $20 = $1,000 + $1,000 = $2,000.
  2. Nominal 2025 = 110 × $12 + 50 × $22 = $1,320 + $1,100 = $2,420. Nominal growth = (2,420 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 21%.
  3. Real 2025 at 2024 prices = 110 × $10 + 50 × $20 = $1,100 + $1,000 = $2,100. Real 2024 = $2,000 (base year). Real growth = (2,100 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 5%.
  4. Deflator 2025 = 2,420 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = 115.24, so prices of GDP goods rose about 15.24%.

Answer: Nominal GDP grew 21%, but only 5% was more output; the rest was higher prices. Real growth is roughly nominal growth minus inflation (21% − 15.24% is about 5.8%; the exact answer is 1.21 ÷ 1.1524 − 1 = 5%, and the shortcut works best when both numbers are small).

Comparing countries: China and the U.S.

Edwards opened this class (slides 13 and 14) by asking how big China's economy is compared with the U.S. economy. China's 2024 nominal GDP, from its National Bureau of Statistics, was ¥134,908.40 billion. U.S. 2024 nominal GDP, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) via FRED (the St. Louis Fed's data site), was $29,298.013 billion. Those cannot be compared directly, because yuan and dollars are different units. His point: "Currency units are great, except when they come to have different meanings, like across space or across time."

The fix is the same idea as real GDP. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) converts both into "international dollars" meant to achieve purchasing power parity (PPP), which values each country's output at a common set of prices. In 2024 PPP dollars, the IMF estimates China at $38,210.00 billion and the U.S. at $29,300.00 billion. Prices for many goods and services are lower in China, so a yuan buys more there than its market exchange rate suggests, and PPP shows a larger Chinese economy than a simple currency conversion would.

The price level and the CPI

The price level P is the expenditure-weighted average price for the average consumer (slide 16). Consumers buy a basket of goods and services, the prices in it change by different amounts, and consumers change what is in the basket in response.

The most common measure is the consumer price index (CPI), published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Slide 33: choose a base year, and always use the base year's quantities to multiply each year's prices. The quantities are the weights.

CPI2022 = (Σi Pi,2022 × Qi,1983) ÷ (Σi Pi,1983 × Qi,1983) × 100. In words: the cost of the base-year basket at this year's prices, divided by the cost of the same basket at base-year prices, times 100. The CPI is 100 in the base year.

Contrast with real GDP: real GDP fixes prices and lets quantities change; the CPI fixes quantities and lets prices change. The CPI also covers only what consumers buy, while the GDP deflator covers everything produced domestically.

Example: building a CPI

Use the pizza and haircut economy as the consumer basket, with the 2024 quantities (100 pizzas, 50 haircuts) as the weights.

  1. Basket cost at 2024 prices: 100 × $10 + 50 × $20 = $2,000.
  2. Same basket at 2025 prices: 100 × $12 + 50 × $22 = $1,200 + $1,100 = $2,300.
  3. CPI2025 = 2,300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 115.0. CPI2024 = 100.
  4. Inflation = (115 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 15%.

Answer: CPI inflation is 15.0%, slightly different from the deflator's 15.24%, because the CPI weights by the old basket (100 pizzas) while the deflator weights by current production (110 pizzas, whose price rose 20%).

Updating the basket. A basket fixed in 1983 has a problem: smartphones and electric cars did not exist then. The BLS now updates the basket every year. With a changing basket you can measure inflation with the old basket (a Laspeyres index), the new basket (a Paasche index), or their geometric average (a Fisher index), often "chained" year to year. Edwards says plainly this is "Not on the exam" (slide 34).

Quality changes and new goods (slide 46). If a good gets better and its price rises, the BLS tries to remove the part of the price increase that pays for the better quality and counts only the rest as inflation. New goods give households new ways to get utility, which the index also tries to account for. The textbook adds substitution bias: when one good gets relatively expensive, people switch to cheaper substitutes, but a fixed basket assumes they keep buying the same amounts, so it overstates how much the cost of living rose.

Inflation

Inflation is growth in the overall level of prices, the percent change in a price index. Economists write it as π.

π2022 = (CPI2022 − CPI2021) ÷ CPI2021 × 100. If π is negative, prices are falling, which is called deflation.

Examples: the CPI goes from 300 to 309, so π = 9 ÷ 300 = 3%. If it then falls from 309 to 306, π = −3 ÷ 309 = −0.97%, a small deflation. Divide by the earlier year's index.

His charts (slides 36 to 41) show U.S. inflation measured with the price index for personal consumption expenditures since 1953, deflation during 1930 to 1933, the CPI inflation spike after 2020 (circled on slide 38), chronically high inflation in Argentina since 2002, and hyperinflation in Venezuela from 2018 to 2020.

Why inflation matters. Slide 39 starts with an argument for not caring: in aggregate, spending equals income, so inflation raises both. He compares that argument to "the preposterous idea that we might not notice Daylight Savings Time," and lists three arguments for caring. The slide gives only the three words; the explanations below are the standard textbook reasoning.

  • Redistribution. Inflation that people did not expect moves purchasing power between groups. A borrower repays a fixed-dollar loan with dollars that buy less, so the lender loses and the borrower gains. People with fixed nominal incomes lose.
  • Psychology. People dislike rising prices and find inflation stressful and confusing, even when their wages keep up.
  • Efficiency. Inflation makes prices noisier signals, so it is harder to tell whether a price rose because a good is scarcer or because everything rose; that leads to worse decisions. (The textbook also lists tax distortions and the cost of holding less cash.)

Comparing dollars across years

This is the tool behind the Big Mac question: finding what $XA in year A is worth in year B dollars. Let PA and PB be the price index in years A and B (slides 42 and 43).

$XB = $XA × (PB ÷ PA). Equivalently, $XB ÷ PB = $XA ÷ PA: two amounts have the same purchasing power when each divided by its year's price index is equal. To convert to a year's dollars, multiply by that year's index and divide by the other year's.

Edwards' two examples (CPI "now" = 300.536):

  • Cal tuition (slide 44). In-state tuition and fees in 1985 to 1986 were $1,296, when the CPI was 108.94. In today's dollars: $1,296 × 300.536 ÷ 108.94 = $3,575. Actual tuition and fees today are $14,796, so real tuition is much higher today, about 4.1 times the 1985 level.
  • Desktop computers (slide 45). A good desktop cost about $4,000 in 1996, when the CPI was 156.85. In today's dollars: $4,000 × 300.536 ÷ 156.85 = $7,664. Today a good desktop costs about $1,000, so its real price is much lower, about 13% of the 1996 level.

The same comparison works with ratios. If a price rose by a bigger factor than the price index, its real price rose. If it rose by a smaller factor, its real price fell. If the factors are equal, the real price stayed the same.

Fall 2025 final question 2(a): the Big Mac

The Economist reported that a Big Mac cost $1.60 in 1986 and $5.65 in 2021, about 3.5 times higher. The CPI (1982 to 1984 = 100) was about 110 in 1986 and about 271 in 2021, about 2.5 times higher. Did the real price of a Big Mac rise, fall, or stay the same? Equivalently: if your income rose at the rate of inflation, could you buy fewer, more, or the same number of Big Macs?

  1. Ratio method: Big Mac factor = 5.65 ÷ 1.60 = 3.53. CPI factor = 271 ÷ 110 = 2.46. The Big Mac rose by a bigger factor than prices in general, so its real price increased.
  2. Conversion method: the 1986 price in 2021 dollars is $1.60 × 271 ÷ 110 = $3.94. The actual 2021 price, $5.65, is higher.
  3. Size: 5.65 ÷ 3.94 = 1.43, so the real price rose about 43%. (Check: 3.53 ÷ 2.46 = 1.43.)
  4. Income version: an income that grew with the CPI buys fewer Big Macs in 2021, about 1 ÷ 1.43 = 70% as many.

The key's answer: "The CPI rose by a factor of 2.5, while the Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5. Therefore the real price of a Big Mac has increased." Part (b) of the same question followed up with the reason: making and serving a Big Mac takes workers and a restaurant and cannot be automated much, so its marginal cost has not fallen the way factory goods' costs have. That is the same story as the desktop computer, in reverse.

Indexing and real wages

These come from the textbook chapter on prices and inflation, and they use the same conversion formula.

  • A real wage is a nominal wage divided by the price index (times 100), which measures it in base-year dollars. Example: $20 an hour when the CPI is 200 is $10 in base-year dollars; $28 an hour when the CPI is 250 is $11.20. The real wage rose 12%, even though the nominal wage rose 40%.
  • Indexing means automatically raising a payment by the inflation rate so its real value stays the same. Social Security benefits and many labor contracts are indexed to the CPI. Example: a $1,500 monthly benefit, with the CPI rising from 300 to 309 (3% inflation), becomes $1,500 × 309 ÷ 300 = $1,545.
  • The textbook's real interest rate is the nominal interest rate minus inflation, r = i − π. A loan at 5% when inflation is 3% has a real interest rate of 2%: the lender's purchasing power grows only 2% a year.

Unemployment

Slide 30 shows the U.S. unemployment rate from 1948 to 2023, with recessions shaded: the rate jumps in every recession and drifts down in expansions, and its largest spike is in 2020. The definitions come from the textbook (Chapter 17 covers GDP and unemployment together). The BLS surveys households every month and sorts every person age 16 and older into one of three groups:

  • Employed: worked for pay in the survey week, full time or part time (or was temporarily absent from a job).
  • Unemployed: did not work, but was available for work and actively looked for a job in the past four weeks.
  • Not in the labor force: everyone else, such as full-time students, retirees, people caring for family, and people who want a job but have stopped looking.

Labor force = employed + unemployed. Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100. Participation rate = labor force ÷ population age 16+ × 100. The unemployment rate divides by the labor force, not the whole population.

Two known gaps in the official rate (textbook): discouraged workers, who want a job but stopped looking, are not counted as unemployed, which makes the rate look better than reality; and involuntary part-time workers, who want full-time work but can only find part-time, are counted as employed. The textbook also sorts unemployment into frictional (short spells while people search for a good match), structural (long-term mismatch between workers' skills or locations and the available jobs, like the China-shock towns in the trade unit), and cyclical (the extra unemployment during recessions, which is the part tied to the output gap in the Keynesian cross units).

Potential output: the long-run framework

The end of the Class 17 deck sets up the next unit, long-run growth. Macro has two big topics: the long-run trend in output and short-run booms and recessions. Recessions push output below normal for a while, but the economy does not stay depressed forever. Potential output Y* is the output the economy produces when it uses its resources at normal rates; Edwards says "normal output" might be a better name (slide 50). Its three key determinants are labor, capital, and technology.

Y* ÷ POP = (Y* ÷ N*) × (N* ÷ POP), and Y* ÷ N* = f(K* ÷ N*, T). Potential output per person equals normal average labor productivity (output per worker) times the normal employment-to-population ratio, and productivity depends on normal capital per worker K* ÷ N* and technology T.

Example: if normal output per worker is $120,000 and half the population works (N* ÷ POP = 0.5), potential output per person is $60,000. If the employment-to-population ratio rises to 0.55 with productivity unchanged, output per person rises in proportion, to $66,000. Capital includes physical capital (machines, buildings, computers), infrastructure (roads, telecommunications, dams), and human capital (education, job training). Technology is everything that affects output per worker for a given amount of capital per worker: production techniques, management techniques, economic institutions, and local culture.

Small differences in growth rates compound into big differences in living standards (slides 59 and 60). If country A's real income per person grows 1 percentage point a year faster than country B's, A is 1% richer after a year, 2.01% richer after two (1.01 × 1.01 = 1.0201), about twice as rich after 70 years (1.0170 ≈ 2), and more than 7 times as rich after two centuries (1.01200 ≈ 7.3).

Worked examples

Example: three real-price verdicts

For each item, did the real price rise, fall, or stay the same?

  1. A burrito: $4.00 when the CPI was 160, $9.00 when the CPI is 320. CPI factor 2.00, burrito factor 2.25. The old price in today's dollars is $4.00 × 320 ÷ 160 = $8.00, below $9.00. Rose, by 9 ÷ 8 − 1 = 12.5%.
  2. A calculator: $20 when the CPI was 100, $15 when the CPI is 300. The old price in today's dollars is $20 × 3 = $60. Today's $15 is a quarter of that. Fell, by 75%. (Its nominal price fell too, which makes the real fall even bigger.)
  3. A gallon of gas: $1.50 when the CPI was 150, $3.00 when the CPI is 300. Both doubled. Stayed the same.

Answer: Compare the item's price factor with the CPI factor, or convert the old price into today's dollars and compare. Rose, fell, same.

Example: the expenditure identity with numbers

In trillions of dollars: C = 19, I = 5, G = 7, exports = 3, imports = 4. Find GDP and net exports. Then households buy $0.5 trillion more imported TVs, with nothing else changing. What happens to GDP?

  1. NX = 3 − 4 = −1, a trade deficit.
  2. Y = 19 + 5 + 7 + (−1) = 30.
  3. The TVs raise C by 0.5 and imports by 0.5, so NX falls by 0.5. Y = 19.5 + 5 + 7 + (3 − 4.5) = 30.

Answer: GDP is $30 trillion with NX = −$1 trillion (the same numbers as the Fall 2025 final's Keynesian cross). Buying imports leaves GDP unchanged, because the TVs were made abroad; subtracting imports cancels them out of C.

Example: unemployment rate and participation

A country has 250 million people age 16 and older. 152 million are employed and 8 million are unemployed.

  1. Labor force = 152 + 8 = 160 million.
  2. Unemployment rate = 8 ÷ 160 = 5.0%.
  3. Participation rate = 160 ÷ 250 = 64%.
  4. Now 2 million unemployed stop looking. Unemployed = 6, labor force = 158. Unemployment rate = 6 ÷ 158 = 3.8%. Participation = 158 ÷ 250 = 63.2%.

Answer: 5.0% and 64%, then 3.8% and 63.2%. The unemployment rate fell even though the number of jobs did not change, because discouraged workers drop out of the labor force.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying the Big Mac got cheaper in real terms because the CPI went up too, or comparing $5.65 with $1.60 and calling the real price 3.5 times higher.

Correct: Compare growth factors: 3.53 for the Big Mac against 2.46 for the CPI. The Big Mac outpaced inflation, so its real price rose, by about 43%, not by 253%.

Mistake: Flipping the conversion, multiplying by PA ÷ PB when converting old dollars into today's dollars.

Correct: Converting into year B dollars, multiply by PB ÷ PA. Sanity check: with positive inflation, an old amount becomes a bigger number in today's dollars ($1,296 in 1985 becomes $3,575).

Mistake: Counting intermediate goods, used goods, or financial transactions in GDP.

Correct: Only final, newly produced goods and services made in the country count. Flour sold to a baker, a used car, and a share of stock are out. The dealer's markup and the broker's fee are new services, so those count.

Mistake: Putting Social Security checks or SNAP benefits in G.

Correct: They are transfer payments; the government buys no good or service. They enter GDP only through C when they are spent.

Mistake: Calling a stock purchase "investment" in Y = C + I + G + NX.

Correct: In GDP, investment means spending on new capital goods: equipment, structures, new housing, and inventories.

Mistake: Thinking imports are subtracted because they hurt GDP.

Correct: Imports are subtracted only to remove foreign-made goods already counted in C, I, and G. Buying an imported good does not lower GDP by itself.

Mistake: Dividing unemployed people by the whole population.

Correct: Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force, where the labor force is employed plus unemployed. Students, retirees, and discouraged workers are outside it.

Mistake: Mixing up which part is held fixed in real GDP and in the CPI.

Correct: Real GDP fixes base-year prices and uses current quantities. The CPI fixes base-year quantities and uses current prices.

Mistake: Computing inflation by dividing by the later year's index.

Correct: Divide by the earlier year: 300 to 309 is 9 ÷ 300 = 3%, not 9 ÷ 309.

Fall 2025 final, question 2(a) (2 points)

(d) (2 points) In 1986 you earned $20,000 a year, and your income rose exactly with the CPI. How many Big Macs could you buy with a year's income in 1986 and in 2021?

Show answer

(a) $1.60 × 271 ÷ 110 = $3.94.

(b) $5.65 × 110 ÷ 271 = $2.29, more than the $1.60 actually paid in 1986.

(c) 5.65 ÷ 3.94 = 1.43 (or equivalently 2.29 ÷ 1.60 = 1.43, or (5.65 ÷ 1.60) ÷ (271 ÷ 110) = 3.53 ÷ 2.46 = 1.43). The real price rose about 43%.

(d) 1986: $20,000 ÷ $1.60 = 12,500 Big Macs. 2021 income: $20,000 × 271 ÷ 110 = $49,272.73, which buys $49,272.73 ÷ $5.65 = about 8,721 Big Macs. That is about 30% fewer, the same 43% real price rise seen from the buyer's side (12,500 ÷ 8,721 = 1.43).

Full credit: $3.94; $2.29; about 43%; 12,500 and about 8,721, with the formula $XB = $XA × PB ÷ PA written out.

8 points Up, down, or flat? For each, say whether the real price rose, fell, or stayed the same, and show one line of work.

(a) (2 points) A movie ticket was $12.00 when the CPI was 240 and is $13.20 when the CPI is 270.

(b) (2 points) A burrito was $4.00 when the CPI was 160 and is $9.00 when the CPI is 320.

(c) (2 points) A pocket calculator was $20 when the CPI was 100 and is $15 when the CPI is 300.

(d) (2 points) A gallon of gas was $1.50 when the CPI was 150 and is $3.00 when the CPI is 300.

Show answer

(a) Fell. $12.00 × 270 ÷ 240 = $13.50 in today's dollars, more than $13.20. Ticket factor 1.10 is below CPI factor 1.125. The real price fell about 2.2% (13.20 ÷ 13.50 = 0.978).

(b) Rose. $4.00 × 320 ÷ 160 = $8.00, less than $9.00. Burrito factor 2.25 beats CPI factor 2.00. Up 12.5%.

(c) Fell. $20 × 300 ÷ 100 = $60, and today's price is $15, one quarter of that. Down 75%.

(d) Stayed the same. $1.50 × 300 ÷ 150 = $3.00, exactly today's price. Both doubled.

Full credit: the right verdict for each, backed by either the two growth factors or the converted old price. Note (a): the nominal price rose, but the real price fell.

6 points Go Bears, pay more. Edwards' slides give these numbers: Cal in-state tuition and fees were $1,296 in 1985 to 1986, when the CPI was 108.94; the CPI "now" is 300.536; tuition and fees now are $14,796. A good desktop computer cost about $4,000 in 1996, when the CPI was 156.85, and costs about $1,000 now.

(a) (2 points) What is 1985 tuition in today's dollars? Has real tuition risen or fallen?

(b) (2 points) What is today's $1,000 desktop worth in 1996 dollars?

(c) (2 points) Using marginal cost reasoning, explain why one real price rose and the other fell.

Show answer

(a) $1,296 × 300.536 ÷ 108.94 = $3,575. Today's $14,796 is about 4.1 times that, so real tuition has risen a lot.

(b) $1,000 × 156.85 ÷ 300.536 = $521.90 in 1996 dollars, about 13% of the $4,000 a desktop cost then.

(c) Computers are made in factories where better technology and automation keep pushing down the marginal cost of making each one, so their real price falls. Education, like the Big Mac in the final's question 2(b), is labor-intensive: it needs instructors and buildings, and productivity in teaching does not rise the way factory productivity does, so its cost keeps pace with wages, which rise faster than prices in general.

Full credit: $3,575 and "risen"; $521.90; factory goods' marginal costs fall with automation, labor-intensive services' do not.

10 points Pizza and haircuts. An economy produces only pizzas and haircuts. Base year 2024.

YearPizzasPrice of pizzaHaircutsPrice of haircut
2024100$1050$20
2025110$1250$22

(a) (2 points) Compute nominal GDP in both years.

(b) (2 points) Compute real GDP in 2025 at 2024 prices, and the growth rate of real GDP.

(c) (2 points) Compute the GDP deflator in 2025.

(d) (2 points) Using the 2024 quantities as the basket, compute the CPI in 2025 and the inflation rate.

(e) (2 points) Why do the deflator and the CPI give slightly different inflation rates?

Show answer

(a) 2024: 100 × 10 + 50 × 20 = $2,000. 2025: 110 × 12 + 50 × 22 = $2,420.

(b) 110 × 10 + 50 × 20 = $2,100. Growth = (2,100 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 5%.

(c) 2,420 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = 115.24, so the deflator rose 15.24% from 100.

(d) Basket at 2025 prices: 100 × 12 + 50 × 22 = $2,300. CPI = 2,300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 115.0. Inflation = 15%.

(e) They use different weights. The CPI holds the 2024 basket fixed (100 pizzas). The deflator uses what was actually produced in 2025 (110 pizzas). Pizza prices rose 20% while haircut prices rose 10%, so the deflator, which puts more weight on pizza, shows slightly more inflation.

Full credit: 2,000 and 2,420; 2,100 and 5%; 115.24; 115.0 and 15%; different weights (fixed old basket versus current output).

8 points Does it count? For each transaction in 2026, say whether it adds to 2026 U.S. GDP, and if so, in which component (C, I, G, or NX) and by how much.

(a) (2 points) A chipmaker sells $500 of chips to a phone maker, which puts them into a phone sold to a U.S. consumer for $1,000.

(b) (2 points) You buy a used car from a dealer for $12,000. The dealer had bought it for $10,500.

(c) (2 points) A tech company spends $2 billion building a new AI data center in Texas, and you buy $3,000 of its stock.

(d) (2 points) A car factory in Kentucky builds a $40,000 car in December 2026 that is still on the lot in January 2027.

Show answer

(a) Only the final good counts: $1,000 in C. The $500 of chips is an intermediate good already inside the phone's price. (If the phone was imported, C rises $1,000 and imports rise $1,000, so GDP is unchanged.)

(b) The car was produced in an earlier year, so only the dealer's service is new: $1,500 in C (12,000 − 10,500).

(c) The data center is new capital: $2 billion in I. The stock purchase is a financial transaction that moves ownership of an existing asset: not counted.

(d) It was produced in the U.S. in 2026, so it counts in 2026 GDP as $40,000 of I (inventory investment). When it sells in 2027, C rises and inventories fall by the same amount, so 2027 GDP is not affected.

Full credit: final-good rule, newly-produced rule, investment means new capital not stocks, and inventories count in the year produced.

6 points Who is unemployed? A country has 250 million people age 16 and older. 152 million are employed and 8 million are unemployed.

(a) (2 points) Compute the labor force, the unemployment rate, and the participation rate.

(b) (2 points) In a long slump, 2 million of the unemployed give up looking. Recompute the unemployment rate and participation rate.

(c) (2 points) Is the economy better off in (b)? What does this show about the official unemployment rate?

Show answer

(a) Labor force = 152 + 8 = 160 million. Unemployment rate = 8 ÷ 160 = 5.0%. Participation = 160 ÷ 250 = 64%.

(b) Unemployed = 6, labor force = 158. Unemployment rate = 6 ÷ 158 = 3.8%. Participation = 158 ÷ 250 = 63.2%.

(c) No. Nobody found a job; employment is still 152 million. Discouraged workers leave the labor force and stop counting as unemployed, so the official rate can fall in bad times and understate how weak the job market is. Look at participation or the employment-to-population ratio (152 ÷ 250 = 60.8% in both cases) as well.

Full credit: 160, 5.0%, 64%; 3.8% and 63.2%; not better off, because discouraged workers are not counted.

6 points Who is bigger? Edwards' slides give China's 2024 nominal GDP as ¥134,908.40 billion and U.S. 2024 nominal GDP as $29,298.013 billion. The IMF's 2024 PPP estimates are $38,210 billion for China and $29,300 billion for the U.S.

(a) (2 points) Why can't you compare the two nominal numbers directly?

(b) (2 points) Suppose, hypothetically, the exchange rate were ¥7 per dollar. What is China's GDP in dollars at that rate, and how does it compare with the U.S.?

(c) (2 points) Why is China's GDP bigger than the U.S.'s at PPP but smaller at the market exchange rate in (b)?

Show answer

(a) They are in different units, yuan and dollars. In his words, currency units are great "except when they come to have different meanings, like across space or across time."

(b) 134,908.40 ÷ 7 = $19,272.63 billion, about two thirds of U.S. GDP.

(c) PPP values both countries' output at a common set of prices. Many goods and services, especially local services, cost less in China, so the same yuan buys more there than the exchange rate suggests. Valuing China's output at common prices raises it. This is the cross-country version of real versus nominal: PPP removes price differences across space the way a price index removes them across time.

Full credit: different currency units; $19,272.63 billion; PPP adjusts for lower prices in China, like real GDP adjusts for price changes.

4 points The shutdown and SNAP. Edwards' Class 17 slides noted that the fall 2025 government shutdown caused a lapse in SNAP ("food stamps") benefits.

(a) (2 points) Are SNAP benefits part of G in GDP? Explain.

(b) (2 points) If households that lost benefits cut their grocery spending, which component of GDP falls first?

Show answer

(a) No. SNAP is a transfer payment: the government hands purchasing power to households and buys no good or service in return. Only government purchases of goods and services are in G.

(b) Consumption, C. Benefits enter GDP only when households spend them on food; when that spending stops, C falls. (In the Keynesian cross units, that drop in C lowers planned spending and, through the multiplier, output.)

Full credit: transfer, not a purchase, so not in G; the effect shows up in C.

6 points Keeping up.

(a) (2 points) The CPI rises from 300 to 309. What is the inflation rate? A retiree's benefit of $1,500 a month is indexed to the CPI. What is her new benefit?

(b) (2 points) Ten years ago you earned $20 an hour and the CPI was 200. Now you earn $28 and the CPI is 250. Did your real wage rise or fall, and by how much?

(c) (2 points) The next year the CPI goes from 309 to 306. What is the inflation rate, and what is this called?

Show answer

(a) (309 − 300) ÷ 300 = 3%. Indexed benefit = $1,500 × 309 ÷ 300 = $1,545, so its real value is unchanged.

(b) Real wage then: 20 ÷ 2.00 = $10.00 in base-year dollars. Now: 28 ÷ 2.50 = $11.20. Real wage rose 12%, though the nominal wage rose 40%. (Equivalently, $20 × 250 ÷ 200 = $25 is what you would need now to keep up, and you earn $28, which is 12% more.)

(c) (306 − 309) ÷ 309 = −0.97%. Negative inflation is deflation.

Full credit: 3% and $1,545; real wage up 12%; −0.97%, deflation, dividing by the earlier year's index.

4 points Normal output. In a country, normal output per worker is $120,000 and the normal employment-to-population ratio is 0.5.

(a) (2 points) What is potential output per person? Write the formula you used.

(b) (2 points) Country A's income per person grows 2% a year and country B's grows 1% a year, starting from the same level. Roughly how many times richer is A after 70 years? Why?

Show answer

(a) Y* ÷ POP = (Y* ÷ N*) × (N* ÷ POP) = $120,000 × 0.5 = $60,000.

(b) About twice as rich. A 1-percentage-point gap compounds: relative income grows by about 1.01 each year, and 1.0170 ≈ 2.

Full credit: $60,000 from productivity times the employment-to-population ratio; about 2 times from 1.0170.

2 points Multiple choice. The consumer price index measures the cost of
(i) the current year's basket at base-year prices
(ii) the base-year basket at current prices, relative to its cost at base-year prices
(iii) all final goods and services produced in the country at current prices
(iv) the current year's basket at current prices, divided by real GDP

Show answer

(ii). The CPI fixes base-year quantities as weights and prices them in each year. (iii) is nominal GDP, and (i) is closer to real GDP's logic.

2 points Multiple choice. Which of these adds to this year's U.S. GDP?
(i) Your purchase of a used textbook from a classmate
(ii) The government's Social Security payments to retirees
(iii) A new house built this year in Berkeley
(iv) Your purchase of shares in an index fund

Show answer

(iii). A newly built house is residential investment. The used book was produced earlier, Social Security is a transfer, and shares are financial assets.

2 points Multiple choice. Nominal GDP grows 7% in a year, and the price level (GDP deflator) rises 3%. Real GDP grew by about
(i) 10%
(ii) 7%
(iii) 4%
(iv) 3%

Show answer

(iii). Real growth is approximately nominal growth minus inflation: 7% − 3% = 4%. (Exactly, 1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 3.9%.)

2 points Multiple choice. The unemployment rate equals
(i) unemployed people divided by the population age 16 and older
(ii) unemployed people divided by the labor force, where the labor force is employed plus unemployed
(iii) people not working divided by the labor force
(iv) people not in the labor force divided by the population

Show answer

(ii). Only people who are actively looking count as unemployed, and the denominator is the labor force. (iii) would wrongly count students and retirees.

One-minute review

  • GDP = market value of final goods and services newly produced in a country in a period. Final, new, domestic, in the period.
  • Out of GDP: intermediate goods, used goods (except the dealer's fee), stocks and bonds, transfer payments, home production.
  • Y = C + I + G + NX, with NX = EX − IM. I is new capital and inventories, not stocks. Transfers are not G. U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion, about $88,000 per person.
  • Three approaches give the same number: spending, value added, income. Value added sums to the final price.
  • Nominal uses current prices; real uses base-year prices (basically Q). Deflator = nominal ÷ real × 100.
  • CPI = cost of the base-year basket now ÷ its cost in the base year × 100. Quantities are the weights. Inflation π = percent change in the index, dividing by the earlier year.
  • Convert dollars: $XB = $XA × PB ÷ PA. Real price rose if the item's factor beats the CPI's factor.
  • Big Mac: 3.53 versus 2.46, real price up about 43%; $1.60 in 1986 is $3.94 in 2021 dollars. Cal tuition $1,296 is $3,575 today versus $14,796 actual. Desktop $4,000 is $7,664 today versus $1,000.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ (employed + unemployed). Discouraged workers are not in the labor force, so the rate can fall when people give up.
  • Y* ÷ POP = (Y* ÷ N*) × (N* ÷ POP). A 1-point growth gap doubles relative income in about 70 years.
