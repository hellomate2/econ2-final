Class 16 · Trade Policy

Trade in supply and demand: exports, imports, tariffs

This unit puts international trade on the ordinary supply and demand graph. A horizontal world price line, above or below the price the country would have without trade, tells you whether the good is exported or imported, and the horizontal gap between supply and demand at that price is the amount traded. Fall 2025 final question 4 (12 points) was built on exactly this graph: soybean exports at a world price, then China's tariffs wiping out that demand while the harvest was already fixed, so the price fell all the way down the domestic demand curve, then the labor market for soybean workers, then winners and losers.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

This unit puts trade on the ordinary supply and demand graph by adding one flat line, the world price. If the world price is above the price the country would have on its own, the country exports the extra it makes, and if the world price is below it, the country imports what it is short. The one thing to remember for the final: read quantity supplied and quantity demanded at the world price, and the horizontal gap between them is the amount exported or imported.

From the PPC to supply and demand

Class 15 showed trade with a production possibilities curve (PPC) and a consumption possibilities curve (CPC). Class 16 starts from that picture and moves it onto supply and demand. Edwards' running example has the U.S. making two goods, soybeans and washing machines, with a bowed-out PPC, which means the opportunity cost of making more of either good rises as you make more of it. He sets the world price of soybeans at $400 a ton and the world price of a washing machine at $300, so the terms of trade are $400 ÷ $300 = 1⅓ washing machines per ton of soybeans. In other words, selling one ton of soybeans abroad pays for 1⅓ washing machines. Another name for the terms of trade is the world relative price.

The U.S. produces where its PPC is tangent to a line with slope −1⅓ (point A), then trades along that line, the CPC, to consume at a different point (point B). It exports soybeans and imports washing machines. The supply and demand graphs below show the same trade, one market at a time.

Three facts link the two pictures (Class 16, slide 18):

  • Because opportunity cost rises, the country does not specialize completely. It still makes some of the imported good.
  • The point where the world price line crosses domestic supply is the production point, the tangency point A on the PPC.
  • The point where the world price line crosses domestic demand is the consumption point, point B on the CPC.

The setup: domestic curves plus a flat world price

Draw the ordinary U.S. market: demand DUS and supply SUS. Where they cross is the price and quantity with no trade, which economists call autarky. Edwards labels these P1US and Q1US, "U.S. Price without Trade."

Now open the market to the world. The U.S. is assumed to be a price taker in the world market: it is small relative to the world, so it can buy or sell as much as it wants at the world price Pw without moving it. That is why the world price is drawn as a horizontal line. Edwards' slide says world demand and world supply at that price "are perfectly elastic." Perfectly elastic means the curve is flat: any amount can be bought or sold at that one price. Once trade is open, U.S. buyers will not pay more than Pw (they can buy from abroad) and U.S. sellers will not accept less than Pw (they can sell abroad), so the domestic price becomes Pw.

In plain words: once the country can trade, nobody at home pays more than the rest of the world charges, and nobody at home sells for less than the rest of the world pays. So the home price becomes the world price.

Two more interpretation notes from the slides. The upward slope of U.S. supply reflects rising opportunity cost, the same idea as the bowed-out PPC. And the price on the vertical axis is always a relative price, the price of soybeans compared with other goods.

At the world price, read two quantities: quantity demanded QD (where Pw meets demand) and quantity supplied QS (where Pw meets supply). If Pw is above the no-trade price, QS > QD and the country exports QS − QD. If Pw is below the no-trade price, QD > QS and the country imports QD − QS. Exports and imports are always the horizontal gap between the curves at the world price.

In plain words: find the world price on the price axis and slide straight across to the demand curve and to the supply curve. The distance between those two points is how much the country trades. If the country makes more than it wants, the extra is exported. If it wants more than it makes, the shortfall is imported.

Exports: world price above the no-trade price

Running example (hypothetical numbers): U.S. demand for soybeans is P = 12 − Q and U.S. supply is P = Q. Without trade, 12 − Q = Q, so Q = 6 and P = $6.

Now suppose the world price is $8. U.S. buyers want 12 − 8 = 4 units. U.S. farmers want to supply 8 units, because supply P = Q means they sell 8 when the price is $8. The 4 units that U.S. buyers do not take are sold abroad: exports = 8 − 4 = 4, which is half of the quantity supplied, the same shape the Fall 2025 final asked for.

Who wins and who loses when an export market opens:

  • Domestic consumers lose. They now pay $8 instead of $6 and buy 4 instead of 6. Consumer surplus (CS), the area under demand and above the price, falls from ½ × 6 × 6 = 18 to ½ × 4 × 4 = 8, a loss of 10.
  • Domestic producers win. They get $8 instead of $6 and sell 8 instead of 6. Producer surplus (PS), the area above supply and below the price, rises from ½ × 6 × 6 = 18 to ½ × 8 × 8 = 32, a gain of 14.
  • The country as a whole gains. Total surplus rises from 36 to 40. The gain of 4 is the triangle between the world price line and the two curves: ½ × (8 − 4) × (8 − 6) = 4. Producers gain more than consumers lose, because the extra units are sold to foreigners who pay $8.

Imports: world price below the no-trade price

Same curves, but now suppose the good is washing machines and the world price is $4. U.S. buyers want 12 − 4 = 8. U.S. producers supply only 4. The missing 4 are bought from abroad: imports = 8 − 4 = 4.

  • Domestic consumers win. CS rises from 18 to ½ × 8 × 8 = 32, a gain of 14.
  • Domestic producers lose. PS falls from 18 to ½ × 4 × 4 = 8, a loss of 10.
  • The country gains 4 overall, again the triangle ½ × (8 − 4) × (6 − 4) = 4.

Edwards showed real data for this case: a FRED chart of the price of appliances relative to other goods falling steadily from the early 1990s to the late 2010s (Class 16, slide 21). Cheaper imported appliances are the import graph in action.

Opening to trade always produces a net gain equal to the triangle between the world price line and the two domestic curves. With exports, producers win and consumers lose. With imports, consumers win and producers lose. In both cases the winners gain more than the losers lose, which is why "gains from trade" are real even though trade creates losers.

In plain words: opening to trade always helps one side of the market and hurts the other. The side that gains always gains more than the other side loses, so the country as a whole comes out ahead.

Trade and the labor market

Labor demand is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MR is the marginal revenue from one more unit of output (the price, for a competitive firm) and MPL is the marginal product of labor, the extra output from one more worker. Trade changes the price firms get, so it shifts labor demand.

In plain words: a firm values a worker by the extra goods that worker makes times the price those goods sell for. When trade raises the price of the good, each worker is worth more, so firms hire more people and pay more. When trade lowers the price, firms hire fewer people and pay less.

  • Export industry (soybeans when trade opens): the world price is above the no-trade price, so MR rises and the MRPL curve shifts up. Wages and employment in that industry rise (slide 26).
  • Import industry (washing machines): the world price is below the no-trade price, so MR falls and MRPL shifts down. Wages and employment fall (slide 23).

His summary (slide 27): trade "tends to rearrange jobs, rather than raise or lower employment overall." Jobs grow in export industries and shrink in import industries, but the move can be painful for workers who lose jobs and lack the skills for the growing industries.

The same logic runs in reverse in a trade war. When China's tariffs cut the price of U.S. soybeans, MR fell, so MRPL for soybean workers shifted down. That was part (c) of the Fall 2025 final question.

The China shock

Edwards spent several slides on why the labor-market costs of trade turned out bigger than economists expected. Around 2000, the consensus was that trade did not jar labor markets much: displaced workers could move to new jobs, and the effects would spread thinly across many workers. Then China joined the World Trade Organization (WTO) in late 2001. The U.S. gained an export market for soybeans, but furniture and other labor-intensive manufacturing took a huge hit, and the workers in those industries often lacked college degrees.

The new feature was geography. Earlier job losses were spread out, but the China shock hit places where those industries were clustered, and those local labor markets did not recover (Autor, Dorn, and Hanson, "On the Persistence of the China Shock," 2021). His slide 34 compares two North Carolina places with similar exposure to the shock. North Hickory, a furniture factory town with a high manufacturing share and few college graduates, had an estimated impact on log personal income per capita (2000 to 2019) of −5.32. Raleigh-Cary, larger, more diversified, and with more college graduates, had −3.84 and ended up in better shape.

Trade policy terms

  • Free trade: a country puts up no barriers to international trade.
  • Protection: a country puts limits on trade.
  • Trade policy: a country's policies toward trade.

Trade policy is not the only thing that determines how much trade happens. Shipping costs, logistics, and communication matter too. Edwards' example is the container ship, introduced in the 1950s, which made shipping far cheaper by cutting out the dockworkers (stevedores) who used to load cargo by hand.

The three methods of protection on his slide 38:

  • Tariff: a tax on imports.
  • Quota: a limit on the quantity of imports.
  • Subsidies for domestic production.

In plain words: a tariff is a tax on goods coming into the country, a quota is a cap on how many can come in, and a subsidy is money the government pays home producers. All three help home producers, and someone else pays for that help.

His chart of average U.S. tariff rates on dutiable imports (slide 39) shows the long history. Dutiable imports are the imports that are subject to a tariff. Tariffs peaked around the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, then fell steadily through the Trade Agreements Act (1934), the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, 1947), and later trade rounds to low levels by 2000.

A tariff on the graph

A tariff of t dollars per unit raises the price of every imported unit from Pw to Pw + t. Domestic sellers can now charge Pw + t too, since buyers' only alternative costs that much. So the domestic price rises by the full tariff (for a price-taking country). Then:

  • Quantity demanded falls from QD1 to QD2 (buyers slide up demand).
  • Domestic quantity supplied rises from QS1 to QS2 (producers slide up supply).
  • Imports shrink from QD1 − QS1 to QD2 − QS2.
  • The government collects tariff revenue = t × imports after the tariff.

Worked numbers (hypothetical): demand P = 20 − Q, supply P = Q, world price $4, tariff $2. Before the tariff, QS1 = 4 and QD1 = 16, so imports are 12. After, the price is $6, QS2 = 6 and QD2 = 14, so imports are 8.

Deadweight loss (DWL) is surplus that existed before the tariff and now goes to nobody: not to consumers, not to producers, and not to the government. What the two deadweight loss triangles mean:

  • Area d is a production loss. Units from QS1 to QS2 are now made at home at a marginal cost above $4, even though the world would sell them for $4. Society wastes resources making them.
  • Area f is a consumption loss. Buyers from QD2 to QD1 valued these units above $4 (the true cost to the country) but no longer buy them because the price is $6.

Tariff winners and losers: domestic consumers lose c + d + e + f; domestic producers gain c; the government gains e; deadweight loss is d + f. Shortcut with a tariff t: revenue = t × (QD2 − QS2) and DWL = ½ × t × (QS2 − QS1) + ½ × t × (QD1 − QD2).

In plain words: a tariff raises the price at home. Buyers lose. Part of what they lose goes to home producers, part goes to the government as tax money, and two small triangles go to nobody. Those two triangles are the waste.

A tariff big enough to lift Pw + t to the no-trade price or higher is prohibitive: imports fall to zero, revenue is zero, and the market goes back to autarky. In the example, the no-trade price is $10, because 20 − Q = Q gives Q = 10 and P = 10. A tariff of $6 lifts the price to 4 + 6 = $10, so any tariff of $6 or more is prohibitive. The whole gains-from-trade triangle is then lost: its base is the free-trade imports of 12 and its height is 10 − 4 = 6, so ½ × 12 × 6 = 36.

Edwards' real example: a New York Times chart of washing machine prices during the first Trump administration (slide 43). Prices fell every year from 2014 to 2017, then rose in 2018, after the tariff fight began in early 2018 (slide 47).

A quota

A quota caps imports at a fixed number of units. The domestic price rises until the gap between demand and domestic supply equals the quota. In the example, a quota of 8 units works like the $2 tariff: at a price of $6, demand is 14 and domestic supply is 6, so imports are exactly 8. Algebra: imports at price P are quantity demanded minus domestic quantity supplied, (20 − P) − P = 20 − 2P. Set 20 − 2P = 8, so 2P = 12 and P = 6.

In plain words: a quota pushes the home price up the same way a tariff does. The difference is that the government collects nothing unless it sells the import licenses. The extra money goes to whoever is allowed to import.

The difference between a tariff and a quota with the same import quantity is who gets rectangle e. With a tariff, the government collects it as revenue. With a quota, the textbook calls it a quota rent: whoever holds the right to import buys at $4 and sells at $6, so if foreign exporters capture it, the country loses e as well as d + f. (The slides define a quota; this tariff and quota comparison is standard textbook content.)

Employment effects and arguments for protection

Edwards' slide 45 on the employment effects of a tariff:

  • It likely raises employment in the protected industry, because the higher price raises MR and so raises MRPL there.
  • It probably does not raise total employment.
  • Trade retaliation might reduce foreign demand for the country's goods and services.
  • Currencies might move: the tariffing country's currency might appreciate and the tariffed country's might depreciate (covered later, in the exchange rate unit).
  • There may be unintended effects on workers in other industries, for example industries that buy the tariffed good as an input.

Possible arguments for protection (slide 46): national security; concern about supply chain disruptions; diversification; infant industry or dynamic comparative advantage (protect a young industry until it can compete); jobs for particular workers; positive domestic externalities from domestic production. His last bullet, "Capriciousness? Punishment? Covfefe?", is a joke that makes a point: some tariffs are not justified by any of these.

Retaliation and trade wars

A tariff in one country is a lower price for exporters in another. When China put tariffs on U.S. soybeans, Chinese buyers had to pay the tariff on top of the price U.S. farmers charged. So Chinese buyers would keep buying only if U.S. farmers cut their own price by roughly the amount of the tariff, and with a steep tariff they mostly stopped buying. To U.S. farmers it was as if the world price they received collapsed. Edwards' chart from the Peterson Institute for International Economics (PIIE, slide 47) shows the U.S.-China "fisticuffs" beginning in early 2018 with Section 201 tariffs on manufactured goods, Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum, and Section 301 retaliatory tariffs, then a huge jump in both countries' tariffs in 2025.

The 2025 legal and political story comes from his slides (Class 16, slide 48, and Class 17, slides 11 and 12). Most of the 2025 U.S. tariffs were invoked under the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). On August 29, 2025 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit ruled 7 to 4 that most of them were illegal under IEEPA, because Congress had not delegated that power to the President. The consolidated case (Learning Resources v. Trump) was set for Supreme Court argument on November 5, 2025. On October 28, 2025 the Senate voted 52 to 48 to end the emergency used to justify 50% tariffs on Brazilian goods, even though the U.S. had trade surpluses with Brazil in 2024 ($6.8 billion in goods and $23.1 billion in services). On October 30, 2025, Presidents Trump and Xi met, with reported agreement touching tariffs and soybeans.

The trade war on the export graph: Fall 2025 final question 4(b)

This is the twist he tested. Go back to the export graph: world price $8, U.S. farmers supply 8, U.S. buyers take 4, and 4 are exported (all to China, by assumption). Then China's tariffs stop all purchases, and no other country will buy at any price. Three facts from the question make the answer unusual:

  1. Farmers planted in the spring, before the escalation, and cannot change that quantity. In the short run, supply is a vertical line at QS = 8. Supply is perfectly inelastic.
  2. Soybeans cannot be stored or destroyed, so all 8 units must be sold now.
  3. Only U.S. buyers are left, so the 8 units must be sold along the U.S. demand curve.

U.S. buyers will take 8 units only if the price falls to the height of demand at Q = 8: P = 12 − 8 = $4. Edwards' key calls this Ptw (the trade-war price), "read off the demand curve at quantity QSUS, a really big reduction."

Trade-war rule: if the quantity is fixed and the export market disappears, the price is the height of domestic demand at the fixed quantity, not the no-trade price. It ends up below the no-trade price whenever the fixed harvest is larger than the no-trade quantity, and with exports it always is, because exporters were selling more than the autarky quantity.

In plain words: if farmers have already grown the crop and must sell all of it at home, the price has to drop until home buyers are willing to take every bushel. That price is usually far below the old one, and even below the price before trade.

Who gains and loses in the short run, in the example:

  • Farmers' revenue falls from $8 × 8 = 64 to $4 × 8 = 32. Their costs (the area under supply up to 8, ½ × 8 × 8 = 32) were already spent, so producer surplus falls from 32 to 0.
  • U.S. consumers gain. They now buy 8 at $4 instead of 4 at $8: CS rises from 8 to ½ × 8 × 8 = 32.
  • The country loses 8 on net. The 4 units that China used to buy for $8 each ($32) now go to U.S. buyers who value them at only $4 to $8 (the area under demand from 4 to 8, ½ × (8 + 4) × 4 = 24). 32 − 24 = 8.

Next spring, farmers can choose a new quantity. If exports are still gone, they plant along their supply curve until it meets U.S. demand: back to the no-trade outcome, 6 units at $6.

The story's ending on the final (part d): in late October 2025 the U.S. and China reached a truce in which China agreed to buy more U.S. soybeans; in early December the U.S. announced a one-time $12 billion payment to soybean farmers; and China reported a record trade surplus. The key's reading: the U.S. lost this round and China won. The U.S. government felt it had to compensate the losers, U.S. farmers, whose perishable crop had nowhere else to go. China's exports are mostly durable manufactured goods, which do not spoil and can wait for new buyers, and the record surplus suggests they found them.

Worked examples

Example: reading exports and surplus off the graph

U.S. demand for soybeans is P = 12 − Q and U.S. supply is P = Q (Q in billions of bushels, P in dollars per bushel). The world price is $8. Find the no-trade outcome, exports, and the gains from trade.

  1. No trade: 12 − Q = Q, so Q1 = 6 and P1 = $6.
  2. Quantity demanded at $8: 12 − Q = 8, so QD = 4.
  3. Quantity supplied at $8: Q = 8, so QS = 8.
  4. Exports = QS − QD = 8 − 4 = 4. The world price is above the no-trade price, which is why the country exports.
  5. CS: ½ × 4 × (12 − 8) = 8, down from 18. PS: ½ × 8 × 8 = 32, up from 18.
  6. Gain from trade: 32 + 8 − 36 = 4, which matches the triangle ½ × 4 × 2 = 4.

Answer: Without trade, 6 units at $6. At $8, the U.S. produces 8, consumes 4, and exports 4. Consumers lose 10, producers gain 14, and the country gains 4.

Example: Fall 2025 final question 4(a) and (b) with numbers

Same market. Draw about half of the quantity supplied as exports, then show what happens when China's tariffs stop all purchases, the harvest is fixed, and soybeans cannot be stored or destroyed.

  1. Draw Pw = $8 above P1 = $6 as a horizontal line. Mark QD = 4 on demand and QS = 8 on supply. Bracket the gap from 4 to 8 and label it "exports." Exports are 4 of the 8 supplied, about half.
  2. Write one sentence: the world price is above the price that equates domestic supply and demand, so domestic quantity supplied exceeds domestic quantity demanded, and the horizontal gap is exports.
  3. Trade war: draw a vertical line at QS = 8 for the fixed harvest. Go down that line to the demand curve. Demand at Q = 8 is 12 − 8 = $4. Label that price Ptw.
  4. Explain: farmers cannot plant less, store, or destroy, so all 8 units must be sold to domestic buyers, and they will only buy 8 at $4.

Answer: Exports of 4 at a world price of $8. After the trade war, the price falls by half, from $8 to $4, which is even below the $6 no-trade price.

Example: the labor market for soybean workers (final question 4(c))

A soybean worker's marginal product is MPL = 3 − L/4 bushels per hour (L in thousands of workers). Labor supply is w = L. The soybean price falls from $8 to $4.

  1. Before: MRPL = P × MPL = 8 × (3 − L/4) = 24 − 2L. Set equal to supply: 24 − 2L = L, so L = 8 and w = $8. Check: MPL at L = 8 is 1 bushel, worth $8.
  2. After: MRPL = 4 × (3 − L/4) = 12 − L. Set equal to supply: 12 − L = L, so L = 6 and w = $6. Check: MPL at L = 6 is 1.5 bushels, worth $6.
  3. Graph: labor demand shifts down (inward) because MR, the soybean price, fell. Label D1 and D2, W1 and W2, L1 and L2.

Answer: The wage falls from $8 to $6 an hour and employment from 8,000 to 6,000 workers, because the lower soybean price lowers marginal revenue, which lowers MRPL = MR × MPL at every level of employment.

Example: a $2 tariff on imported washing machines

Demand P = 20 − Q, supply P = Q, world price $4, tariff $2. Find imports, tariff revenue, and deadweight loss, and say who wins and who loses.

  1. Free trade: QS1 = 4, QD1 = 20 − 4 = 16, imports 12.
  2. With the tariff the domestic price is 4 + 2 = $6. QS2 = 6, QD2 = 14, imports 8.
  3. Revenue e = $2 × 8 = 16.
  4. Deadweight loss d + f = ½ × 2 × (6 − 4) + ½ × 2 × (16 − 14) = 2 + 2 = 4.
  5. Consumers lose c + d + e + f = 128 − 98 = 30. Producers gain c = 10. Government gains e = 16. Net: −30 + 10 + 16 = −4.

Answer: Imports fall from 12 to 8. Revenue is 16, deadweight loss is 4. Domestic producers and the government gain, consumers lose more than both gain together.

Example: the trade war when other buyers exist

Same soybean market and harvest of 8, but now other countries will buy any amount of U.S. soybeans at $7 (and nothing above that). What happens?

  1. U.S. buyers would pay $4 for all 8 units, but foreign buyers offer $7, so no farmer sells at home for less than $7. The price floor is the new world price, $7.
  2. At $7, U.S. buyers take 12 − 7 = 5.
  3. The rest, 8 − 5 = 3, is exported to the other buyers.
  4. Farmers' revenue: 7 × 8 = 56, instead of 64 with China or 32 with nobody.

Answer: The price falls only from $8 to $7, U.S. consumption rises from 4 to 5, and exports fall from 4 to 3. Other buyers act as a cushion; the fall all the way down demand happens only when there are no buyers abroad at any price.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Drawing an exported good with the world price below the domestic equilibrium.

Correct: Exports happen only when Pw is above the no-trade price, so domestic supply exceeds domestic demand. A world price below the no-trade price means imports. The key: "the world price must be higher than the price that equates domestic supply and demand."

Mistake: Measuring exports or imports vertically, or as the distance from the old equilibrium quantity.

Correct: Trade is the horizontal gap between QS and QD, both read at the world price. In the soybean example, 8 − 4 = 4, not 8 − 6.

Mistake: Saying the trade-war price falls back to the no-trade price ($6).

Correct: The harvest is stuck at QS = 8, which is more than the no-trade quantity of 6. Buyers only take 8 units at the demand curve's height at 8, which is $4. The price falls below the autarky price.

Mistake: Shifting the domestic supply or demand curve to show a world price or a tariff.

Correct: The world price is a new horizontal line, and a tariff is a second horizontal line t above it. Domestic curves do not move; buyers and sellers move along them. (The trade war adds a vertical short-run supply line because the harvest is fixed, which is a separate assumption.)

Mistake: Counting tariff revenue (area e) as deadweight loss.

Correct: Revenue goes to the government, so it is a transfer from consumers. Deadweight loss is only the two triangles d and f. (Under a quota, e goes to import-rights holders instead.)

Mistake: Computing tariff revenue as tariff × imports before the tariff.

Correct: Revenue = t × imports after the tariff: $2 × 8 = 16, not $2 × 12 = 24.

Mistake: Saying trade, or a tariff, changes total employment.

Correct: Trade rearranges jobs from import industries to export industries. A tariff likely raises employment in the protected industry but probably not in total, and retaliation can cut jobs in export industries.

Mistake: Saying the labor demand curve shifts because workers became less productive.

Correct: In the trade war, MPL did not change. The output price, and so MR, fell. MRPL = MR × MPL falls because MR fell.

Mistake: Computing deadweight loss as consumers' loss minus producers' gain and forgetting the revenue.

Correct: DWL = consumers' loss − producers' gain − tariff revenue. In the washing machine example, 30 − 10 − 16 = 4, not 30 − 10 = 20.

Mistake: Drawing the world price line through the no-trade equilibrium.

Correct: If the world price equaled the no-trade price, there would be no trade. Draw Pw clearly above the no-trade price for an export good or clearly below it for an import good, so the horizontal gap shows.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Find the no-trade point first by setting the demand and supply equations equal. Every trade question compares against that price.
  • Write "Pw above P1: exports" or "Pw below P1: imports" in the margin before you draw anything.
  • Draw a dotted horizontal line at every price in the question (Pw, Pw + t, a trade-war price) and read where it hits demand and where it hits supply. Quantities always come from those crossings.
  • If the question says the quantity is fixed, draw a vertical supply line at that quantity and read the price straight up on the demand curve.
  • For any labor part, write MRPL = MR × MPL and ask which piece changed. In trade questions it is MR, the price of the good, so labor demand shifts and labor supply stays put.
  • For winners and losers, list consumers, producers, the government, and the country as a whole, and give each a plus or minus sign before you compute any area.

12 points Shell shock. Suppose the U.S. grows pistachios and sells the surplus to one large foreign buyer. U.S. demand is P = 10 − Q and U.S. supply is P = 2 + Q, with Q in billions of pounds and P in dollars per pound. The world price is $8.

(a) (4 points) Find the no-trade price and quantity. Then show the world price on a supply and demand graph, and find and label U.S. consumption, U.S. production, and exports. Briefly explain what you drew.

(b) (2 points) The foreign buyer's government imposes a steep tariff that stops all its purchases, and no one else will buy U.S. pistachios at any price. The crop is already on the trees and cannot be stored or destroyed. What is the new price? Show it on your graph.

(c) (4 points) Illustrate the labor market for pistachio workers before and after part (b). Show demand and supply curves and the equilibrium wages and employment. Briefly explain.

(d) (2 points) Who in the U.S. gains and who loses in part (b), compared with part (a)? Explain using surplus.

Show answer

(a) No trade: 10 − Q = 2 + Q, so Q = 4 and P = $6. At the world price of $8, U.S. buyers want 10 − 8 = 2, and U.S. growers supply Q = 8 − 2 = 6. Exports = 6 − 2 = 4. The world price is above the price that equates domestic supply and demand, so domestic production exceeds domestic consumption, and the horizontal gap at Pw is exports.

Full credit: no-trade point (4, $6); Pw drawn above it; QD = 2 and QS = 6 marked; the horizontal gap of 4 labeled exports; one sentence of explanation.

(b) Quantity is fixed at 6 and must all be sold at home, so the price is the height of U.S. demand at Q = 6: P = 10 − 6 = $4. It falls by half, and below the $6 no-trade price, because the crop of 6 is larger than the 4 that U.S. buyers would choose at $6.

Full credit: $4, located on the demand curve straight above QS = 6, with the reason (fixed quantity, no storage, no foreign buyers).

(c) Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL. The pistachio price is the marginal revenue of each pound, and it fell from $8 to $4, so MRPL falls at every level of employment. The labor demand curve shifts down or inward from D1 to D2, along an unchanged labor supply curve. The equilibrium wage falls from W1 to W2 and employment from L1 to L2. (The graph looks like the soybean labor graph in this unit.)

Full credit: labor supply and two labor demand curves, D2 below D1, with W1, W2, L1, L2 labeled and both falling; the reason is the lower output price (MR), not lower productivity.

(d) Growers lose: revenue falls from 8 × 6 = $48 billion to 4 × 6 = $24 billion. U.S. consumers gain: they buy 6 instead of 2 at $4 instead of $8, so consumer surplus rises from ½ × 2 × 2 = 2 to ½ × 6 × 6 = 18. Growers lose more (24) than consumers gain (16), so the country loses 8 overall: the 4 pounds foreigners used to buy at $8 ($32 billion) are now worth only ½ × (8 + 4) × 4 = $24 billion to U.S. buyers.

Full credit: growers lose, consumers gain, and the net effect on the country is a loss.

6 points Use the soybean market from this unit: demand P = 12 − Q, supply P = Q, world price $8.

(a) (2 points) Compute consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus with no trade and at the world price.

(b) (2 points) Who gains and who loses from opening to exports? By how much?

(c) (2 points) Show that the net gain equals the area of a triangle on the graph, and name its corners.

Show answer

(a) No trade (6 units at $6): CS = ½ × 6 × (12 − 6) = 18, PS = ½ × 6 × 6 = 18, total 36. At $8: CS = ½ × 4 × (12 − 8) = 8, PS = ½ × 8 × 8 = 32, total 40.

(b) Consumers lose 18 − 8 = 10 because they pay more and buy less. Producers gain 32 − 18 = 14 because they get a higher price and sell more. The country gains 40 − 36 = 4.

(c) The triangle has corners at (4, $8) on demand, (8, $8) on supply, and the no-trade point (6, $6). Area = ½ × (8 − 4) × (8 − 6) = 4.

Full credit: all six surplus numbers; consumers −10, producers +14, net +4; the triangle between Pw and the two curves.

2 points In the soybean trade war (demand P = 12 − Q, harvest fixed at 8, exports to China gone), suppose instead that farmers can store soybeans for free and expect China to start buying again next year at $8. Would the price still fall to $4? Explain.

Show answer

No. The $4 price comes from being forced to sell all 8 units now. If storage is free and farmers expect to sell at $8 next year, no farmer would sell today for less than about what he expects to get later (ignoring interest). Farmers would hold back the 4 units that used to be exported and sell only what U.S. buyers want at a price near $8, so the price would fall little or not at all. The question's "cannot be stored or destroyed" assumption is what makes supply perfectly inelastic and the price collapse so large.

Full credit: no (or much less); storage lets farmers keep unsold soybeans off the market, so they do not have to accept the price on demand at Q = 8.

4 points Same soybean market. China's tariffs stay in place the following year, and no other country buys U.S. soybeans.

(a) (2 points) Next spring farmers can choose how much to plant. What price and quantity result? Explain.

(b) (2 points) Compare the price with part (b) of the final ($4) and with the $8 world price. Why is it in between?

Show answer

(a) With quantity free to adjust, farmers produce along their supply curve and sell only to U.S. buyers, so the market returns to the domestic equilibrium: 12 − Q = Q, 6 units at $6.

(b) $6 is above $4 because farmers plant less (6 instead of 8), so they no longer have to push extra units onto U.S. buyers. It is below $8 because the foreign buyers who were willing to pay $8 are gone. In the short run supply was vertical; in the longer run it is the ordinary upward-sloping supply curve.

Full credit: 6 units at $6 from domestic S = D, and the reason: quantity can now adjust, so supply is no longer fixed.

6 points Retaliation with time to adjust. Suppose that instead of halting purchases, a foreign tariff lowers the world price U.S. soybean farmers receive from $8 to $7, and farmers have time to change how much they plant. Use demand P = 12 − Q and supply P = Q.

(a) (2 points) Find U.S. production, consumption, and exports at $7.

(b) (2 points) Compute the change in producer surplus and consumer surplus compared with $8.

(c) (2 points) Does the U.S. as a whole gain or lose from the retaliation? By how much?

Show answer

(a) Production QS = 7, consumption QD = 12 − 7 = 5, exports = 7 − 5 = 2.

(b) PS at $7 = ½ × 7 × 7 = 24.5, down from 32: farmers lose 7.5. CS at $7 = ½ × 5 × 5 = 12.5, up from 8: consumers gain 4.5.

(c) Total surplus falls from 40 to 37, a loss of 3. The gain from trade shrinks from 4 to ½ × (7 − 5) × (7 − 6) = 1. Retaliation hurts the export industry more than it helps domestic consumers.

Full credit: 7, 5, and 2; PS −7.5 and CS +4.5; net loss of 3.

2 points In the soybean trade war, the U.S. demand curve is P = 12 − Q and the harvest is fixed at 8, but other countries are willing to buy U.S. soybeans at $5. What is the U.S. price, and how many soybeans are exported?

Show answer

Without other buyers the price would fall to $4. Since other countries pay $5, no farmer sells at home for less than $5, so the price is $5. U.S. buyers take 12 − 5 = 7, and the remaining 8 − 7 = 1 is exported. (If the other buyers offered only $3, they would not matter: U.S. buyers would pay $4 for all 8, and nothing would be exported.)

Full credit: $5 and exports of 1, with the reason that the outside offer sets a floor above the $4 domestic-demand price.

10 points Spin cycle. The U.S. imports washing machines. U.S. demand is P = 20 − Q and U.S. supply is P = Q (Q in millions, P in hundreds of dollars). The world price is 4. The government imposes a tariff of 2 per machine.

(a) (2 points) How many machines are imported before and after the tariff?

(b) (2 points) How much tariff revenue does the government collect?

(c) (2 points) Draw the graph and shade the deadweight loss. How big is it?

(d) (2 points) How much do consumers lose, and how much do domestic producers gain?

(e) (2 points) Edwards says a tariff "likely raises employment in the protected industry. But it probably does not raise total employment." Explain both halves.

Show answer

(a) Before: QS = 4, QD = 16, imports 12 million. After: price 6, QS = 6, QD = 14, imports 8 million.

(b) Revenue = 2 × 8 = 16 (hundreds of dollars times millions of machines, so $1.6 billion).

(c) Two triangles between the price lines 4 and 6: one from Q = 4 to 6 next to supply, one from Q = 14 to 16 next to demand. Each is ½ × 2 × 2 = 2, so DWL = 4 ($400 million). The graph is the lettered tariff graph above (areas d and f).

(d) Consumers lose c + d + e + f = 128 − 98 = 30. Producers gain c = 10. (Government gains 16, and 30 − 10 − 16 = 4 is the deadweight loss.)

4 points Same washing machine market (demand P = 20 − Q, supply P = Q, world price 4).

(a) (2 points) What import quota would raise the domestic price to the same 6 as the tariff? Show the algebra.

(b) (2 points) Is the U.S. better off, worse off, or the same under this quota as under the tariff? Explain.

Show answer

(a) At a price of 6, imports are demand minus domestic supply: (20 − 6) − 6 = 8. A quota of 8 million machines gives the same price, the same domestic production (6), and the same consumption (14).

(b) Worse off, or at best the same. Consumers lose the same 30 and producers gain the same 10, and d + f = 4 is lost either way. The difference is the rectangle of 16. Under the tariff the government collects it. Under the quota it goes to whoever holds the right to import, buying at 4 and selling at 6. If foreign exporters capture it, the U.S. loses 4 + 16 = 20 instead of 4. If U.S. importers get the licenses, the U.S. total is the same as with the tariff.

Full credit: quota of 8; the same price and quantities; the rectangle becomes quota rent instead of revenue, so the U.S. is worse off if foreigners collect it.

2 points In the washing machine market above, what is the smallest tariff that eliminates all imports? What happens to tariff revenue?

Show answer

Imports end when the domestic price reaches the no-trade price. No trade: 20 − Q = Q gives P = 10. The tariff must lift 4 to 10, so a tariff of 6 or more is prohibitive. Revenue falls to zero because nothing is imported, and the whole gain from trade, ½ × 12 × 6 = 36, is lost.

Full credit: a tariff of 6, found from the no-trade price of 10; revenue zero.

4 points A tale of two towns. North Hickory and Raleigh-Cary, North Carolina, had similar exposure to import competition from China after 2001.

(a) (2 points) Using a labor market graph for an import-competing industry, explain why the wage and employment in that industry fell.

(b) (2 points) Edwards' slide shows a larger estimated income loss for North Hickory (−5.32) than for Raleigh-Cary (−3.84). Explain why, using his slides.

Show answer

(a) Cheaper imports lower the price, and so the marginal revenue, of goods like furniture. Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, so it shifts down. Along an unchanged labor supply curve, the wage falls from W1 to W2 and employment from L1 to L2.

(b) North Hickory was a furniture factory town with a much higher share of manufacturing jobs and a low share of college graduates. Raleigh-Cary was larger, more diversified, and had more college graduates. Displaced workers in Raleigh-Cary had other industries nearby and skills that moved across industries; in North Hickory the losses were concentrated, and workers had few local alternatives, so the damage lasted.

Full credit: MRPL shifts down because the output price falls; the concentrated, less-educated, less-diversified town had fewer places for displaced workers to go.

2 points Name two arguments for protection from Edwards' slides and say, for one of them, what a tariff would actually achieve.

Show answer

His list: national security, supply chain disruptions, diversification, infant industry or dynamic comparative advantage, jobs for particular workers, and positive domestic externalities from domestic production. Example: for national security, a tariff on steel raises the domestic price, so domestic producers expand output (from QS1 to QS2) and the country keeps the ability to make steel in an emergency. The cost is the deadweight loss and the higher price paid by every steel buyer.

Full credit: two items from his list and a correct statement of what the tariff does for one of them (raises domestic production or employment in that industry) with its cost.

12 points Bike tariff. U.S. demand for bicycles is QD = 120 − 2P, which is P = 60 − ½Q in Edwards' form. Domestic supply is QS = 3P − 30, which is P = 10 + ⅓Q. Q is in thousands of bikes and P in hundreds of dollars. The world price is Pw = 20. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2023 midterm 2, Q5, and Fall 2024 final, Q5(a), made numeric

(a) (2 points) Find the no-trade price and quantity.

(b) (2 points) At the world price, find U.S. consumption, U.S. production, and imports.

(c) (2 points) A tariff of 5 per bike is imposed. Find the new consumption, production, and imports.

(d) (2 points) Find the tariff revenue.

(e) (2 points) How much do consumers lose and domestic producers gain? Show your work.

(f) (2 points) Draw the graph, label areas c, d, e, f, and compute the deadweight loss.

Show answer

(a) Set QD = QS: 120 − 2P = 3P − 30, so 5P = 150, P = 30 and Q = 60 thousand bikes.

(b) At P = 20: QD = 120 − 40 = 80, QS = 60 − 30 = 30, imports = 80 − 30 = 50 thousand. The world price is below the no-trade price, which is why the U.S. imports.

(c) The domestic price becomes 20 + 5 = 25. QD = 120 − 50 = 70, QS = 75 − 30 = 45, imports = 25 thousand.

(d) Revenue e = tariff × imports after the tariff = 5 × 25 = 125. Since P is in hundreds of dollars and Q in thousands, that is 125 × $100,000 = $12.5 million.

(e) Consumers lose the strip between 20 and 25 out to demand, a trapezoid: 5 × (70 + 80) ÷ 2 = 375. Producers gain area c, the strip out to supply: 5 × (30 + 45) ÷ 2 = 187.5.

6 points A quota instead. Use the bike market above (Pw = 20, demand QD = 120 − 2P, supply QS = 3P − 30). The government sets an import quota instead of a tariff. Adapted from Edwards Class 16 (quota definition) and the textbook (Frank, Bernanke, Antonovics, Heffetz), trade chapter

(a) (2 points) What quota gives the same domestic price of 25 as the tariff? Show the algebra.

(b) (2 points) Who gets area e under the quota, and why does that matter for the U.S.?

(c) (2 points) What would a quota of 60 thousand bikes do? Explain.

Show answer

(a) Imports at price P are QD − QS = (120 − 2P) − (3P − 30) = 150 − 5P. At P = 25 that is 150 − 125 = 25, so a quota of 25 thousand bikes.

(b) Price, quantities, and the deadweight loss of 62.5 are the same as with the tariff. Full credit: a quota of 25 from 150 − 5P; same price and DWL, but e goes to license holders, so the U.S. is worse off if foreigners get it; a 60 quota does not bind because imports are only 50.

6 points Subsidize instead of tax. Same bike market, with no tariff. The government instead pays domestic bike makers a subsidy of 5 for every bike they produce. Buyers still pay the world price of 20. Adapted from Saez ECON 2 Fall 2024 midterm 2, Q4 (sugar subsidy)

(a) (2 points) Find U.S. production, consumption, and imports.

(b) (2 points) Find the change in producer surplus, the cost to the government, and the deadweight loss.

(c) (2 points) Compare the deadweight loss with the tariff's and explain the difference.

Show answer

(a) Producers receive 20 + 5 = 25 per bike, so QS = 75 − 30 = 45. Buyers still pay 20, so QD = 80. Imports = 80 − 45 = 35 thousand.

(b) Supply starts at P = 10. Producer surplus rises from ½ × (20 − 10) × 30 = 150 to ½ × (25 − 10) × 45 = 337.5, a gain of 187.5 (the same as area c). The government pays 5 × 45 = 225. DWL = 225 − 187.5 = 37.5, which is the production triangle d.

4 points What the revenue tells you. Before a 20% tariff, the U.S. imported $250 billion a year of a group of products, valued at world prices. The tariff raises $45 billion a year. Assume U.S. buyers pay the full tariff. Adapted from Olney Econ 1 Fall 2019 midterm 1, Q3

2 points Before trade, U.S. buyers paid $6 a bushel for soybeans. Once trade opens at a world price of $8, they pay $8, even though nothing about U.S. supply or U.S. demand changed. Explain why, using Edwards' assumption about the world market.

Show answer

Edwards assumes that world demand and world supply at the world price "are perfectly elastic," so the U.S. is a price taker (Class 16, slide 14). U.S. farmers can sell as much as they want abroad at $8. No farmer will sell to a U.S. buyer for less than $8, so U.S. buyers must pay $8 or go without. Neither curve shifts. Buyers move up their demand curve from 6 bushels to 4, and farmers move up their supply curve from 6 to 8.

Full credit: the U.S. is a price taker facing perfectly elastic world demand at $8, so farmers will not accept less at home; the change is a movement along the curves, not a shift.

4 points Good news for farmhands. Before the U.S. opens to trade, the soybean price is the no-trade price, $6. When trade opens, it rises to the world price, $8. A soybean worker's marginal product is MPL = 3 − L/4 bushels per hour (L in thousands of workers), and labor supply is w = L.

(a) (2 points) Find the wage and employment before and after trade opens. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Describe the labor market graph and explain why the curve moved. What happens at the same time to workers making washing machines, the import good?

Show answer

(a) Before: MRPL = P × MPL = 6 × (3 − L/4) = 18 − 1.5L. Set it equal to labor supply: 18 − 1.5L = L, so 18 = 2.5L and L = 7.2 thousand workers, with w = $7.20. Check: MPL at L = 7.2 is 3 − 1.8 = 1.2 bushels, and 1.2 × $6 = $7.20. After: MRPL = 8 × (3 − L/4) = 24 − 2L. Set 24 − 2L = L, so 3L = 24 and L = 8 thousand, with w = $8. Check: MPL at L = 8 is 1 bushel, worth $8. The wage rises from $7.20 to $8 an hour and employment from 7,200 to 8,000.

(b) Draw an upward-sloping labor supply curve S, labor demand D1 (MRPL at $6), and D2 (MRPL at $8) above it, with an arrow from D1 to D2. 4 points Reverse the revenue. Use the washing machine market from this unit: demand P = 20 − Q, supply P = Q, world price 4. The government imposes a tariff and reports that it collected revenue of 18.

(a) (2 points) How big is the tariff? Show the algebra.

(b) (2 points) Find the domestic price, imports, and the deadweight loss.

Show answer

(a) Call the tariff t. The home price is 4 + t, so QS = 4 + t and QD = 20 − (4 + t) = 16 − t. Imports = (16 − t) − (4 + t) = 12 − 2t. Revenue = t × (12 − 2t) = 12t − 2t². Set 12t − 2t² = 18 and move everything to one side: 2t² − 12t + 18 = 0. Divide by 2: t² − 6t + 9 = 0, which is (t − 3)² = 0, so t = 3. Check: 3 × (12 − 6) = 18. In fact 18 is the most any tariff can raise here: a tariff of 2 or 4 raises 16, and a tariff of 6 raises 0 because it is prohibitive. Past t = 3, imports shrink faster than the tax per unit grows.

(b) Price = 4 + 3 = 7. QS = 7 and QD = 13, so imports = 6. Area d = ½ × 3 × (7 − 4) = 4.5 and area f = ½ × 3 × (16 − 13) = 4.5, so DWL = 9. Check: consumers lose 128 − ½ × 13 × 13 = 128 − 84.5 = 43.5; producers gain ½ × 7 × 7 − 8 = 24.5 − 8 = 16.5; and 43.5 − 16.5 − 18 = 9.

Full credit: revenue written as t × (12 − 2t), solved to t = 3; price 7, imports 6, DWL 9 as two triangles of 4.5.

One-minute review

Quick check

The world price is above the no-trade price. Does the country export or import?

It exports, because at that price home producers make more than home buyers want.

Is tariff revenue the tariff times imports before or after the tariff?

After: t × (QD2 − QS2), because only the units still imported pay the tax.

Which areas on the tariff graph are deadweight loss?

Only triangles d and f, because c goes to producers and e goes to the government.

The harvest is fixed, cannot be stored, and the only foreign buyer quits. Where is the new price?

On domestic demand at the harvested quantity, because home buyers must take the whole crop.

A trade war cuts the price of soybeans. Which labor market curve moves?

Labor demand shifts down, because MRPL = MR × MPL and MR (the price) fell; labor supply stays put.

A quota and a tariff give the same price. What is different?

Who gets rectangle e: the government under a tariff, the holders of import licenses under a quota.

  • Draw domestic S and D, then a flat world price line. The country is a price taker, so the domestic price becomes Pw.
  • Pw above the no-trade price: exports = QS − QD. Pw below: imports = QD − QS. Always the horizontal gap at Pw.
  • Exports: producers win, consumers lose. Imports: consumers win, producers lose. Net gain = triangle between Pw and the curves (4 in the soybean example).
  • Trade war with a fixed, unstorable harvest: price falls to domestic demand at QS. Soybeans: $8 to $4, below the $6 no-trade price.
  • Other buyers at a lower world price set a floor; next season, quantity adjusts and the market returns to the domestic equilibrium.
  • Labor demand MRPL = MR × MPL: up in export industries, down in import industries and in a trade war. Trade rearranges jobs.
  • Tariff: price rises to Pw + t. Consumers lose c+d+e+f, producers gain c, revenue e = t × imports after, DWL = d + f. Example: 30, 10, 16, 4.
  • Quota with the same imports gives the same price; rectangle e becomes quota rent. A tariff that lifts the price to the no-trade price is prohibitive.
  • Arguments for protection: national security, supply chains, diversification, infant industry, particular jobs, domestic externalities.
  • China shock: concentrated, lasting losses (North Hickory −5.32 vs Raleigh-Cary −3.84). Final 4(d): the U.S. lost the soybean round; China won.
