Practice final B: shaken confidence and the price of a machine

A full 60-point final in the exact shape of Edwards' Fall 2025 final: 2 admin points, six 2-point multiple choice questions, five 2-point short answers, a 24-point Keynesian cross problem, and a 12-point problem on capital and labor. Set a 90-minute timer, keep one page of notes (front and back) and a calculator, and write every answer on paper before you open the key. For graph parts, copy the blank axes and draw your full answer, with labels and values, first. Every number and every story on this page is made up for practice.

The exam

A workable split is about 15 minutes on Question 1, 15 on Question 2, 35 on Question 3, and 20 on Question 4, which leaves 5 minutes to check your work. Edwards tells you to confine answers to the box and that less is more, so practice saying each idea in two to four sentences. When you grade a part, compare it to the "Full credit" line, which is modeled on what his Fall 2025 key wrote in red.

Abbreviations used below: PAE is planned aggregate expenditure (C + I + G + NX), mpc is the marginal propensity to consume, GDP is gross domestic product, MPL and MPK are the marginal products of labor and capital, MR is marginal revenue, and MRPL and MRPK are the marginal revenue products of labor and capital (MR × MPL and MR × MPK).

1. (12 points total) Multiple choice: the margin of victory

For each of these questions, circle the one correct answer.

2 points 1(a). You split your daily snack budget between coffee and frozen yogurt. You are maximizing your utility when

(i) The marginal utility of coffee equals the marginal utility of frozen yogurt
(ii) The marginal utility per dollar spent is the same for coffee and frozen yogurt
(iii) You buy the same number of coffees and frozen yogurts
(iv) The total utility from coffee equals the total utility from frozen yogurt

Show answer

(ii). This is the rational spending rule from his final review: MUc ÷ Pc = MUf ÷ Pf. If the last dollar on coffee bought more utility than the last dollar on frozen yogurt, you could move a dollar from yogurt to coffee and be better off, so you are not yet at the best bundle. Option (i) ignores prices, and that only works when the two goods cost the same.

Full credit: circling (ii).

2 points 1(b). At the quantity a profit-maximizing monopolist chooses to sell,

(i) Marginal benefit equals marginal cost, so the outcome is efficient
(ii) Marginal benefit is greater than marginal cost, so producing more would add more to society's benefit than to its cost
(iii) Marginal benefit is less than marginal cost, so the monopolist produces too much
(iv) Price equals marginal revenue

Show answer

(ii). The monopolist sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost, and because it must cut its price on every unit to sell one more, marginal revenue is below price. Price measures buyers' marginal benefit, so at the monopoly quantity the marginal benefit is above the marginal cost. The units between the monopoly quantity and the efficient quantity are never made, and that lost surplus is the deadweight loss. His review slide on monopoly says exactly this: at the monopoly quantity, MB is greater than MC. Option (iv) is wrong because for a monopolist, price is above marginal revenue.

Full credit: circling (ii).

2 points 1(c). Which of these shifts the labor demand curve for hospital nurses to the right?

(i) New monitoring equipment lets each nurse care for more patients per shift
(ii) Hospitals can charge more for each patient visit
(iii) The wage of nurses falls
(iv) Both (i) and (ii)

Show answer

(iv). Labor demand is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL. Better equipment raises the marginal product of labor (MPL), and a higher price per visit raises marginal revenue (MR). Either one raises MRPL at every quantity of nurses, so the whole demand curve shifts right. A lower wage, option (iii), is a movement along the demand curve, not a shift.

Full credit: circling (iv).

2 points 1(d). Over long periods of time, the key source of rising real GDP per person is

(i) Higher average labor productivity, which comes mainly from capital accumulation and technological progress
(ii) Larger government budget deficits
(iii) Lower interest rates set by the Federal Reserve
(iv) A weaker currency that raises net exports

Show answer

(i). His final review lists this as a core macro lesson: improvements in average labor productivity are the key source of long-run growth, and capital accumulation and technological progress are the key sources of those improvements. Options (iii) and (iv) can raise spending and output for a while, but in the long run the Fed cannot hold the real interest rate away from its long-run level, so they cannot raise living standards for decades.

Full credit: circling (i).

2 points 1(e). Suppose inflation rises well above the Fed's 2% target. According to the Fed's reaction function, the Fed will

(i) Raise the real interest rate, which lowers planned spending and output and over time brings inflation down
(ii) Lower the real interest rate so that output rises and prices fall
(iii) Raise government purchases to absorb the extra spending
(iv) Do nothing, because inflation always returns to target on its own

Show answer

(i). The reaction function says the Fed raises the real interest rate when inflation rises and lowers it when inflation falls. A higher real rate reduces planned investment and interest-sensitive consumption, so PAE shifts down and output falls below potential. Inflation then responds gradually to that negative output gap and comes down. Option (iii) is fiscal policy, which Congress controls, not the Fed.

Full credit: circling (i).

2 points 1(f). If a country's domestic saving is smaller than its domestic investment, then

(i) It has net capital inflows and runs a trade deficit
(ii) It has net capital outflows and runs a trade surplus
(iii) Its trade must be balanced
(iv) Its currency must appreciate every year

Show answer

(i). His balance of payments slide shows S + NKI = I and NX = −NKI, where NKI is net capital inflows. If saving S is less than investment I, the gap must be filled by foreign funds, so NKI is positive. Since net exports equal minus net capital inflows, NX is negative, which is a trade deficit.

Full credit: circling (i).

2. (10 points total) Short answers, real returns

Answer each part in the box in a few sentences. Show your work where there is arithmetic.

2 points 2(a). Which of these transactions this year add to this year's U.S. GDP? For each one, say yes or no and give the reason in a few words. (1) A student buys a new laptop that was assembled this year in a factory in Fremont. (2) A tire company in Ohio sells $40,000 of tires to an automaker, which puts them on new cars that it sells this year. (3) A family buys a house built in 1995 directly from its previous owners. (4) A student buys a new phone that was assembled this year in China.

Show answer

GDP is the market value of final goods and services newly produced within the country during the period.

  1. Yes. The laptop is a final good, newly produced, inside the U.S. It shows up as consumption.
  2. No for the tires by themselves. Tires sold to an automaker are an intermediate good. Their value is already inside the price of the new cars, which are the final goods and do count. Counting the tires separately would count them twice.
  3. No. The house was built and counted in 1995. Selling it again this year moves ownership of an existing asset and creates no new production.
  4. No (apart from the U.S. store's markup). The phone was produced in China. Consumption goes up by its price, but imports go up by the same amount, so net exports fall and the two cancel in C + I + G + NX.

Full credit: yes, no, no, no, with the reasons: final versus intermediate, newly produced, and produced within the country.

2 points 2(b). A tiny economy produces only burritos and textbooks. In year 1 it makes 100 burritos at $10 each and 20 textbooks at $50 each. In year 2 it makes 110 burritos at $12 each and 20 textbooks at $60 each. Find nominal GDP in both years and real GDP in year 2 using year 1 as the base year. By what percent did nominal GDP and real GDP grow? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Nominal GDP, year 1 (year 1 prices × year 1 quantities): 100 × $10 + 20 × $50 = $1,000 + $1,000 = $2,000. With year 1 as the base year, real GDP in year 1 is also $2,000.
  2. Nominal GDP, year 2 (year 2 prices × year 2 quantities): 110 × $12 + 20 × $60 = $1,320 + $1,200 = $2,520.
  3. Real GDP, year 2 (year 1 prices × year 2 quantities): 110 × $10 + 20 × $50 = $1,100 + $1,000 = $2,100.
  4. Nominal growth: (2,520 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 26%. Real growth: (2,100 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 5%.

Only 5 points of the 26% are more stuff. The rest is higher prices: 2,520 ÷ 2,100 = 1.20, so prices rose 20% on average, and 1.05 × 1.20 = 1.26 checks out. Real GDP removes price changes by valuing both years' quantities at the same prices.

Full credit: $2,000, $2,520, and $2,100, with nominal growth of 26% and real growth of 5%, and a sentence that the difference is price increases.

2 points 2(c). The exchange rate moves from 20 Mexican pesos per U.S. dollar to 16 pesos per U.S. dollar. Which currency appreciated and which depreciated? A hotel in Mexico City charges 800 pesos a night. What does a night cost an American traveler in dollars before and after? What happens to U.S. net exports to Mexico?

Show answer

A dollar now buys fewer pesos, so the dollar depreciated and the peso appreciated. (Before, one peso cost 1 ÷ 20 = 5 cents. After, one peso costs 1 ÷ 16 = 6.25 cents.)

The hotel costs 800 ÷ 20 pesos per dollar = $40 before and 800 ÷ 16 pesos per dollar = $50 after. Mexican goods got more expensive for Americans, so U.S. imports from Mexico fall. U.S. goods got cheaper for Mexicans (a $100 pair of U.S.-made boots falls from 2,000 pesos to 1,600 pesos), so U.S. exports rise. Both changes raise U.S. net exports.

Full credit: dollar depreciated and peso appreciated, $40 before and $50 after, and net exports rise because exports rise and imports fall.

2 points 2(d). In one day, a typical U.S. worker can produce either 4 tons of oranges or 8 pairs of sneakers. A typical Vietnamese worker can produce either 1 ton of oranges or 6 pairs of sneakers. Which country has an absolute advantage in each good? Which has a comparative advantage in each good? What should each country export? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Absolute advantage: the U.S. worker makes more of both goods (4 tons versus 1, and 8 pairs versus 6), so the U.S. has the absolute advantage in both.
  2. Opportunity cost of 1 ton of oranges: U.S. 8 ÷ 4 = 2 pairs of sneakers; Vietnam 6 ÷ 1 = 6 pairs.
  3. Opportunity cost of 1 pair of sneakers: U.S. 4 ÷ 8 = ½ ton of oranges; Vietnam 1 ÷ 6 = ⅙ ton.

The U.S. gives up less to make oranges (2 pairs versus 6), so it has the comparative advantage in oranges and should export oranges. Vietnam gives up less to make sneakers (⅙ ton versus ½ ton), so it has the comparative advantage in sneakers and should export sneakers. Both countries gain from trade at any price between 2 and 6 pairs of sneakers per ton of oranges.

Full credit: U.S. absolute advantage in both; opportunity costs computed; U.S. exports oranges and Vietnam exports sneakers because each is the low opportunity cost producer of that good.

2 points 2(e). A small town has 10 households with these annual incomes, in thousands of dollars: 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 100, 250, and 300. What share of total income goes to the top 10% of households? Researchers like Piketty and Saez measure top income shares using income tax returns rather than household surveys. Why?

Show answer

Total income is 20 + 30 + 40 + 50 + 60 + 70 + 80 + 100 + 250 + 300 = 1,000 thousand. The top 10% is the single richest household, with 300. The top 10% share is 300 ÷ 1,000 = 30%. (The top 20% share is (250 + 300) ÷ 1,000 = 55%.)

His slides on Piketty and Saez explain the tax return choice: surveys typically do not tell us much about incomes at the very top, because any sample contains very few very rich households. Tax returns cover top earners, and the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) publishes how many taxpayers earn different amounts. Combined with total income and the total number of households from other sources, that gives the fraction of income going to the top 1%, the top 10%, and so on.

Full credit: 30%, plus the reason that surveys miss the top of the distribution while tax returns capture it.

3. (24 points total) Shaken confidence: a Keynesian cross-examination

Suppose that at the start of 2027, potential output is Y* = $30 trillion, the marginal propensity to consume is mpc = 0.6, and this system models planned aggregate expenditures (PAE) in trillions of dollars:

ComponentEquation
ConsumptionC = 6 + 0.6Y
Planned investmentI = 4
Government purchasesG = 4
Net exportsNX = −2 (a trade deficit of $2 trillion)
Planned aggregate expenditurePAE = C + I + G + NX

2 points 3(a). Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find the level of PAE when Y = 0, which is the vertical intercept in the Keynesian cross diagram. Show your work.

Show answer

Combining, PAE = C + I + G + NX = 6 + 0.6Y + 4 + 4 − 2 = 12 + 0.6Y. When Y = 0, PAE = 12, and that is the vertical intercept. The 12 is autonomous spending, the part of spending that does not depend on income.

Full credit: PAE = 12 + 0.6Y written out, and the intercept of 12.

2 points 3(b). Using your answer to part (a), set PAE = Y and solve for the short-run equilibrium level of Y. Is there an output gap? Show your work.

Show answer

Set 12 + 0.6Y = Y. Then 12 = 0.4Y, so Y = 30. Check: C = 6 + 0.6 × 30 = 24, and 24 + 4 + 4 − 2 = 30. Equilibrium output equals potential output of $30 trillion, so there is no output gap.

Full credit: 12 = 0.4Y, Y = 30, and "no gap" because Y equals Y*.

In early 2027, a survey shows consumer confidence falling as tariffs raise the prices of imported goods, and households cut back on spending at every level of income. At the same time, trading partners retaliate with tariffs on U.S. exports. Suppose retaliation cuts U.S. exports by more than the U.S. tariffs cut imports, so the new situation is:

ComponentEquation
ConsumptionC' = 5.4 + 0.6Y
Planned investmentI = 4
Government purchasesG = 4
Net exportsNX' = −2.6
Planned aggregate expenditurePAE' = C' + I + G + NX'

2 points 3(c). Combine the expenditure terms in PAE' and find its vertical intercept. Show your work.

Show answer

PAE' = C' + I + G + NX' = 5.4 + 0.6Y + 4 + 4 − 2.6 = 10.8 + 0.6Y. When Y = 0, PAE' = 10.8, the new vertical intercept.

Full credit: PAE' = 10.8 + 0.6Y and the intercept of 10.8.

2 points 3(d). Set PAE' = Y and solve for the new short-run equilibrium Y. What is the output gap now, and what kind of gap is it? Show your work.

Show answer

Set 10.8 + 0.6Y = Y. Then 10.8 = 0.4Y, so Y = 27. Check: C' = 5.4 + 0.6 × 27 = 5.4 + 16.2 = 21.6, and 21.6 + 4 + 4 − 2.6 = 27. The output gap is Y − Y* = 27 − 30 = −3, a recessionary gap of $3 trillion, because output is below potential.

Full credit: Y = 27 with the work, and a recessionary (negative) gap of $3 trillion.

4 points 3(e). On the axes below, graph the original PAE line from part (a), the new PAE' line from part (c), and the 45-degree line. Show both equilibria and label the intercepts, the lines, and the values on both axes.

Show answer

Draw the 45-degree line through the origin (every point where Y = PAE). Draw PAE1 starting at 12 on the vertical axis with slope 0.6, so it rises 6 for every 10 to the right and reaches 30 at Y = 30. Draw PAE2 starting at 10.8 with the same slope, so it is parallel and 1.2 lower everywhere. Mark the equilibria where each PAE line crosses the 45-degree line, at (30, 30) and (27, 27), with dotted lines to both axes.

Full credit: a 45-degree line through the origin; two parallel PAE lines with intercepts 12 and 10.8 and a slope flatter than 45 degrees; equilibria at Y = 30 and Y = 27 marked on both axes; every line labeled.

2 points 3(f). By how much did planned aggregate expenditure change between (a) and (c)? Express it in trillions of dollars and say whether it is an increase, a decrease, or no change. Explain fully what the arithmetic means about planned spending and how it appears in the graph.

Show answer

PAE decreased by $1.2 trillion at every level of income. Autonomous consumption fell by $0.6 trillion (from 6 to 5.4) because households lost confidence, and net exports fell by $0.6 trillion (from −2 to −2.6) because retaliation cut exports. Investment and government purchases did not change, and neither did the mpc. In the graph this is a parallel, vertical drop of 1.2 in the PAE line, from an intercept of 12 to an intercept of 10.8.

Full credit: a decrease of $1.2 trillion, the two sources (C down 0.6 and NX down 0.6), and "a vertical, parallel shift down of the PAE line."

2 points 3(g). By how much did GDP change between (b) and (d)? Express it in trillions of dollars and say whether it is an increase, a decrease, or no change. Explain fully what the arithmetic means about GDP and how it appears in the graph.

Show answer

GDP decreased by $3 trillion, from $30 trillion to $27 trillion. PAE fell by 1.2 at every income level, and because the PAE line slopes upward with a slope less than 1, its crossing with the 45-degree line moves down and to the left by more than the vertical drop: from (30, 30) to (27, 27). Firms produce what they expect to sell, so output falls until it again equals planned spending, which happens at 27.

Full credit: a decrease of $3 trillion, and the equilibrium moving left along the 45-degree line from 30 to 27.

2 points 3(h). Are your answers to (f) and (g) the same or different? If different, which is larger in magnitude, and why? Compute the multiplier and use it to connect the two numbers. Explain fully, referencing the model.

Show answer

They are different: the change in GDP ($3 trillion) is larger than the change in PAE ($1.2 trillion). The reason is the nonzero marginal propensity to consume. When output falls, household income falls, and households cut consumption by 0.6 of every lost dollar, which cuts spending and output again, and so on: 1.2 + 0.72 + 0.432 + ... adds up to 3. The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 2.5, and 2.5 × (−1.2) = −3, which matches part (g).

Full credit: "different, GDP change is larger," the mpc of 0.6 as the reason, and the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 2.5 with 2.5 × 1.2 = 3.

Congress is gridlocked, so the Federal Reserve decides to respond. Before the shock, the Fed's policy rate (the nominal interest rate it sets) is 6% and inflation is 2%.

2 points 3(i). Explain fully how the Fed cutting its policy rate raises planned investment and consumption. Then show on your graph from (e) what the cut does to the PAE line and to output.

Show answer

The Fed lowers the nominal interest rate i, using its tools such as the interest rate it pays on banks' reserves and open-market operations. Because of inflation inertia, inflation π barely moves in the short run, so the real interest rate r = i − π falls by about as much as i. A lower real interest rate makes borrowing cheaper and raises the present value of the future returns from a new machine or building, so planned investment rises. A lower real rate also makes saving less attractive, so households spend more on interest-sensitive purchases like new cars and homes, which means autonomous consumption rises. (A lower r also tends to depreciate the dollar and raise net exports.) All of these raise PAE at every level of income, so the PAE line shifts up in parallel, and output rises by the multiplier times that shift.

Full credit: the chain from a lower nominal rate to a lower real rate (because inflation moves slowly), to higher I and C, to an upward parallel shift of PAE, to higher Y on the graph.

2 points 3(j). Suppose each 1 percentage point cut in the real interest rate raises planned investment by $0.3 trillion and autonomous consumption by $0.1 trillion, and inflation stays at 2% in the short run. Ignore any effect on net exports. How much autonomous spending is needed to close the gap from (d), and what policy rate should the Fed set? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. The gap is $3 trillion and the multiplier is 2.5, so autonomous spending must rise by 3 ÷ 2.5 = $1.2 trillion.
  2. Each 1 point cut in r adds 0.3 + 0.1 = $0.4 trillion of autonomous spending, so r must fall by 1.2 ÷ 0.4 = 3 percentage points.
  3. The real rate starts at r = i − π = 6% − 2% = 4%. With inflation stuck at 2%, cutting i by 3 points to 3% lowers r to 1%.
  4. Check: I rises from 4 to 4 + 0.9 = 4.9, autonomous consumption rises from 5.4 to 5.4 + 0.3 = 5.7, so PAE = 5.7 + 0.6Y + 4.9 + 4 − 2.6 = 12 + 0.6Y, and 12 = 0.4Y gives Y = 30.

Like the offsetting change in G on the Fall 2025 final, the Fed only needs to undo the $1.2 trillion drop in PAE, not the $3 trillion drop in GDP, because the multiplier works in both directions.

Full credit: $1.2 trillion of autonomous spending (3 ÷ 2.5), a 3 point cut (1.2 ÷ 0.4), and a policy rate of 3% (real rate 1%).

2 points 3(k). Suppose the Fed overshoots and cuts its policy rate by 4 percentage points instead. What is the new equilibrium output, and what does the model say will happen to inflation and to the Fed's rate after that? Explain.

Show answer

A 4 point cut adds 4 × 0.4 = $1.6 trillion of autonomous spending, so PAE = 10.8 + 1.6 + 0.6Y = 12.4 + 0.6Y. Setting 12.4 + 0.6Y = Y gives 12.4 = 0.4Y, so Y = 31, an expansionary gap of $1 trillion above potential. Output above potential means inflation will gradually rise. Following its reaction function, the Fed will then raise the real interest rate, which lowers PAE and brings output back toward potential. The Fed can push the real rate around in the short run, but it cannot keep it away from its long-run level forever.

Full credit: Y = 31 (an expansionary gap of 1), inflation gradually rises, and the Fed responds by raising r.

4. (12 points total) Packing it in: machines, workers, and present value

A fruit packing house in the Central Valley is deciding whether to buy a new sorting and packing line, which costs $14,000 and lasts two years. The line needs a crew of workers to run it. The packing house sells in a competitive market at $2 per box, so its marginal revenue is MR = $2 per box. The line's marginal product of capital (MPK) is 5,500 extra boxes in year 1 and 3,025 extra boxes in year 2, as the machine wears out. Payments arrive at the end of each year. Recall that the present value of a stream of payments Ft is

PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2

and that for a unit of capital, Ft is its marginal revenue product, MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

2 points 4(a). Compute the line's MRPK in each year and its present value when the interest rate is i = 10%. Should the packing house buy it? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. MRPK in year 1: $2 × 5,500 = $11,000. MRPK in year 2: $2 × 3,025 = $6,050.
  2. PV = 11,000 ÷ 1.10 + 6,050 ÷ 1.102 = 11,000 ÷ 1.10 + 6,050 ÷ 1.21 = 10,000 + 5,000 = $15,000.

A firm buys a machine up to the point where the present value of its stream of MRPKs equals its price. Here the present value of $15,000 is above the $14,000 price, so yes, buy it: it adds $1,000 to the firm's value in today's dollars. $15,000 is also the most any buyer would pay for this line.

Full credit: MRPKs of $11,000 and $6,050, PV = $15,000 with the discounting shown, and "buy" because PV is above the price.

2 points 4(b). Suppose the interest rate rises to i = 20%. Recompute the present value. What happens to the price of a unit of this kind of capital, and does the packing house still buy the line? Explain.

Show answer

PV = 11,000 ÷ 1.20 + 6,050 ÷ 1.202 = 11,000 ÷ 1.20 + 6,050 ÷ 1.44 = 9,166.67 + 4,201.39 = $13,368.06.

A higher interest rate makes every future dollar worth less today, because each payment is divided by a larger (1 + i)t. The present value, which is what a unit of capital is worth to a buyer, falls from $15,000 to $13,368.06. That is now below the $14,000 price, so the packing house does not buy. This is why the investment demand curve slopes down: higher interest rates mean fewer machines purchased.

Full credit: $13,368.06 (or about $13,368), the price of capital falls when i rises, and "do not buy" because PV is now below $14,000.

2 points 4(c). Go back to i = 10%. Now suppose stepped-up immigration enforcement is expected to remove many of the workers who staff packing lines, so in year 2 the line can only be run part time and its MPK falls to 1,815 boxes. What happens to the line's present value and to the price of this kind of capital? Should the packing house still buy it? Show your work and explain.

Show answer

Year 2 MRPK falls to $2 × 1,815 = $3,630. PV = 11,000 ÷ 1.10 + 3,630 ÷ 1.21 = 10,000 + 3,000 = $13,000.

Capital and labor work together: a packing line with no crew packs nothing. If less labor will be available in the future, the future marginal product of capital falls, which pulls down its future marginal revenue product and therefore its present value. The price of a unit of capital falls, here from $15,000 to $13,000. Since $13,000 is below the $14,000 price, the packing house does not buy, even though interest rates did not change.

Full credit: PV of $13,000 with the work, the reason (less future labor lowers future MPK and MRPK), and "price of capital falls, do not buy."

Now consider the labor market for packing-house workers, holding the number of packing machines fixed. Recall that labor demand is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, and labor supply reflects workers' marginal cost of supplying their time. Suppose labor demand is w = 30 − L and labor supply before enforcement is w = 10 + L, where w is the hourly wage and L is thousands of workers. Enforcement removes 4 thousand workers at every wage.

4 points 4(d). Illustrate the labor market before and after the enforcement. Show all demand and supply curves, and the equilibrium wages and employment with values. Briefly explain.

Show answer

Before: 30 − L = 10 + L gives L = 10 thousand workers and w = $20 per hour. Removing 4 thousand workers at every wage shifts supply left to w = 10 + (L + 4) = 14 + L. After: 30 − L = 14 + L gives L = 8 thousand and w = $22. Labor demand does not move, because neither the price of a box (MR) nor the marginal product of a given worker (MPL) changed. The supply shift moves the market up and to the left along the demand curve: employment falls by 2 thousand and the wage rises by $2.

Full credit: downward-sloping demand labeled MRPL, supply shifting left from S1 to S2, equilibria at (10, $20) and (8, $22) marked on both axes, and one sentence saying supply fell while demand stayed put.

2 points 4(e). Who wins and who loses in this market, given what you found in (c) and (d)? Why is the MRPL of the last worker hired now higher than before?

Show answer

The workers who remain win: their wage rises from $20 to $22 an hour. The workers who are removed lose their jobs and earnings here. Packing-house owners lose twice: they pay more per worker while running with fewer workers, and, from part (c), the machines they own or planned to buy are worth less because each machine will have fewer hands to run it.

The last worker's MRPL rises to $22 because firms hire until MRPL equals the wage, and with fewer workers on the same machines, each worker's marginal product is higher. That is diminishing returns running in reverse: moving back up the MRPL curve.

Full credit: remaining workers gain (higher wage), removed workers and packing-house owners lose (higher labor costs and less valuable capital), and MRPL is higher because fewer workers means a higher marginal product along the same demand curve.

The three habits this exam drills: the change in GDP equals the multiplier, 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), times the vertical shift in PAE, so a policy only has to undo the shift in PAE. A rate cut works because inflation moves slowly, so a lower nominal rate means a lower real rate, which raises planned investment and interest-sensitive consumption. The price of capital is the present value of its future MRPKs, so it falls when interest rates rise or when anything, like less future labor, lowers its future marginal product.
