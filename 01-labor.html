Class 12 · Labor and wages

Labor and wages

Wages are a price, so they come from supply and demand. Firms demand workers up to the point where one more worker brings in as much revenue as she costs, which makes the labor demand curve the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL. On the Fall 2025 final, question 4(c) was worth 4 of the 12 trade points: after China stopped buying U.S. soybeans, you had to draw the soybean workers' labor market and show the whole MRPL curve shifting inward because the price, and so the marginal revenue, of soybeans collapsed.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

A wage is a price, so the labor market is an ordinary supply and demand graph with the wage on the vertical axis and the number of workers on the horizontal axis. Firms are the buyers: a firm keeps hiring until the last worker brings in as much extra revenue as her wage, so the labor demand curve is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL (marginal revenue times marginal product). For the final, remember one sorting rule: if the price of what workers make or their productivity changes, labor demand shifts; if the number of people willing to do the job changes, labor supply shifts.

The labor market: you are the seller here

Labor and capital (machines and buildings) are the inputs firms use to make things. The market for an input works like any other market, with one twist that trips people up. In most markets you are the buyer. In the labor market you are the seller: you sell your time to an employer. Edwards' slide calls this the "Tricky part."

  • Firms demand labor. Their demand comes from profit maximization: a firm hires another worker only if that worker adds at least as much to revenue as to cost.
  • Households supply labor. Their supply comes from utility maximization, which means each person picks the mix of work and free time that leaves her best off. She works another hour only if what the wage buys is worth more to her than the hour of free time she gives up.
  • The wage is the price of labor. Supply and demand together set the wage (W) and the number of workers employed (L).

He gives three reasons the labor market matters (Class 12, slide 9). Wages and jobs are central to people's lives. The supply and demand diagram tells you how any new development will change wages and employment. And the same diagram helps explain rising inequality, which is the next unit.

A vocabulary note from slide 7. When economists and reporters say "high-skilled" workers, they usually mean highly credentialed, that is, workers with more formal education such as a college degree. "Low-skilled" workers are those with less formal education. They may have real manual and communication skills; the label is shorthand for schooling, not a judgment about ability.

Marginal product of labor and diminishing returns

The marginal product of labor (MPL) is the extra output produced by one more worker, holding everything else (machines, land, factory space) fixed. You get it from a production table by subtracting: MPL of the 3rd worker = output with 3 workers minus output with 2 workers.

In plain words: the marginal product is how much more gets made when you add one more worker.

MPL falls as a firm adds workers. This is diminishing returns. The reason is that the extra workers share the same fixed equipment and space. On a farm with one tractor, the first few workers each add a lot. The sixth worker mostly waits for the tractor. Edwards is explicit (slide 14) that the downward slope "isn't because of market power." It comes from adding workers without adding capital.

In plain words: each extra worker adds less than the one before, because they all have to share the same tractor, oven, or office.

MRPL = MR × MPL: what one more worker is worth to the firm

A firm does not care about bushels for their own sake; it cares about revenue. The marginal revenue product of labor (MRPL) is the extra revenue generated by one more worker. It has two pieces (slide 11):

  • MPL, the extra output the worker produces, and
  • marginal revenue (MR), the extra revenue from selling one more unit of output.

MRPL = MR × MPL. The extra revenue from one more worker equals the extra units she makes times the extra revenue per unit.

In plain words: a worker is worth to the firm the extra things she makes times the extra money each of those things brings in.

A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: it is so small that it can sell as much as it wants at the market price. So its MR equals the market price P, because it can sell another unit without lowering its price. For a competitive firm, then, MRPL is the price times the MPL, which has its own name (slide 12):

VMPL = P × MPL, the value of the marginal product of labor. For a competitive firm, MRPL = VMPL.

In plain words: when a firm can sell all it wants at the going price, a worker is worth the extra things she makes times that price.

Why MRPL falls as L rises (slide 13): MPL falls because of diminishing returns, and MR is either constant (competitive firm) or falling (a firm with market power, which must cut its price to sell more). A falling number times a constant or falling number is a falling number.

The hiring rule: MRPL = W

Profit is maximized where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. In the labor market, the marginal revenue of a worker is her MRPL and her marginal cost is the wage W. So a firm keeps hiring as long as the next worker's MRPL is at least the wage, and stops where they are equal (slide 15):

Hire up to the point where MRPL = W. If MRPL > W, the next worker adds more revenue than cost, so hire her. If MRPL < W, she costs more than she brings in, so do not.

In plain words: keep hiring while the next worker brings in more money than her pay, and stop when she would bring in less.

With a table, the rule means: hire every worker whose MRPL is at least the wage, and stop at the first worker whose MRPL is below it. At $10 a bushel and a wage of $350 a day, the farm above hires 4 workers: the 4th adds $400, more than $350, and the 5th would add only $200.

The MRPL curve is the labor demand curve

Pick any wage. The firm hires the quantity of labor where MRPL equals that wage. So reading across from a wage to the MRPL curve and down gives the number of workers the firm wants. That is exactly what a demand curve does, so the firm's MRPL curve is its labor demand curve (slide 16). A lower wage means more workers hired, which is why it slopes down.

In plain words: the line showing what each extra worker is worth to the firm is the same line that shows how many workers it wants at each wage.

The market labor demand curve, MRPL = D, adds up the hiring of all firms at each wage (slide 17). He writes lowercase (mrpL, d, l) for one firm and uppercase (MRPL, D, L) for the market. On the exam, label the demand curve "D = MRPL" so the grader sees you know where it comes from.

Firms with market power: MRPL is below VMPL

A firm has market power in its product market when it can set its own price, like the only bakery in town. Such a firm must lower its price to sell more, and the lower price applies to every unit it sells, so its MR is below the price. Then MRPL = MR × MPL is below VMPL = P × MPL, and the firm hires fewer workers than a competitive firm with the same technology would. Worked example 4 below has the numbers.

In plain words: a firm that has to cut its price to sell more gets less from each extra item, so each extra worker is worth less to it, and it hires fewer people.

Market power on the other side of the labor market is different. When the buyer of labor has market power, it is called monopsony; when workers form a union, the union may have market power as a seller. Edwards leaves both to ECON 151 and 152 and says monopsony "won't be on our exams" (slide 18). Know the words; do not study the models.

Labor supply: leisure has a price, and the price is the wage

Households like goods and services, and they also like leisure, meaning time not spent working in the market (slide 19). Leisure has diminishing marginal utility like any good: the 10th free hour of the day is worth less to you than the 2nd. The price of an hour of leisure is the wage, because that is the income you give up by not working that hour.

In plain words: every hour you do not work costs you the pay you would have earned in that hour.

So the rational spending rule from the household unit applies (slide 20). MU is marginal utility, the extra happiness from one more unit:

MUleisure ÷ Pleisure = MUgoods and services ÷ Pgoods and services, where Pleisure = W. The last dollar's worth of free time should give the same extra happiness as the last dollar spent on goods.

In plain words: you have split your time well when one more dollar's worth of free time and one more dollar of stuff would make you equally happier.

When the wage rises, leisure gets more expensive, so the left side falls below the right side. Two effects follow, and they pull in opposite directions (slide 21):

  • Substitution effect: leisure now costs more relative to goods, so you substitute away from leisure and work more.
  • Income effect: at a higher wage you are richer. Richer people want more of normal goods (goods people buy more of when their income rises), and leisure is one of them, so you work less.

Which effect wins is, in his words, "an empirical question!" For this course he assumes the substitution effect dominates, so the labor supply curve slopes up, both for one household and for the market (slide 22). Draw it upward on every exam graph.

In plain words: a raise makes free time more costly, which pushes you to work more, and it also makes you richer, which pushes you to relax more. This class assumes the first push is stronger.

What moves the labor supply curve (as opposed to moving along it): the number of people with the relevant skills, such as through immigration or more education, and people's tastes for work versus leisure (Class 13, slide 31 says its position "depends on tastes and on the number of workers with the relevant skills").

Equilibrium: wage and employment together

The market wage W1 and employment L1 are where labor supply crosses labor demand (slide 8). If the wage were higher, more people would want jobs than firms want to fill. If it were lower, firms could not fill all the jobs they want to. To find the crossing with algebra, set the two equations equal. With D: W = 30 − 2L and S: W = 6 + 2L, write 30 − 2L = 6 + 2L. Add 2L to both sides and subtract 6: 24 = 4L, so L = 6 (thousand workers). Plug back into demand: W = 30 − 2(6) = 30 − 12 = $18. Supply gives the same wage, 6 + 2(6) = $18, which checks the answer.

In plain words: the market wage is the one where the number of people who want this kind of job equals the number of these jobs firms want to fill.

What shifts labor demand

Because labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, anything that changes MR or MPL at every level of employment shifts the whole curve. Slide 10 lists the demand for the firm's product, the productivity of labor, and the prices of other inputs. His final review deck put it in one line next to the labor market diagram: demand "increases when workers become more productive and/or when the price or marginal revenue of their output rises."

Labor demand shifts out (up) when the output price or MR rises, or when MPL rises (more capital, better technology). It shifts in (down) when the output price or MR falls, or when MPL falls. A change in the wage itself is a move along the curve, never a shift.

In plain words: if what the workers make sells for more, or they can make more of it, firms want more of them at every wage. If only the wage changes, firms slide along the same curve.

Example 1 from his slides: a fall in demand for the product. When the housing bubble burst in 2008, demand for construction services fell (slides 23 to 27). Follow two markets in order:

  1. In the market for construction services, demand shifts left, so the price of construction falls (P1 to P2).
  2. For a construction firm, MR is that price. A lower price lowers MRPL = P × MPL at every level of employment, so the labor demand curve for construction workers shifts left.
  3. Wages and employment of construction workers both fall.

His FRED chart (slide 27) shows employment of hourly construction workers peaking at a little over 6 million around 2006 and falling to below 4.5 million around 2010 and 2011 (read from the chart).

Example 2 from his slides: more capital or better technology. Computers spread across industries in the late 1980s and 1990s (slides 28 to 30). For workers whose jobs use computers, such as architects, engineers, and scientists, more machines or better technology raise MPL. In most cases that raises MRPL, so labor demand shifts out and both wages and employment rise. He shows two kinds of evidence. Autor's chart of real wages of full-time male workers by education (slide 31) shows workers with more than a bachelor's degree pulling far ahead of everyone else since the 1980s. Lindert and Williamson's data on English workers during the Industrial Revolution (slides 33 and 34) show real average earnings of white-collar employees rising from £61.94 in 1755 to £258.88 in 1851 (about 4.2 times), while farm labourers went from £19.01 to £29.04 (about 1.5 times). He marks the first group "Big increases" and the second "Some increases."

The complication (slide 32). Higher productivity also shifts out the supply curve of the product, which lowers the product's price. Since MRPL = MR × MPL, MPL goes up while MR goes down. If the price falls by a lot, MRPL could fall. He calls this possible but "not normal." Historically, capital and technology have raised workers' employment and wages, "maybe because product demand is elastic." Elastic product demand means buyers take a lot more of the good after a small price cut, so the price does not need to fall much to sell the extra output.

The quick test is the multiplication. Turn each percentage into a factor: a 50% rise is × 1.5, and a 20% fall is × 0.8. If MPL rises 50% and the price falls 20%, the new MRPL is 1.5 × 0.8 = 1.2 times the old one. That is up 20%, so labor demand still shifts out. A factor above 1 means demand shifts out; below 1 means it shifts in.

In plain words: a machine that lets each worker make more also floods the market and lowers the price, so check whether the extra output outweighs the lower price.

The soybean trade war: exactly what Q4 asked

The Fall 2025 final ran this chain across two graphs. First the soybean market: before the trade war, the U.S. exported about half its crop to China at a world price Pw (the price buyers on the world market pay) above the price that would clear the domestic market. Exports are the horizontal gap between domestic quantity supplied and domestic quantity demanded at Pw. Then China's tariffs halted its purchases. With the crop already planted and no way to store or destroy it, the whole harvest had to be sold at home, so the price fell all the way down the domestic demand curve to the price at which Americans would buy the entire crop.

Then the labor market for soybean workers, part (c). A soybean farm is a price taker, so its MR is the price of soybeans. When the price falls, MR falls, and so MRPL = MR × MPL is lower at every level of employment. Nothing happened to MPL: the workers and tractors are as productive as before. Nothing happened to labor supply either. So the only curve that moves is labor demand, and it shifts inward (down). The new equilibrium has a lower wage and fewer workers.

For full credit on a graph like Q4(c), show both curves before (S and MRPL1), the new demand curve MRPL2, both equilibria, and W1, W2, L1, L2 on the axes. Then one sentence: the price of soybeans, which is the farms' marginal revenue, fell, so MRPL = MR × MPL fell at every L and labor demand shifted inward, lowering wages and employment. The key asked for exactly that. A technical note: since every MRPL value gets multiplied by the same fraction (in the made-up numbers, $8 ÷ $14), the exact new curve is lower by more at small L than at large L. His slides draw demand shifts as parallel, and the key only asks that the whole curve shift inward or downward, so a parallel shift is fine.

What shifts labor supply: immigration

Example 4 in his deck (slides 38 and 39): an increase in immigration of low-skilled workers adds people to the market for low-skilled labor, so labor supply shifts out (right). Along the unchanged demand curve, the wage falls and employment rises. Note what moves: supply, not demand. Immigrants are new sellers of labor.

In plain words: more people looking for the same kind of job pushes that job's pay down and raises the number of people working in it.

The reverse shock, fewer workers (for example, if immigrant workers are detained or removed, which the Fall 2025 final's macro story mentioned), shifts labor supply in: the wage rises and employment falls.

Card's Mariel boatlift study

Testing this prediction with data is hard (slide 40). Earlier studies compared wages and the number of immigrants across cities. But immigrants often move to a city because its labor demand is growing, which pushes wages up at the same time the new supply pushes them down. You could find no correlation between immigration and wages even if the supply effect were exactly as the theory says. In his Class 13 version: "flows of immigrants are not randomly applied treatments!"

David Card used a natural experiment (slide 41). A natural experiment is a real event that changes one thing for one group for reasons unrelated to the outcome you care about, so you can compare that group with similar groups the event missed. His event was the Mariel Boatlift of May to September 1980, when 125,000 Cubans migrated to the U.S. and almost all went to Miami. They came for political reasons, not because Miami's labor market was booming. There was also excellent wage and employment data from before and after.

In plain words: Card found a case where a big group of workers arrived for reasons that had nothing to do with jobs, so he could see the effect of the newcomers by themselves.

Second, labor demand may have been quite elastic, because Miami had industries that used low-skilled workers and could expand easily. Elastic labor demand means firms' hiring responds a lot to a small change in the wage, so the demand curve is flat. With a very flat demand curve, a big shift in supply moves the wage only a little and mostly raises employment.

In plain words: if employers can easily add many more jobs when pay dips a little, newcomers get hired without pulling pay down much.

A wage above equilibrium: unions and the minimum wage

Example 3 in his deck (slides 35 to 37): an autoworkers' union negotiates a wage WN above the equilibrium wage. The negotiated wage works like a price floor, a minimum price that buyers must pay. Workers who keep their jobs earn more. But a profit-maximizing firm will not pay more than the MRPL of the last worker hired, so it cuts employment back to where MRPL = WN, at LD1 on the demand curve. Meanwhile more people want auto jobs at the higher wage, LS1 on the supply curve. The gap LS1 − LD1 is unemployment among autoworkers.

A legal minimum wage above the equilibrium wage is the same graph (this is the textbook's standard treatment; his Class 13 slides list the minimum wage as one way to raise incomes at the bottom). A floor set below the equilibrium wage does nothing, because the market already pays more.

In plain words: a required wage above the market wage helps the people who keep their jobs, but firms hire fewer people, and more people want those jobs than can get them.

Worked examples

Example 1: How many workers should the soybean farm hire?

Use the farm table above. Soybeans sell for $10 a bushel in a competitive market and the wage is $350 a day. Then the trade war cuts the price to $5.

  1. MPL from the output column: 100, 80, 60, 40, 20, 10 bushels for workers 1 through 6.
  2. The farm is competitive, so MR = P and MRPL = VMPL = $10 × MPL: $1,000, $800, $600, $400, $200, $100.
  3. Hire every worker with VMPL at least $350. Workers 1 to 4 qualify ($400 ≥ $350); worker 5 does not ($200 < $350). Hire 4.
  4. Check with profit (revenue minus wages, ignoring other costs): 3 workers give 2,400 − 1,050 = $1,350; 4 give 2,800 − 1,400 = $1,400; 5 give 3,000 − 1,750 = $1,250. Four is best.
  5. At $5 a bushel, VMPL is halved: $500, $400, $300, $200, $100, $50. Workers 1 and 2 qualify ($400 ≥ $350); worker 3 does not ($300 < $350). Hire 2.
  6. Check: 2 workers give 180 × $5 − 700 = $200; 3 give 1,200 − 1,050 = $150; 1 gives 500 − 350 = $150. Two is best.

Answer: 4 workers at $10, and 2 workers at $5. The price cut did not change how productive anyone is. It cut the revenue each worker brings in, which is exactly the Q4(c) logic at the level of one farm.

Example 2: Equilibrium, then two demand shifts

Labor demand is W = 30 − 2L and labor supply is W = 6 + 2L, with L in thousands of workers and W in dollars per hour. (a) Find the equilibrium. (b) A fall in the product's price shifts demand to W = 22 − 2L. (c) Starting again from (a), new technology instead shifts demand to W = 38 − 2L.

  1. (a) Set demand equal to supply: 30 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 24 = 4L and L = 6. Then W = 30 − 2(6) = $18. Check on supply: 6 + 2(6) = 18.
  2. (b) 22 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 16 = 4L and L = 4. W = 22 − 8 = $14. The wage falls $4 and employment falls by 2 thousand.
  3. (c) 38 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 32 = 4L and L = 8. W = 38 − 16 = $22. The wage rises $4 and employment rises by 2 thousand.

Answer: (a) 6 thousand workers at $18. (b) 4 thousand at $14. (c) 8 thousand at $22. A demand shift moves the wage and employment in the same direction.

Example 3: Fall 2025 question 4, with made-up numbers

Domestic soybean demand is P = 20 − Q and domestic supply is P = 2 + Q (Q in millions of bushels, P in dollars). The U.S. exports to China at a world price at which exports are half of what farmers grow.

  1. No trade: 20 − Q = 2 + Q gives Q = 9 and P = $11.
  2. Find the world price. At price P, farmers grow QS = P − 2 and Americans buy QD = 20 − P. Exports are half of supply when QS = 2 × QD: P − 2 = 40 − 2P, so 3P = 42 and Pw = $14. Then QS = 12, QD = 6, exports = 6. The world price is above $11, as it must be for an exported good.
  3. Trade war: China buys nothing, the 12 already planted cannot be stored or destroyed, so all 12 sell at home. The price is read off domestic demand at Q = 12: 20 − 12 = $8. The price falls from $14 to $8, a drop of $6, or about 43%.
  4. Labor market: MR for a soybean farm is the price, so every worker's MRPL = MR × MPL is now 8/14 of what it was. Labor demand shifts in, and soybean workers' wages and employment fall.
  5. Winners and losers (part d): the key said the U.S. lost this round. The government felt it had to pay $12 billion to compensate farmers, the other losers. China's goods are durable manufactured goods that do not spoil, and China reported a record trade surplus, which suggests its goods found new buyers.

Answer: Exports of 6 at Pw = $14, price after the trade war $8, and labor demand for soybean workers shifts in because MR fell. The workers are hurt even though nothing happened to their productivity.

Example 4: A firm with market power

A bakery is the only seller in town and faces demand P = 30 − 0.5Q. With 1, 2, 3, 4 workers it bakes 10, 18, 24, 28 cakes a day. The wage is $100 a day. How many workers does it hire, and how does that compare with VMPL?

  1. MPL: 10, 8, 6, 4.
  2. Price at each output: Q = 10 gives P = $25; Q = 18 gives $21; Q = 24 gives $18; Q = 28 gives $16.
  3. Total revenue: 10 × 25 = $250; 18 × 21 = $378; 24 × 18 = $432; 28 × 16 = $448.
  4. MRPL is the change in total revenue from each worker: $250, $128, $54, $16. (This is MR × MPL: for the 2nd worker, MR averages $128 ÷ 8 = $16 per cake over those 8 cakes.)
  5. VMPL = P × MPL at the new price: 25 × 10 = $250, 21 × 8 = $168, 18 × 6 = $108, 16 × 4 = $64.
  6. Hire while MRPL ≥ $100: workers 1 and 2. Profit check: 2 workers give 378 − 200 = $178; 3 give 432 − 300 = $132; 1 gives 250 − 100 = $150.

Answer: 2 workers. The VMPL column would have said 3 ($108 ≥ $100), but the bakery must cut its price on every cake to sell more, so its MR is below P and its MRPL is below VMPL. The correct demand curve for a firm with market power is MRPL, not P × MPL.

Example 5: A wage floor

Same market as Example 2 (D: W = 30 − 2L, S: W = 6 + 2L). A union negotiates WN = $22 per hour. Then consider a floor of $15 instead.

  1. Quantity demanded at $22: 30 − 2L = 22, so LD = 4 thousand.
  2. Quantity supplied at $22: 6 + 2L = 22, so LS = 8 thousand.
  3. Employment is whatever firms choose, 4 thousand (down from 6). Unemployment is 8 − 4 = 4 thousand.
  4. A floor of $15 is below the $18 equilibrium, so it does not bind: the market still pays $18 and employs 6 thousand.

Answer: At $22: employment 4 thousand, unemployment 4 thousand, and employed workers earn $4 more per hour. At $15: no effect.

Example 6: Immigration with steep versus elastic demand

Supply shifts from W = 6 + 2L to W = 2 + 2L. Compare demand W = 30 − 2L with very elastic demand W = 21 − 0.5L (both pass through the starting point, 6 thousand at $18).

  1. Steep demand: 30 − 2L = 2 + 2L, so L = 7 and W = $16. Wage falls $2.
  2. Elastic demand: 21 − 0.5L = 2 + 2L, so 19 = 2.5L, L = 7.6, and W = 21 − 3.8 = $17.20. Wage falls only $0.80.

Answer: The same inflow of workers lowers the wage by $2 with steep demand but only $0.80 with elastic demand, and employment rises more (to 7.6 thousand instead of 7). This is Card's second explanation for Miami.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Showing the soybean trade war as a shift in labor supply, or shifting both curves.

Correct: Workers did not change; the value of what they produce did. The price (MR) fell, so MRPL = MR × MPL shifts in. Supply stays put.

Mistake: Saying a lower soybean price lowers MPL.

Correct: MPL is physical output per extra worker, and it does not depend on the price. The price change works through MR. MRPL falls because MR falls with MPL unchanged.

Mistake: Treating a change in the wage as a shift in labor demand ("wages rose, so firms demand less labor, so demand shifts left").

Correct: The wage is on the vertical axis. A wage change is a movement along the demand curve. Only changes in MR or MPL shift it.

Mistake: Labeling labor demand only "D" and not saying where it comes from.

Correct: Label it "D = MRPL" and, when you explain a shift, name the piece of MR × MPL that changed. That is what the Fall 2025 key's answer did.

Mistake: Using P × MPL for a firm with market power.

Correct: P × MPL (VMPL) equals MRPL only when MR = P, which is true for competitive firms. With market power MR < P, so MRPL < VMPL (Example 4).

Mistake: Saying the labor demand curve slopes down because firms have market power.

Correct: It slopes down because of diminishing marginal product: more workers share the same capital. His slide says it directly: this "isn't because of market power."

Mistake: Saying a wage rise always makes people work more.

Correct: The substitution effect says work more; the income effect says work less. Which wins is an empirical question. The course assumes substitution wins, so supply slopes up.

Mistake: Concluding from Card's study that more workers never lower wages.

Correct: The theory still predicts a supply shift lowers wages. Card's point is that in Miami the effect was small, probably because labor demand was very elastic and some other migration to Miami did not happen.

Mistake: Drawing a minimum wage below the equilibrium and showing unemployment.

Correct: A floor only matters if it is above the equilibrium wage. Below it, nothing changes.

Mistake: Computing MRPL from total output, for example multiplying the 75 loaves made by 3 workers by the price.

Correct: MRPL uses the marginal product: the change in output from the last worker. With 55 loaves at 2 workers and 75 at 3, the 3rd worker's MPL is 20, so her MRPL at $4 a loaf is $80, not $300.

Mistake: Stopping one worker too early or too late when reading a hiring table.

Correct: Hire every worker whose MRPL is at least the wage, and stop before the first worker whose MRPL is below it. If the 4th worker's MRPL is $75 and the wage is $70, the 4th is hired.

Mistake: Saying a minimum wage creates unemployment only because some workers are laid off.

Correct: Unemployment is quantity supplied minus quantity demanded at the floor. Part of it is lost jobs, and part is people the higher wage pulls into looking for work. In the warehouse practice question, 8 thousand jobs are lost but 24 thousand are unemployed.

Mistake: Saying every worker loses from a union.

Correct: Union members who keep their jobs earn more. The losers are the workers who lose union jobs and the nonunion workers whose wage falls when those workers crowd into the nonunion sector. Total output falls, because MRPL is no longer equal across sectors.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Write MRPL = MR × MPL at the top of the answer box and ask which of the two pieces the story changed. If the story changed neither one, labor demand does not shift.
  • If the story is about how many people want this kind of job (immigration, removals, retirements, a wage subsidy paid to workers), shift labor supply instead.
  • Draw the starting graph before deciding anything: W on the vertical axis, L on the horizontal, an upward S, a downward "D = MRPL," and W1 and L1 marked with dotted lines. Those labels are needed whatever the shift turns out to be.
  • For a production table, add two columns: MPL (the change in output from the row above) and MRPL (price times MPL). Then hire every worker whose MRPL is at least the wage.
  • For a wage floor, first compare it with the equilibrium wage. If it is lower, write that it does not bind and has no effect, and move on.
  • If the numbers will not come out, write the sentence chain: the price or productivity changed, so MRPL changed at every L, so demand shifted, so W and L moved. The Fall 2025 key's full answer to Q4(c) was that chain plus a labeled graph, with no numbers.

"Already planted" or "cannot be stored" means the harvest is fixed, so the price falls along demand. "One-time payment" or "not tied to hiring" means MRPL is unchanged. 4 points Work or play. Maya earns $15 an hour. The marginal utility of her last hour of leisure is 30 utils. The marginal utility per dollar of her spending on goods is 3 utils.

Does a rise in the market wage shift the labor demand curve?

Why did Card study the Mariel Boatlift instead of comparing cities?

Immigrants usually move where labor demand is growing, while the boatlift sent people to Miami for political reasons.

  • Firms demand labor (profit maximization); households supply it (utility maximization). You are the seller here.
  • MPL is the extra output from one more worker. It falls with more workers because of diminishing returns, not market power.
  • MRPL = MR × MPL is the extra revenue from one more worker. For a competitive firm MR = P, so MRPL = VMPL = P × MPL. With market power, MRPL is below VMPL.
  • Hire up to MRPL = W, so the MRPL curve is the labor demand curve. Label it "D = MRPL."
  • Labor supply slopes up because the course assumes the substitution effect beats the income effect. The wage is the price of leisure.
  • Demand shifts out when the output price (MR) or MPL rises, and in when they fall. A wage change is a movement along the curve.
  • Soybean trade war (Fall 2025 Q4(c)): the price collapsed, MR fell, MRPL shifted in, so soybean workers' wages and employment fell. Supply did not move.
  • Technology raises MPL and normally shifts demand out; if the product price falls by more, MRPL can fall. Multiply the two factors to check.
  • Immigration shifts labor supply out: wage down, employment up. Card's Mariel study found no obvious wage effect, likely because demand was very elastic.
  • A union wage or minimum wage above equilibrium is a price floor: higher pay for those employed, lower employment, and unemployment equal to LS − LD.
  • Monopsony (an employer with market power) and union market power are left to ECON 151 and 152, and his slide says monopsony will not be on the exams. The union wage floor graph from Example 3 is still fair game.
