Class 12 · Labor and wages

Labor and wages

Wages are a price, so they come from supply and demand. Firms demand workers up to the point where one more worker brings in as much revenue as she costs, which makes the labor demand curve the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL. On the Fall 2025 final, question 4(c) was worth 4 of the 12 trade points: after China stopped buying U.S. soybeans, you had to draw the soybean workers' labor market and show the whole MRPL curve shifting inward because the price, and so the marginal revenue, of soybeans collapsed.

The ideas, explained

The labor market: you are the seller here

Labor and capital (machines and buildings) are the inputs firms use to make things. The market for an input works like any other market, with one twist that trips people up. In most markets you are the buyer. In the labor market you are the seller: you sell your time to an employer. Edwards' slide calls this the "Tricky part."

  • Firms demand labor. Their demand comes from profit maximization: a firm hires another worker only if that worker adds at least as much to revenue as to cost.
  • Households supply labor. Their supply comes from utility maximization: a person works another hour only if what the wage buys is worth more to her than the hour of free time she gives up.
  • The wage is the price of labor. Supply and demand together set the wage (W) and the number of workers employed (L).

He gives three reasons the labor market matters (Class 12, slide 9). Wages and jobs are central to people's lives. The supply and demand diagram tells you how any new development will change wages and employment. And the same diagram helps explain rising inequality, which is the next unit.

A vocabulary note from slide 7. When economists and reporters say "high-skilled" workers, they usually mean highly credentialed, that is, workers with more formal education such as a college degree. "Low-skilled" workers are those with less formal education. They may have real manual and communication skills; the label is shorthand for schooling, not a judgment about ability.

Marginal product of labor and diminishing returns

The marginal product of labor (MPL) is the extra output produced by one more worker, holding everything else (machines, land, factory space) fixed. You get it from a production table by subtracting: MPL of the 3rd worker = output with 3 workers minus output with 2 workers.

MPL falls as a firm adds workers. This is diminishing returns. The reason is that the extra workers share the same fixed equipment and space. On a farm with one tractor, the first few workers each add a lot; the sixth worker mostly waits for the tractor. Edwards is explicit (slide 14) that the downward slope "isn't because of market power." It comes from adding workers without adding capital.

MRPL = MR × MPL: what one more worker is worth to the firm

A firm does not care about bushels for their own sake; it cares about revenue. The marginal revenue product of labor (MRPL) is the extra revenue generated by one more worker. It has two pieces (slide 11):

  • MPL, the extra output the worker produces, and
  • marginal revenue (MR), the extra revenue from selling one more unit of output.

MRPL = MR × MPL. The extra revenue from one more worker equals the extra units she makes times the extra revenue per unit.

For a perfectly competitive firm, MR equals the market price P, because the firm can sell another unit without lowering its price. So for a competitive firm, MRPL is the price times the MPL, which has its own name (slide 12):

VMPL = P × MPL, the value of the marginal product of labor. For a competitive firm, MRPL = VMPL.

Why MRPL falls as L rises (slide 13): MPL falls because of diminishing returns, and MR is either constant (competitive firm) or falling (a firm with market power, which must cut its price to sell more). A falling number times a constant or falling number is a falling number.

The hiring rule: MRPL = W

Profit is maximized where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. In the labor market, the marginal revenue of a worker is her MRPL and her marginal cost is the wage W. So a firm keeps hiring as long as the next worker's MRPL is at least the wage, and stops where they are equal (slide 15):

Hire up to the point where MRPL = W. If MRPL > W, the next worker adds more revenue than cost, so hire her. If MRPL < W, she costs more than she brings in, so do not.

With a table, the rule means: hire every worker whose MRPL is at least the wage, and stop at the first worker whose MRPL is below it. At $10 a bushel and a wage of $350 a day, the farm above hires 4 workers: the 4th adds $400, more than $350, and the 5th would add only $200.

The MRPL curve is the labor demand curve

Pick any wage. The firm hires the quantity of labor where MRPL equals that wage. So reading across from a wage to the MRPL curve and down gives the number of workers the firm wants. That is exactly what a demand curve does, so the firm's MRPL curve is its labor demand curve (slide 16). A lower wage means more workers hired, which is why it slopes down.

The market labor demand curve, MRPL = D, adds up the hiring of all firms at each wage (slide 17). He writes lowercase (mrpL, d, l) for one firm and uppercase (MRPL, D, L) for the market. On the exam, label the demand curve "D = MRPL" so the grader sees you know where it comes from.

Firms with market power: MRPL is below VMPL

If the firm has market power in its product market, it must lower its price to sell more, so its MR is below the price. Then MRPL = MR × MPL is below VMPL = P × MPL, and the firm hires fewer workers than a competitive firm with the same technology would. The worked example below has the numbers.

Market power on the other side of the labor market is different. When the buyer of labor has market power, it is called monopsony; when workers form a union, the union may have market power as a seller. Edwards leaves both to ECON 151 and 152 and says monopsony "won't be on our exams" (slide 18). Know the words; do not study the models.

Labor supply: leisure has a price, and the price is the wage

Households like goods and services, and they also like leisure, meaning time not spent working in the market (slide 19). Leisure has diminishing marginal utility like any good: the 10th free hour of the day is worth less to you than the 2nd. The price of an hour of leisure is the wage, because that is the income you give up by not working that hour.

So the rational spending rule from the household unit applies (slide 20):

MUleisure ÷ Pleisure = MUgoods and services ÷ Pgoods and services, where Pleisure = W. The last dollar's worth of free time should give the same extra happiness as the last dollar spent on goods.

When the wage rises, leisure gets more expensive, so the left side falls below the right side. Two effects follow, and they pull in opposite directions (slide 21):

  • Substitution effect: leisure now costs more relative to goods, so you substitute away from leisure and work more.
  • Income effect: at a higher wage you are richer, and richer people want more of normal goods, including leisure, so you work less.

Which effect wins is, in his words, "an empirical question!" For this course he assumes the substitution effect dominates, so the labor supply curve slopes up, both for one household and for the market (slide 22). Draw it upward on every exam graph.

What moves the labor supply curve (as opposed to moving along it): the number of people with the relevant skills, such as through immigration or more education, and people's tastes for work versus leisure (Class 13, slide 31 says its position "depends on tastes and on the number of workers with the relevant skills").

Equilibrium: wage and employment together

The market wage W1 and employment L1 are where labor supply crosses labor demand (slide 8). If the wage were higher, more people would want jobs than firms want to fill; if lower, firms could not fill all the jobs they want to. Algebra: set the two equations equal. With D: W = 30 − 2L and S: W = 6 + 2L, 30 − 2L = 6 + 2L gives 24 = 4L, so L = 6 (thousand workers) and W = 30 − 12 = $18.

What shifts labor demand

Because labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, anything that changes MR or MPL at every level of employment shifts the whole curve. Slide 10 lists the demand for the firm's product, the productivity of labor, and the prices of other inputs. His final review deck put it in one line next to the labor market diagram: demand "increases when workers become more productive and/or when the price or marginal revenue of their output rises."

Labor demand shifts out (up) when the output price or MR rises, or when MPL rises (more capital, better technology). It shifts in (down) when the output price or MR falls, or when MPL falls. A change in the wage itself is a move along the curve, never a shift.

Example 1 from his slides: a fall in demand for the product. When the housing bubble burst in 2008, demand for construction services fell (slides 23 to 27). Follow two markets in order:

  1. In the market for construction services, demand shifts left, so the price of construction falls (P1 to P2).
  2. For a construction firm, MR is that price. A lower price lowers MRPL = P × MPL at every level of employment, so the labor demand curve for construction workers shifts left.
  3. Wages and employment of construction workers both fall.

His FRED chart (slide 27) shows employment of hourly construction workers peaking at a little over 6 million around 2006 and falling to below 4.5 million around 2010 and 2011 (read from the chart).

Example 2 from his slides: more capital or better technology. Computers spread across industries in the late 1980s and 1990s (slides 28 to 30). For workers whose jobs use computers, such as architects, engineers, and scientists, more machines or better technology raise MPL. In most cases that raises MRPL, so labor demand shifts out and both wages and employment rise. He shows two kinds of evidence. Autor's chart of real wages of full-time male workers by education (slide 31) shows workers with more than a bachelor's degree pulling far ahead of everyone else since the 1980s. Lindert and Williamson's data on English workers during the Industrial Revolution (slides 33 and 34) show real average earnings of white-collar employees rising from £61.94 in 1755 to £258.88 in 1851 (about 4.2 times), while farm labourers went from £19.01 to £29.04 (about 1.5 times). He marks the first group "Big increases" and the second "Some increases."

The complication (slide 32). Higher productivity also shifts out the supply curve of the product, which lowers the product's price. Since MRPL = MR × MPL, MPL goes up while MR goes down. If the price falls by a lot, MRPL could fall. He calls this possible but "not normal": historically, capital and technology have raised workers' employment and wages, "maybe because product demand is elastic," so the price does not need to fall much to sell the extra output. The quick test is the multiplication. If MPL rises 50% and the price falls 20%, the new MRPL is 1.5 × 0.8 = 1.2 times the old one, up 20%, so labor demand still shifts out.

The soybean trade war: exactly what Q4 asked

The Fall 2025 final ran this chain across two graphs. First the soybean market: before the trade war, the U.S. exported about half its crop to China at a world price Pw above the price that would clear the domestic market. Exports are the horizontal gap between domestic quantity supplied and domestic quantity demanded at Pw. Then China's tariffs halted its purchases. With the crop already planted and no way to store or destroy it, the whole harvest had to be sold at home, so the price fell all the way down the domestic demand curve to the price at which Americans would buy the entire crop.

Then the labor market for soybean workers, part (c). A soybean farm is a price taker, so its MR is the price of soybeans. When the price falls, MR falls, and so MRPL = MR × MPL is lower at every level of employment. Nothing happened to MPL: the workers and tractors are as productive as before. Nothing happened to labor supply either. So the only curve that moves is labor demand, and it shifts inward (down). The new equilibrium has a lower wage and fewer workers.

For full credit on a graph like Q4(c), show both curves before (S and MRPL1), the new demand curve MRPL2, both equilibria, and W1, W2, L1, L2 on the axes. Then one sentence: the price of soybeans, which is the farms' marginal revenue, fell, so MRPL = MR × MPL fell at every L and labor demand shifted inward, lowering wages and employment. The key asked for exactly that. A technical note: since every MRPL value gets multiplied by the same fraction (in the made-up numbers, $8 ÷ $14), the exact new curve is lower by more at small L than at large L. His slides draw demand shifts as parallel, and the key only asks that the whole curve shift inward or downward, so a parallel shift is fine.

What shifts labor supply: immigration

Example 4 in his deck (slides 38 and 39): an increase in immigration of low-skilled workers adds people to the market for low-skilled labor, so labor supply shifts out (right). Along the unchanged demand curve, the wage falls and employment rises. Note what moves: supply, not demand. Immigrants are new sellers of labor.

The reverse shock, fewer workers (for example, if immigrant workers are detained or removed, which the Fall 2025 final's macro story mentioned), shifts labor supply in: the wage rises and employment falls.

Card's Mariel boatlift study

Testing this prediction with data is hard (slide 40). Earlier studies compared wages and the number of immigrants across cities. But immigrants often move to a city because its labor demand is growing, which pushes wages up at the same time the new supply pushes them down. You could find no correlation between immigration and wages even if the supply effect were exactly as the theory says. In his Class 13 version: "flows of immigrants are not randomly applied treatments!"

David Card used a natural experiment (slide 41): the Mariel Boatlift of May to September 1980, when 125,000 Cubans migrated to the U.S. and almost all went to Miami. They came for political reasons, not because Miami's labor market was booming, and there was excellent wage and employment data before and after.

What he found (slides 42 and 43, Card's Tables 3 and 5). The numbers are logarithms of real hourly earnings, so a difference of 0.01 is roughly 1%:

Card's explanations (slide 44): some migration to Miami that would otherwise have happened did not, because of the boatlift; and labor demand may have been quite elastic, because Miami had industries that used low-skilled workers and could expand easily. With a very flat demand curve, a big shift in supply moves the wage only a little and mostly raises employment.

A wage above equilibrium: unions and the minimum wage

Example 3 in his deck (slides 35 to 37): an autoworkers' union negotiates a wage WN above the equilibrium wage. The negotiated wage works like a price floor. Workers who keep their jobs earn more. But a profit-maximizing firm will not pay more than the MRPL of the last worker hired, so it cuts employment back to where MRPL = WN, at LD1 on the demand curve. Meanwhile more people want auto jobs at the higher wage, LS1 on the supply curve. The gap LS1 − LD1 is unemployment among autoworkers.

A legal minimum wage above the equilibrium wage is the same graph (this is the textbook's standard treatment; his Class 13 slides list the minimum wage as one way to raise incomes at the bottom). A floor set below the equilibrium wage does nothing, because the market already pays more.

Worked examples

Example 1: How many workers should the soybean farm hire?

Use the farm table above. Soybeans sell for $10 a bushel in a competitive market and the wage is $350 a day. Then the trade war cuts the price to $5.

  1. MPL from the output column: 100, 80, 60, 40, 20, 10 bushels for workers 1 through 6.
  2. The farm is competitive, so MR = P and MRPL = VMPL = $10 × MPL: $1,000, $800, $600, $400, $200, $100.
  3. Hire every worker with VMPL at least $350. Workers 1 to 4 qualify ($400 ≥ $350); worker 5 does not ($200 < $350). Hire 4.
  4. Check with profit (revenue minus wages, ignoring other costs): 3 workers give 2,400 − 1,050 = $1,350; 4 give 2,800 − 1,400 = $1,400; 5 give 3,000 − 1,750 = $1,250. Four is best.
  5. At $5 a bushel, VMPL is halved: $500, $400, $300, $200, $100, $50. Workers 1 and 2 qualify ($400 ≥ $350); worker 3 does not ($300 < $350). Hire 2.
  6. Check: 2 workers give 180 × $5 − 700 = $200; 3 give 1,200 − 1,050 = $150; 1 gives 500 − 350 = $150. Two is best.

Answer: 4 workers at $10, and 2 workers at $5. The price cut did not change how productive anyone is. It cut the revenue each worker brings in, which is exactly the Q4(c) logic at the level of one farm.

Example 2: Equilibrium, then two demand shifts

Labor demand is W = 30 − 2L and labor supply is W = 6 + 2L, with L in thousands of workers and W in dollars per hour. (a) Find the equilibrium. (b) A fall in the product's price shifts demand to W = 22 − 2L. (c) Starting again from (a), new technology instead shifts demand to W = 38 − 2L.

  1. (a) Set demand equal to supply: 30 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 24 = 4L and L = 6. Then W = 30 − 2(6) = $18. Check on supply: 6 + 2(6) = 18.
  2. (b) 22 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 16 = 4L and L = 4. W = 22 − 8 = $14. The wage falls $4 and employment falls by 2 thousand.
  3. (c) 38 − 2L = 6 + 2L, so 32 = 4L and L = 8. W = 38 − 16 = $22. The wage rises $4 and employment rises by 2 thousand.

Answer: (a) 6 thousand workers at $18. (b) 4 thousand at $14. (c) 8 thousand at $22. A demand shift moves the wage and employment in the same direction.

Example 3: Fall 2025 question 4, with made-up numbers

Domestic soybean demand is P = 20 − Q and domestic supply is P = 2 + Q (Q in millions of bushels, P in dollars). The U.S. exports to China at a world price at which exports are half of what farmers grow.

  1. No trade: 20 − Q = 2 + Q gives Q = 9 and P = $11.
  2. Find the world price. At price P, farmers grow QS = P − 2 and Americans buy QD = 20 − P. Exports are half of supply when QS = 2 × QD: P − 2 = 40 − 2P, so 3P = 42 and Pw = $14. Then QS = 12, QD = 6, exports = 6. The world price is above $11, as it must be for an exported good.
  3. Trade war: China buys nothing, the 12 already planted cannot be stored or destroyed, so all 12 sell at home. The price is read off domestic demand at Q = 12: 20 − 12 = $8. The price falls from $14 to $8, a drop of $6, or about 43%.
  4. Labor market: MR for a soybean farm is the price, so every worker's MRPL = MR × MPL is now 8/14 of what it was. Labor demand shifts in, and soybean workers' wages and employment fall.
  5. Winners and losers (part d): the key said the U.S. lost this round. The government felt it had to pay $12 billion to compensate farmers, the other losers. China's goods are durable manufactured goods that do not spoil, and China reported a record trade surplus, which suggests its goods found new buyers.

Answer: Exports of 6 at Pw = $14, price after the trade war $8, and labor demand for soybean workers shifts in because MR fell. The workers are hurt even though nothing happened to their productivity.

Example 4: A firm with market power

A bakery is the only seller in town and faces demand P = 30 − 0.5Q. With 1, 2, 3, 4 workers it bakes 10, 18, 24, 28 cakes a day. The wage is $100 a day. How many workers does it hire, and how does that compare with VMPL?

  1. MPL: 10, 8, 6, 4.
  2. Price at each output: Q = 10 gives P = $25; Q = 18 gives $21; Q = 24 gives $18; Q = 28 gives $16.
  3. Total revenue: 10 × 25 = $250; 18 × 21 = $378; 24 × 18 = $432; 28 × 16 = $448.
  4. MRPL is the change in total revenue from each worker: $250, $128, $54, $16. (This is MR × MPL: for the 2nd worker, MR averages $128 ÷ 8 = $16 per cake over those 8 cakes.)
  5. VMPL = P × MPL at the new price: 25 × 10 = $250, 21 × 8 = $168, 18 × 6 = $108, 16 × 4 = $64.
  6. Hire while MRPL ≥ $100: workers 1 and 2. Profit check: 2 workers give 378 − 200 = $178; 3 give 432 − 300 = $132; 1 gives 250 − 100 = $150.

Answer: 2 workers. The VMPL column would have said 3 ($108 ≥ $100), but the bakery must cut its price on every cake to sell more, so its MR is below P and its MRPL is below VMPL. The correct demand curve for a firm with market power is MRPL, not P × MPL.

Example 5: A wage floor

Same market as Example 2 (D: W = 30 − 2L, S: W = 6 + 2L). A union negotiates WN = $22 per hour. Then consider a floor of $15 instead.

  1. Quantity demanded at $22: 30 − 2L = 22, so LD = 4 thousand.
  2. Quantity supplied at $22: 6 + 2L = 22, so LS = 8 thousand.
  3. Employment is whatever firms choose, 4 thousand (down from 6). Unemployment is 8 − 4 = 4 thousand.
  4. A floor of $15 is below the $18 equilibrium, so it does not bind: the market still pays $18 and employs 6 thousand.

Answer: At $22: employment 4 thousand, unemployment 4 thousand, and employed workers earn $4 more per hour. At $15: no effect.

Example 6: Immigration with steep versus elastic demand

Supply shifts from W = 6 + 2L to W = 2 + 2L. Compare demand W = 30 − 2L with very elastic demand W = 21 − 0.5L (both pass through the starting point, 6 thousand at $18).

  1. Steep demand: 30 − 2L = 2 + 2L, so L = 7 and W = $16. Wage falls $2.
  2. Elastic demand: 21 − 0.5L = 2 + 2L, so 19 = 2.5L, L = 7.6, and W = 21 − 3.8 = $17.20. Wage falls only $0.80.

Answer: The same inflow of workers lowers the wage by $2 with steep demand but only $0.80 with elastic demand, and employment rises more (to 7.6 thousand instead of 7). This is Card's second explanation for Miami.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Showing the soybean trade war as a shift in labor supply, or shifting both curves.

Correct: Workers did not change; the value of what they produce did. The price (MR) fell, so MRPL = MR × MPL shifts in. Supply stays put.

Mistake: Saying a lower soybean price lowers MPL.

Correct: MPL is physical output per extra worker, and it does not depend on the price. The price change works through MR. MRPL falls because MR falls with MPL unchanged.

Mistake: Treating a change in the wage as a shift in labor demand ("wages rose, so firms demand less labor, so demand shifts left").

Correct: The wage is on the vertical axis. A wage change is a movement along the demand curve. Only changes in MR or MPL shift it.

Mistake: Labeling labor demand only "D" and not saying where it comes from.

Correct: Label it "D = MRPL" and, when you explain a shift, name the piece of MR × MPL that changed. That is what the Fall 2025 key's answer did.

Mistake: Using P × MPL for a firm with market power.

Correct: P × MPL (VMPL) equals MRPL only when MR = P, which is true for competitive firms. With market power MR < P, so MRPL < VMPL (Example 4).

Mistake: Saying the labor demand curve slopes down because firms have market power.

Correct: It slopes down because of diminishing marginal product: more workers share the same capital. His slide says it directly: this "isn't because of market power."

Mistake: Saying a wage rise always makes people work more.

Correct: The substitution effect says work more; the income effect says work less. Which wins is an empirical question. The course assumes substitution wins, so supply slopes up.

Mistake: Concluding from Card's study that more workers never lower wages.

Correct: The theory still predicts a supply shift lowers wages. Card's point is that in Miami the effect was small, probably because labor demand was very elastic and some other migration to Miami did not happen.

Mistake: Drawing a minimum wage below the equilibrium and showing unemployment.

Correct: A floor only matters if it is above the equilibrium wage. Below it, nothing changes.

How it shows up on the final

On the Fall 2025 final, this class was the back half of question 4 ("Oy, Soybeans! So you been exporting?"). The question gave you the formula, "a labor demand curve equal to the marginal revenue product of labor, which is MRPL = MR · MPL," and also described labor supply as "reflecting workers' marginal cost of supplying their labor." Part (c), 4 points, asked you to illustrate the soybean workers' labor market "before and after the trade war escalation," showing "any and all demand and supply curves and equilibrium wages and employment," and to explain briefly. Part (d), 2 points, asked what late-2025 events (a truce, a $12 billion payment to farmers, a record Chinese trade surplus) reveal about winners and losers.

"The trade war escalation significantly reduces the price or marginal revenue of soybeans, and thus it shifts the entire labor demand curve, the marginal revenue product, MRPL = MR · MPL, inward or downward."

Fall 2025 final answer key, question 4(c)

Why it matters: this is the whole grading standard. Name the price drop, say it lowers MR, say MRPL shifts in, and draw it.

His Fall 2025 final review deck had exactly one labor slide, "Lesson 8: Market forces are a fundamental determinant of what workers are paid," with the labor market diagram (D1, MRPL1 and S1, crossing at W1 and L1) and the formula MRPL = MPL ⋅ MR. The same MR × MPL logic showed up in Q2(e) for capital: the price of a unit of capital is the present value of its future marginal revenue products, MRPK = MR × MPK, where MPK is the marginal product of capital (the extra output from one more machine). An expected fall in future labor lowers the future MPK, because each machine has fewer workers to run it, and that lowers the price of capital.

What to expect on this final:

  • A labor market graph inside a longer current-events story (a trade shock, a tariff, AI, immigration enforcement), worth about 4 points, with a 2-point "explain" part.
  • A short answer asking which curve shifts and why, where the answer is a sentence about MR or MPL.
  • A multiple-choice item about what MRPL means, why labor demand slopes down, or the income and substitution effects.

Write the chain in the order the grader reads it: what happened to the product's price (MR) or to productivity (MPL), so MRPL changes at every L, so labor demand shifts, so the wage and employment move. "Less is more" inside the answer box: two sentences and a labeled graph.

Practice questions

Questions 1 to 5 are multiple choice like the final's Q1. The rest are in his long-question style. Do them on paper, and draw every graph with labeled axes, curves, and equilibria.

2 points For a perfectly competitive firm, the labor demand curve is

(i) the marginal product of labor curve
(ii) the value of the marginal product of labor, P × MPL
(iii) the average wage in the industry
(iv) the labor supply curve

Show answer

(ii). Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL. A competitive firm's MR is the price, so MRPL = P × MPL, the value of the marginal product. MPL alone is in units of output, not dollars, so it cannot be compared with a wage.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points A firm's mrpL curve slopes down mainly because

(i) the firm has market power
(ii) adding workers without adding capital or space lowers their marginal product
(iii) workers demand higher wages as more are hired
(iv) the price of the product always rises with output

Show answer

(ii). Diminishing returns: more workers share the same equipment, so each additional worker adds less output. His slide 14 says the slope "isn't because of market power."

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points When the wage rises, the substitution effect and the income effect on hours worked are

(i) substitution: work more; income: work less
(ii) substitution: work less; income: work more
(iii) both: work more
(iv) both: work less

Show answer

(i). A higher wage makes leisure more expensive, so people substitute away from leisure and work more. It also makes them richer, and richer people buy more leisure, so they work less. The course assumes the substitution effect dominates.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points An autoworkers' union negotiates a wage above the equilibrium wage. Compared with equilibrium,

(i) the wage and employment both rise
(ii) the wage of employed workers rises, employment falls, and some autoworkers are unemployed
(iii) labor demand shifts out to match the higher wage
(iv) nothing changes, because firms must pay the wage

Show answer

(ii). The negotiated wage is a price floor. Firms hire only up to where MRPL equals the higher wage, which is fewer workers, while more people want those jobs. The demand curve does not shift; firms move along it.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points David Card's study of the 1980 Mariel Boatlift found that the arrival of about 125,000 Cubans in Miami

(i) cut the wages of low-skilled workers in Miami sharply
(ii) had no obvious effect on the wages of other workers in Miami
(iii) raised wage inequality among non-Cubans in Miami a lot
(iv) could not be studied because there was no data

Show answer

(ii). Wages of Miami's low-wage groups did not fall relative to comparison cities, and the gap between high and low quartiles barely moved (0.84 in 1979, 0.87 in 1985). Card's explanations: other migration to Miami did not happen, and labor demand was probably very elastic.

Full credit: circle (ii).

6 points The bakery table. A competitive bakery produces 0, 40, 75, 105, 130, and 150 loaves a day with 0 to 5 workers. The wage is $85 a day.

(a) (2 points) Loaves sell for $3. Find each worker's MPL and VMPL, and how many workers the bakery hires. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) The price of bread rises to $4. How many workers now? Which curve shifted, and why?

(c) (2 points) Back at $3, a new oven doubles every worker's MPL. How many workers does it hire from these 5? Explain using MRPL = MR × MPL.

Show answer

(a) MPL: 40, 35, 30, 25, 20. VMPL at $3: $120, $105, $90, $75, $60. Hire while VMPL ≥ $85: workers 1, 2, 3 ($90 ≥ $85), but not the 4th ($75 < $85). Hire 3.

Full credit: the MPL and VMPL columns and 3 workers, with the rule VMPL ≥ W.

(b) VMPL at $4: $160, $140, $120, $100, $80. Workers 1 to 4 qualify ($100 ≥ $85), the 5th does not ($80 < $85). Hire 4. Labor demand shifted out, because a higher price is a higher MR, so MRPL = MR × MPL is higher at every number of workers.

Full credit: 4 workers, and the demand (MRPL) curve shifts out because MR rose.

(c) MPL becomes 80, 70, 60, 50, 40, so VMPL at $3 is $240, $210, $180, $150, $120. All are at least $85, so it hires all 5 (and would want more if it could). Higher MPL raises MRPL at every L, so labor demand shifts out.

Full credit: 5 workers, with the reason that MPL rose so MRPL rose.

12 points Nuts to you. Suppose U.S. almond growers export half of what they grow to one large foreign buyer. Domestic demand is P = 10 − Q and domestic supply is P = 1 + Q, with Q in billions of pounds and P in dollars per pound. Suppose that buyer's government suddenly puts a tariff on U.S. almonds so steep that it stops buying them, and no other country steps in.

(a) (4 points) Draw the domestic almond market with exports. Find the world price at which exports are half of domestic production, and show the world price, quantity supplied, quantity demanded, and exports. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) The almonds have already been harvested and cannot be stored or destroyed. What happens to the price? Show it on your graph.

(c) (4 points) Illustrate the labor market for almond orchard workers before and after the tariff. Show all demand and supply curves and the equilibrium wages and employment. Briefly explain, using MRPL = MR × MPL.

(d) (2 points) The government then announces a one-time payment to almond growers, not tied to how many workers they hire. Does this payment shift the labor demand curve for orchard workers? Explain fully.

Show answer

(a) Without trade, 10 − Q = 1 + Q gives Q = 4.5 and P = $5.50. At a price P, growers supply QS = P − 1 and Americans buy QD = 10 − P. Exports are half of production when QS = 2QD: P − 1 = 20 − 2P, so 3P = 21 and Pw = $7. Then QS = 6, QD = 3, and exports = 3 billion pounds. On the graph, draw a horizontal line at $7 above the $5.50 crossing; exports are the horizontal gap between demand (3) and supply (6) at that line.

Full credit: Pw above the domestic equilibrium price, QD and QS marked, exports labeled as the gap, and the $7, 3, 6 numbers.

(b) All 6 billion pounds must be sold at home, so the price is read off domestic demand at Q = 6: P = 10 − 6 = $4. The price falls from $7 to $4, below even the no-trade price of $5.50, because there is now far more on the domestic market than Americans would buy at $5.50.

Full credit: the new price on the demand curve directly above QS = 6, at $4, with a sentence on why.

(c) Draw W on the vertical axis and L on the horizontal, an upward-sloping supply curve S, and a downward-sloping demand curve MRPL1 crossing at W1, L1. Then draw MRPL2 below and to the left of MRPL1, crossing S at a lower W2 and smaller L2. Explanation: growers are price takers, so their MR is the almond price, which fell from $7 to $4. Each worker's MRPL = MR × MPL is now 4/7 of what it was at every level of employment, even though MPL did not change. Labor demand shifts inward, so orchard wages and employment fall. Labor supply does not shift.

Full credit: labeled S and both MRPL curves, both equilibria marked on both axes, and the sentence that the price (MR) fell so MRPL shifted in.

(d) No. Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, and a one-time payment that does not depend on hiring changes neither the price of almonds (MR) nor how much an extra worker produces (MPL). It raises growers' income, but at each wage the value of hiring one more worker is the same, so the demand curve does not move. It helps the growers, not the orchard workers.

Full credit: no shift, because neither MR nor MPL changes; the payment compensates owners without changing the hiring decision.

8 points Code and coders. Suppose AI coding assistants let each experienced software engineer write much more working code per day.

(a) (4 points) Draw the market for experienced software engineers before and after, with all curves and equilibria labeled. Explain which curve moves and why.

(b) (2 points) In the 1980s, word processors replaced many typists. How is that case different from (a), and what happens in the typists' labor market?

(c) (2 points) Suppose the extra code floods the market and the price of software falls by 40%, while engineers' MPL rises by 50%. What happens to MRPL? Show your work.

Show answer

(a) MPL of engineers rises, so MRPL = MR × MPL rises at every level of employment and labor demand shifts out from D1 to D2. Along the unchanged supply curve, the wage rises from W1 to W2 and employment from L1 to L2. This is his Example 2: technology that makes workers more productive raises their labor demand.

Full credit: D shifts right, S fixed, W and L both rise, all four labels on the axes, and "MPL rose so MRPL rose."

(b) In (a) the technology helps engineers do their job, so each engineer produces more. Word processors did the opposite for typists: the machine did much of the typing work itself, so an office needed far fewer typists to get the same documents done, and each additional typist added very little output. The typists' MPL fell, so their MRPL = MR × MPL fell at every level of employment and labor demand for typists shifted in, lowering their wages and the number of typing jobs. Technology raises demand for workers it helps and lowers demand for workers it replaces.

Full credit: the machine replaced typists' work instead of helping it, so their MPL and MRPL fell and labor demand for typists shifted in.

(c) New MRPL = (0.6 × MR) × (1.5 × MPL) = 0.9 × old MRPL. MRPL falls by 10%, so labor demand shifts in slightly. This is his slide 32 complication: if the price falls by enough, higher productivity can lower MRPL. He notes this is not the usual historical outcome, perhaps because product demand is elastic.

Full credit: 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9, a 10% fall, and the conclusion that labor demand shifts in.

8 points Fewer hands in the fields. Suppose immigration enforcement detains and removes many farm workers in a farming region.

(a) (4 points) Draw the market for farm workers before and after. Label all curves, equilibrium wages, and employment. Explain.

(b) (2 points) What happens in the market for the crops these workers pick? Explain the link.

(c) (2 points) Using Card's reasoning about Miami, explain how the size of the wage change depends on the labor demand curve.

Show answer

(a) Fewer people are available to sell farm labor, so labor supply shifts in (left) from S1 to S2. Demand (MRPL) does not change. The new equilibrium has a higher wage W2 and lower employment L2. This is the reverse of his Example 4.

Full credit: supply shifts left, demand fixed, W up and L down, both equilibria labeled.

(b) Labor is a cost of growing crops. Higher wages and fewer workers raise farms' marginal cost, so the supply curve of these crops shifts left: the crop price rises and the quantity sold falls.

Full credit: higher labor cost raises marginal cost, crop supply shifts left, price up, quantity down.

(c) If labor demand is very elastic (flat), a supply shift changes the wage only a little and changes employment a lot; if demand is steep, the wage moves a lot. Card argued Miami's demand for low-skilled labor was elastic because its industries could expand easily, which is why wages barely moved there. In a farm region where crops cannot easily be picked any other way, demand may be steeper, so wages could rise more.

Full credit: elastic demand means small wage change and large employment change, tied to Card's explanation.

6 points Algebra. In a city's market for restaurant workers, labor demand is W = 40 − 3L and labor supply is W = 10 + 2L (L in thousands, W in dollars per hour).

(a) (2 points) Find the equilibrium wage and employment.

(b) (2 points) The city sets a minimum wage of $25. Find employment and unemployment.

(c) (2 points) What if the minimum wage were $20 instead? Explain.

Show answer

(a) 40 − 3L = 10 + 2L, so 30 = 5L, L = 6 thousand and W = $22 (40 − 18 = 22; check 10 + 12 = 22).

Full credit: L = 6 thousand, W = $22, with the equation shown.

(b) Firms hire where MRPL = $25: 40 − 3L = 25, so LD = 5 thousand. Workers who want jobs: 10 + 2L = 25, so LS = 7.5 thousand. Employment is 5 thousand and unemployment is 7.5 − 5 = 2.5 thousand.

Full credit: 5 thousand employed and 2.5 thousand unemployed, with employment read off demand.

(c) $20 is below the $22 equilibrium, so the floor does not bind. The wage stays $22 and employment stays 6 thousand.

Full credit: no effect, because the floor is below equilibrium.

4 points Only bakery in town. Use Example 4's bakery (demand P = 30 − 0.5Q, output 10, 18, 24, 28 with 1 to 4 workers).

(a) (2 points) The wage falls to $50 a day. How many workers does it hire? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Explain in words why this firm's MRPL is below P × MPL.

Show answer

(a) MRPL is $250, $128, $54, $16. Hire while MRPL ≥ $50: workers 1, 2, 3 ($54 ≥ $50), not the 4th ($16). Hire 3. Profit check: 3 workers give 432 − 150 = $282; 4 give 448 − 200 = $248; 2 give 378 − 100 = $278.

Full credit: 3 workers, using MRPL (not VMPL) against the wage.

(b) To sell the extra cakes a new worker bakes, the bakery has to lower its price, and the lower price applies to all its cakes, not only the new ones. So the revenue from the extra cakes (MR) is less than the price, and MRPL = MR × MPL is less than P × MPL.

Full credit: MR < P because the price cut applies to every unit, so MRPL < VMPL.

4 points The housing bust. When the housing bubble burst in 2008, demand for new construction fell.

(a) (2 points) Explain, step by step, how this affected the wages and employment of construction workers.

(b) (2 points) His slides show FRED data on hourly construction employment. Describe what the data show and whether they fit the model.

Show answer

(a) Demand for construction services shifts left, so the price of construction falls. That price is the builders' MR, so MRPL = MR × MPL falls at every level of employment and the labor demand curve for construction workers shifts in. Wages and employment of construction workers both fall.

Full credit: the two-market chain: product price falls, MR falls, MRPL shifts in, W and L fall.

(b) Employment of hourly construction workers peaked at a little over 6 million around 2006 and fell to below 4.5 million around 2010 and 2011, before recovering slowly. That large drop in employment is what an inward shift of labor demand predicts.

Full credit: a sharp fall in construction employment after the mid-2000s peak, consistent with the demand shift.

2 points A chip factory's output price falls 25%, and new equipment raises its workers' MPL by 40%. Does labor demand at the factory shift in or out? Show your work.

Show answer

New MRPL = (0.75 × MR) × (1.4 × MPL) = 1.05 × old MRPL. MRPL rises 5% at every level of employment, so labor demand shifts out, a little.

Full credit: 0.75 × 1.4 = 1.05, so MRPL rises and demand shifts out.

4 points Work or play. Maya earns $15 an hour. The marginal utility of her last hour of leisure is 30 utils. The marginal utility per dollar of her spending on goods is 3 utils.

(a) (2 points) Is she maximizing utility? Should she work more or fewer hours? Explain with the rational spending rule.

(b) (2 points) Her wage rises to $20. Explain why economists cannot say for sure whether she will work more.

Show answer

(a) The price of an hour of leisure is her wage, $15. MU per dollar of leisure = 30 ÷ 15 = 2 utils, less than the 3 utils per dollar from goods. She gets more from a dollar spent on goods than from a dollar's worth of free time, so she should give up some leisure and work more. As she does, leisure becomes scarcer and its marginal utility rises until the two ratios are equal.

Full credit: 30/15 = 2 < 3, so not optimal; work more.

(b) The substitution effect says leisure is now more expensive, so she works more. The income effect says she is richer and wants more leisure, so she works less. Which is bigger is an empirical question. The course assumes the substitution effect wins, so the labor supply curve slopes up.

Full credit: both effects named with their directions, and the point that the net effect is ambiguous.

2 points His slides show Lindert and Williamson's data on average real full-time earnings of English adult male workers. Farm labourers earned £19.01 in 1755 and £29.04 in 1851; white-collar employees earned £61.94 and £258.88. By what factor did each group's earnings grow, and what does the labor demand model say about why?

Show answer

Farm labourers: 29.04 ÷ 19.01 ≈ 1.5 times. White-collar employees: 258.88 ÷ 61.94 ≈ 4.2 times. The Industrial Revolution added machines and technology that raised the MPL of some workers much more than others, so labor demand (MRPL) shifted out much more for white-collar employees. That raised their wages far more and widened the gap.

Full credit: both factors and "technology raised MPL and MRPL more for white-collar workers."

One-minute review

  • Firms demand labor (profit maximization); households supply it (utility maximization). You are the seller here.
  • MPL is the extra output from one more worker. It falls with more workers because of diminishing returns, not market power.
  • MRPL = MR × MPL is the extra revenue from one more worker. For a competitive firm MR = P, so MRPL = VMPL = P × MPL. With market power, MRPL is below VMPL.
  • Hire up to MRPL = W, so the MRPL curve is the labor demand curve. Label it "D = MRPL."
  • Labor supply slopes up because the course assumes the substitution effect beats the income effect. The wage is the price of leisure.
  • Demand shifts out when the output price (MR) or MPL rises, and in when they fall. A wage change is a movement along the curve.
  • Soybean trade war (Fall 2025 Q4(c)): the price collapsed, MR fell, MRPL shifted in, so soybean workers' wages and employment fell. Supply did not move.
  • Technology raises MPL and normally shifts demand out; if the product price falls by more, MRPL can fall. Multiply the two factors to check.
  • Immigration shifts labor supply out: wage down, employment up. Card's Mariel study found no obvious wage effect, likely because demand was very elastic.
  • A union wage or minimum wage above equilibrium is a price floor: higher pay for those employed, lower employment, and unemployment equal to LS − LD.
  • Monopsony (an employer with market power) and union market power are left to ECON 151 and 152, and his slide says monopsony will not be on the exams. The union wage floor graph from Example 3 is still fair game.
