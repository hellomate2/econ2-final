Class 22 · Inflation and the return to potential

Inflation and the return to potential

This unit finishes the short-run story. Inflation barely moves in the short run, but over time it rises when output is above potential and falls when output is below it, and the Fed reacts to inflation by moving the real interest rate, which shifts planned spending until the economy is back at potential. The Fall 2025 final had no separate question on this class, but Lessons 3 to 5 of the six macro lessons in his final review deck are this material (inflation inertia, how policy moves planned spending, and the Fed's reaction function with the "Returning to Potential Output" graph), and the Keynesian cross question (24 of 60 points) used the same multiplier logic you need here.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Tuesday, November 17, 2026.

The ideas, explained

Where Class 22 fits: the long run and the short run

He opens the class by putting the whole second half of the course on one picture. Potential GDP, written Y* (he also writes it as Ȳ, "Y-bar"), is what the economy's labor, capital, and technology can produce when they are all in normal use. That is the long run from Classes 17 and 18. Actual GDP, written Y, is what agents actually spend on in the short run: Y = C + I + G + NX, where C is consumption, I is investment, G is government purchases, and NX is net exports. That is the Keynesian cross from Classes 19 to 21.

The output gap is Y − Y*. When it is negative (a recessionary gap), some workers and machines sit idle, so unemployment is above its normal long-run level. When it is positive (an expansionary gap), the economy is running hot. His FRED chart of real GDP against potential GDP labels the episodes: a "Long lasting recession" after 2008, a "Big, short recession" in 2020, and "Recovery and 'overheating'" in the mid-2000s.

What is new in Class 22 is inflation. His slide 14 adds the labels "more inflation" to the stretches where Y was above Y* and "less inflation" where Y was below it. The rest of the class explains that pattern and what the Federal Reserve (the Fed, the U.S. central bank) does about it.

The Fed's tool: the real interest rate

The Fed is charged with keeping inflation low and stable (his slides use a 2% target) and with full employment. In this course its key policy tool is the real interest rate, r. The Fed directly sets a nominal interest rate, i, the rate written on loans and bonds. The real rate subtracts inflation, π:

r = i − π, which is the same as i = r + π. Because inflation π is fixed in the very short run, choosing i is the same as choosing r. Example: i = 5% and π = 2% means r = 3%.

A higher real interest rate lowers planned aggregate expenditure (PAE, the total spending households, firms, government, and foreigners plan at each level of income) through three channels:

  • Planned investment falls, because borrowing to build a factory or a house costs more. He writes this as IP = a − b r, a downward-sloping line.
  • Consumption falls at every income level, because saving pays more and interest-sensitive purchases like new cars cost more to finance.
  • Net exports fall, because a higher r makes the dollar appreciate (that is Class 23, unit 12).

His conclusion slide: an increase in r reduces PAE at a given Y. In the Keynesian cross, the PAE line shifts down in a parallel way (its slope is still the marginal propensity to consume, mpc), and equilibrium output falls by more than the shift because of the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc).

To keep numbers consistent on this whole page, use one made-up economy: PAE = A + ½Y, where autonomous spending (the part of spending that does not depend on income) is A = 24 − 3r in trillions of dollars, with r in percent. The mpc is ½, so the multiplier is 2 and equilibrium output is Y = 2A. At r = 3%, A = 15 and Y = 30, which we will treat as potential output.

Why a central bank moves rates

His slide 21 gives three reasons a central bank does expansionary or contractionary monetary policy:

  • To offset some other force that is shifting the PAE line. This is countercyclical policy, like cutting rates in the Great Recession or during COVID-19.
  • To pursue another objective, mainly to reduce inflation.
  • By mistake. His example is monetary policy in the Great Depression.

In the Great Depression, his charts show the money stock dropping from about $46 billion to about $35 billion, bank reserves falling, and the real interest rate climbing above 12 percent, while industrial production fell by roughly half. In the cross there are two downward shifts. First, autonomous consumption fell on its own (PAE2). Then the monetary contraction raised r, which pushed PAE down again (PAE3), so output fell far below potential.

Financial crises shift PAE down too

A financial crisis is a time when a number of financial institutions are in danger of failing, people lose confidence in many of them, and lending (financial intermediation) is widely disrupted. His case is Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) in 2023. Many of SVB's assets were long-term U.S. Treasury bonds. A bond's price is the present value of its fixed payments,

Price = PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + ... + FT ÷ (1 + i)T. When market interest rates i rise and the payments F stay fixed, the bond's price falls. Long-term bonds lose more because their payments are discounted over more years.

When rates rose, SVB's bonds lost value. On March 9, depositors including Peter Thiel's Founders Fund pulled their money out in a bank run, because a bank lends out deposits and does not keep all the cash in a vault. SVB was allowed to fail on March 10, fear spread to First Republic Bank (its shares fell sharply), the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) moved to cover all deposits beyond the usual $250,000 per account, and SVB was later bought by other banks at a very low price.

A crisis lowers PAE at every level of Y in three ways: it raises credit spreads (the extra interest that risky loans pay above safe assets), it tightens lending standards so loans are harder to get, and it hurts consumer and business confidence. All three shift the PAE line down, just like a rate hike.

Inflation does not move much in the short run

There are nominal rigidities: prices and wages do not change much in the short run because of limited information, menu costs (the cost of changing posted prices), long-term contracts, and similar factors. He also calls this inflation inertia. In his final review he defined it as prices and wages being unable to "quickly adjust to new optima, especially if the optima are below current prices."

Over time, contracts expire, menus wear out, and uncertainty is resolved, so inflation does respond to the output gap:

When Y > Y* (an expansionary gap), inflation gradually rises. When Y < Y* (a recessionary gap), inflation gradually falls. When Y = Y*, inflation stays the same. The word "gradually" matters: in the short run inflation is fixed.

The reasons, from his slides:

  • When Y > Y*, firms are operating above their comfortable capacity, so they want to raise their prices relative to other firms' prices. They raise prices by more than past inflation, and when many firms do this, inflation rises.
  • When Y < Y*, the same forces work in the opposite direction, so inflation gradually falls.
  • When Y = Y*, firms do not want their prices to rise or fall relative to other firms' prices, so they raise prices just enough to keep up with expected inflation. Past inflation is a main driver of what people expect, so inflation stays where it was. This is where expectations enter the model.

His data slides line up with this. In the first chart (titled 1962 to 1970), output stayed above potential for several years and inflation climbed from under 2% to around 5%. In the second (titled 1979 to 1987), output fell well below potential and inflation dropped from about 9% to about 2% to 3%. In the third (titled 1994 to 1997), the gap stayed close to zero and inflation held near 2%.

Inflation shocks: a different source of inflation

An inflation shock is a sudden jump, usually upward, in a small set of prices (often oil) for reasons unrelated to how Y compares with Y*. Unlike gap-driven inflation, an inflation shock changes inflation immediately. His example is the inflation shock of 1973 to 1974, where his chart of core PCE inflation jumps to above 10%. So there are two kinds of inflation in this model: the slow, demand-driven kind that builds up when the economy runs above potential, and the sudden supply-side kind from a price shock.

Central banks care because keeping inflation reasonably low and stable is part of the legal mandate of almost every central bank, and there is evidence that both sustained very high and sustained very low inflation are harmful (and make people unhappy about the economy).

The Fed's reaction function

When inflation rises, the Fed raises the real interest rate. That lowers planned spending and output, and because inflation responds to output over time, inflation comes back down. When inflation falls, the Fed lowers r and the process runs in reverse. This rule, r up when π is up and r down when π is down, is the Fed's reaction function (RF). Its purpose is to keep inflation from getting too low or too high.

His slide 17 gives a numerical version with a 2% target and a normal real rate of 3%: r − 3 = 0.5 × (π − 2), in percent. Every row of his table follows from it, with i = r + π:

His slide on this graph adds a line in red: the Fed "has no choice about what r is in the long run." In the long run, r* is set by saving and investment (Class 21). His FRED chart for 2002 to 2006 shows the reaction function in the data: the federal funds rate sat near 1% into mid-2004, then rose steadily to above 5% by 2006 as CPI inflation (inflation in the consumer price index) climbed.

How the economy returns to potential on its own

Put the pieces together. Inflation responds to the gap, the Fed responds to inflation, and the Fed's response feeds back into spending. Start in a recession with Y1 < Y*:

  1. After a while, inflation starts to fall, because Y < Y*.
  2. Following its reaction function, the Fed lowers r as inflation falls.
  3. A lower r raises consumption at each Y and raises planned investment, so the PAE line shifts up and Y rises.
  4. As long as Y ≠ Y*, inflation keeps changing, so the Fed keeps moving r and Y keeps moving. The process stops only when the PAE line crosses the 45° line at Y*. That final line is PAELR, the long-run PAE line.

In long-run equilibrium nothing pushes inflation, so π, r, the PAE line, and Y all stay put until a new shock hits. (He notes that Y* is itself growing during this process and the model ignores it: "We can shoot a steadily moving target.")

In a recessionary gap, the self-correcting chain is: Y < Y*, so π falls, so the Fed lowers r, so PAE shifts up, so Y rises, and it repeats until Y = Y*. In an expansionary gap, flip every arrow: π rises, the Fed raises r, PAE shifts down, Y falls back to Y*. Writing this chain in full sentences is most of the credit on a "what happens over time" question.

Lowering inflation on purpose: shifting the reaction function

Suppose the economy is in long-run equilibrium, so inflation is steady, but the Fed decides that inflation is too high and wants it lower in the long run. (His slide says "(suspenseful music plays)" here, and then shows inflation in 1976 to 1979, when core inflation ran about 6% to 7.5%, next to 2021 to 2022, when it climbed from about 3% to above 5%.) Because long-run inflation is where RF crosses r*, the Fed has to shift its reaction function up: at every inflation rate, it now sets a higher r.

What happens, step by step, using his "Steps in Working through This Example" slide:

  • Short run, inflation and r. Inflation does not change (nominal rigidity). The real rate does change, because the Fed now reads r off the higher RF2: in our numbers r jumps from 3% to 4%, and the nominal rate goes from 3 + 4 = 7% to 4 + 4 = 8%.
  • Short run, GDP. The higher r lowers C and I, so the PAE line shifts down. Y falls by more than the downward shift in PAE, because of the multiplier. It is a recession. In our numbers A falls from 15 to 12 (a drop of 3) and Y falls from 30 to 24 (a drop of 6).
  • Return to potential. With Y < Y*, inflation starts to fall. The Fed follows RF2 and lowers r (π = 3% gives r = 3.5% and Y = 27). This continues until Y = Y*.
  • Long run, r and investment. Y is back at Y*. The real rate rose sharply and then fell gradually, so the net effect is not obvious from the path. He says to use the long-run saving and investment diagram instead: nothing about saving or investment demand changed, so r is back at r* = 3% and investment is back where it started.
  • Long run, C and inflation. With Y and r back where they started, consumption is back too. Inflation was falling the whole time Y was below Y*, and Y was never above Y*, so inflation is lower in the long run: 2%, where RF2 crosses r*.

After this example the deck shows a history of the federal funds rate since 1979, with rates near 20% around 1980 and 1981, and a cartoon of Fed chairs starting with Paul Volcker (1979 to 1987).

Movement along the reaction function versus a shift of it

This distinction decides a lot of answers.

  • A movement along the RF happens when inflation changes and the Fed follows its usual rule. An oil shock that pushes π from 2% to 5% moves the Fed up its line from r = 3% to r = 4.5%. Long-run inflation does not change, because the line still crosses r* at 2%.
  • A shift of the RF happens when the Fed changes its rule, for example to get lower long-run inflation. Shifting up lowers long-run inflation. Shifting down would raise it.

The uncomfortable part is in his final review: whether policy is "good" or "bad" is "a little tricky," because inflation typically rises (bad) when GDP is above potential (good). Fighting inflation costs output for a while, and pushing output above potential costs inflation later.

The textbook's version: aggregate demand and inflation adjustment

His slides title the chapter "Aggregate Demand, Aggregate Supply, and Inflation" but draw everything with the Keynesian cross and the reaction function. The textbook (Frank, Bernanke, and coauthors, Chapter 27) puts the same story on one graph with inflation on the vertical axis and output on the horizontal axis. You will not need it if you can do the cross and the RF, but it helps to see that it is the same model.

  • The aggregate demand (AD) curve slopes down: higher inflation makes the Fed raise r, which lowers PAE and output. In our numbers, plugging r = 2 + 0.5π into Y = 48 − 6r gives Y = 36 − 3π.
  • The inflation adjustment (IA) line is horizontal at current inflation, because inflation is fixed in the short run.
  • The long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) line is vertical at Y*.
  • If AD and IA cross left of Y*, inflation falls, so IA shifts down over time until all three meet at Y*. An inflation shock is IA jumping up at once.

Worked examples

Example: reading the reaction function

The Fed follows r − 3 = 0.5(π − 2), in percent. Inflation is 4%. Find the real rate, the nominal rate, and the direction of the output gap.

  1. Inflation is 2 points above target, so r − 3 = 0.5 × 2 = 1, which gives r = 4%.
  2. Nominal rate: i = r + π = 4 + 4 = 8%.
  3. A real rate above the normal 3% lowers PAE, so output dips below potential: the gap turns negative.

Answer: r = 4%, i = 8%, and Y − Y* becomes negative. Check against his table: at π = 3%, r = 3.5%; at π = 5%, r = 4.5%; so π = 4% falls between at 4%.

Example: a rate hike and the multiplier

PAE = A + ½Y with A = 24 − 3r. The Fed raises r from 3% to 3.5%. By how much do PAE and GDP change?

  1. At r = 3: A = 24 − 9 = 15, and Y = A ÷ (1 − ½) = 30.
  2. At r = 3.5: A = 24 − 10.5 = 13.5, and Y = 13.5 ÷ ½ = 27.
  3. PAE fell by 1.5 at every level of Y (a parallel downward shift). GDP fell by 3.
  4. GDP fell twice as much because the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ ½ = 2: lower spending lowers income, which lowers consumption, and so on.

Answer: PAE decreases by $1.5 trillion; GDP decreases by $3 trillion. This is exactly the (g), (h), (i) pattern from the Fall 2025 final's Keynesian cross question.

Example: closing a recessionary gap

Same economy, Y* = 30. A shock leaves r = 4%. Describe the path back to potential with numbers.

  1. At r = 4%: A = 24 − 12 = 12, so Y1 = 24. The gap is 24 − 30 = −6, a recessionary gap.
  2. Inflation does not fall right away (inertia), but after a while it starts to fall because Y < Y*.
  3. The Fed follows its reaction function and lowers r. At r = 3.5%: A = 13.5, Y2 = 27.
  4. Y is still below Y*, so inflation keeps falling and the Fed keeps cutting. At r = 3%: A = 15, Y = 30.
  5. Now Y = Y*, so inflation stops changing and the Fed stops moving r. This is long-run equilibrium.

Answer: The PAE line shifts up in steps (intercept 12, then 13.5, then 15) until it crosses the 45° line at 30. To get there, r has to fall from 4% to 3%, which raises autonomous spending by 3 and output by 6.

Example: the full disinflation, period by period

The economy is in long-run equilibrium with π = 4%, r* = 3%, Y* = 30, under RF1: r = 3 + 0.5(π − 4). The Fed switches to RF2: r = 3 + 0.5(π − 2). Track π, r, i, and Y.

Example: an oil price shock

The economy is at π = 2%, r = 3%, Y = 30 with RF: r = 3 + 0.5(π − 2). An oil shock pushes inflation to 5% overnight. What happens?

  1. An inflation shock changes π immediately: π = 5%.
  2. The Fed moves along its reaction function: r = 3 + 0.5(3) = 4.5%, and i = 4.5 + 5 = 9.5%.
  3. A = 24 − 13.5 = 10.5, so Y = 21. The gap is −9.
  4. With Y < Y*, inflation falls gradually, the Fed lowers r along the same RF, and Y climbs back to 30 with π back at 2%.

Answer: The shock buys a temporary recession (Y falls to 21) but no permanent change in inflation, because the reaction function did not shift.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "Output is above potential, so inflation jumps right away."

Correct: Because of inflation inertia, inflation is fixed in the short run and rises gradually when Y > Y*. Only an inflation shock (like an oil price jump) moves inflation immediately.

Mistake: Treating the nominal and real rate as the same thing, or writing r = i + π.

Correct: r = i − π and i = r + π. With π = 4% and a desired r = 4%, the Fed sets i = 8%.

Mistake: Drawing a rate hike as a change in the slope of PAE, or shifting the 45° line.

Correct: A change in r changes autonomous spending, so PAE shifts parallel (same slope, the mpc) and the 45° line never moves.

Mistake: Saying GDP falls by the same amount PAE fell.

Correct: GDP falls by the multiplier times the PAE shift. A 3 drop in PAE with mpc = ½ cuts GDP by 6. His slide: Y falls "by more than the amount of the downward shift in PAE, because of the multiplier."

Mistake: Calling an oil shock a shift of the reaction function.

Correct: An inflation shock is a movement along the RF (π changes, the Fed follows its rule). The RF shifts only when the Fed changes its rule, for example to lower long-run inflation.

Mistake: "The Fed raised r to fight inflation, so r is permanently higher."

Correct: The Fed has no choice about r in the long run. Long-run r* comes from saving and investment, so r returns to r*. What the Fed chooses with its RF is long-run inflation.

Mistake: "In a recession, inflation falls, so the Fed should raise rates to protect the currency" or any story where the Fed raises r when inflation is falling.

Correct: The reaction function moves r in the same direction as inflation. Falling inflation means a lower r, which shifts PAE up and pulls Y back toward Y*.

Mistake: Saying the economy gets stuck in a recession unless Congress acts.

Correct: In this model the economy returns to Y* on its own through falling inflation and the Fed's response. Fiscal policy can speed things up, but it is not required.

Mistake: Reading the textbook's AD graph as if the vertical axis were the price level, the way OpenStax and Khan Academy draw it.

Correct: In the textbook (and in the AD and IA graph on this page) the vertical axis is the inflation rate π, not the price level. Check the axis label before you read any slope or shift.

Mistake: In the textbook's graph, calling a rise in inflation a shift of AD, or calling a new Fed rule a movement along AD.

Correct: A change in inflation is a movement along AD (it matches a movement along his reaction function). A change in the Fed's rule shifts AD (it matches a shift of his reaction function).

Mistake: "The Fed can keep output above potential for good by keeping rates low," or the same claim about government spending.

Correct: Any gap makes inflation move, and inflation keeps moving until Y = Y*. In the long run policy changes only inflation, not output.

Mistake: Treating a recessionary gap and a downward inflation shock as the same thing.

Correct: A recessionary gap lowers inflation gradually, over many periods. A downward inflation shock (oil prices collapsing, say) lowers inflation at once. In the textbook's graph, the IA line drifts down in the first case and drops in one step in the second.

Mistake: "The economy returns to potential on its own, so stabilization policy is pointless."

Correct: The self-correcting process works slowly, because inflation adjusts gradually. That slowness is the textbook's case for active fiscal and monetary policy in a deep recession.

How it shows up on the final

Facts from Fall 2025: the final had no stand-alone Class 22 question. Its 24-point Q3 was a Keynesian cross problem about 2025 headwinds (tariffs, an AI investment bubble, immigration enforcement) that asked for intercepts, equilibria, the change in PAE versus the change in GDP, the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), and how big a change in G would offset the shock. His final review deck then spent Lessons 3, 4, and 5 on this unit's ideas, with three graphs: a PAE shift in the cross, the Fed's reaction function, and "Returning to Potential Output" with the caption "If something pushes Y above Y*, inflation will eventually rise. This leads the Fed to raise r, which will lower PAE."

So the likely ways this unit appears:

  • A Keynesian cross shock followed by "what happens over time?" Answer with the chain: the gap moves inflation gradually, the Fed moves r along its reaction function, PAE shifts back until it crosses the 45° line at Y*.
  • A "how big a change in policy" part. A $2 trillion drop in PAE is offset by a $2 trillion rise in PAE, whether it comes from G (as on the Fall 2025 key) or from the Fed lowering r. The size of the GDP change is the multiplier times that.
  • A 2-point multiple choice on inflation dynamics (gradual rise when Y > Y*), the Fed's tool (the real interest rate), or what lowers long-run inflation (shifting the reaction function up).
  • A current-events setup like his class openers: tariff rebate checks (a tax cut), a jobs report delayed past a Fed meeting, a bank failure.

When he says "explain fully," name the model step that drives each move: "because of inflation inertia," "following its reaction function," "because the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − mpc)." Inside the box, less is more: the chain in four short sentences beats a paragraph of background.

One-minute review

  • Potential GDP Y* comes from labor, capital, and technology. Actual GDP Y = C + I + G + NX. The output gap is Y − Y*.
  • The Fed's tool is the real interest rate: r = i − π, so i = r + π. Inflation is fixed in the very short run, so setting i sets r.
  • A higher r lowers I, C, and NX, so PAE shifts down in parallel and Y falls by the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) times the shift.
  • Inflation inertia: inflation does not move in the short run. Over time it rises if Y > Y*, falls if Y < Y*, and holds steady if Y = Y* (firms keep up with expected inflation).
  • An inflation shock (often oil) changes inflation immediately, for reasons unrelated to the gap.
  • Reaction function: the Fed raises r when π rises and lowers r when π falls. His example: r − 3 = 0.5(π − 2).
  • Return to potential: Y < Y*, so π falls, so r falls, so PAE shifts up, until PAE crosses the 45° line at Y*. Flip every step for Y > Y*.
  • The Fed has no choice about r in the long run (r* comes from saving and investment). Its reaction function picks long-run inflation: the π where RF crosses r*.
  • To lower long-run inflation, shift the RF up: r jumps, a recession follows, inflation falls, r comes back down, and the economy ends at Y* with r* unchanged and lower inflation.
  • Monetary policy can be countercyclical, aimed at inflation, or a mistake (the Great Depression). Financial crises (SVB, 2023) shift PAE down through credit spreads, tighter lending, and lost confidence.
