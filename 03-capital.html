Class 14 · Capital and Interest

Capital, interest, and present value

Capital is the second big factor input next to labor, and because a machine pays off over many years, its value depends on interest rates through present value. On the Fall 2025 final this was worth 8 points by itself: Q1(c) asked what "capital" means, and Q2(c) to (e) asked what a rising interest rate does to a bond's price, what the price is when the interest rate is zero, and what happens to the price of capital when its future marginal product falls.

The ideas, explained

What economists mean by capital

Physical capital is physical stuff that helps produce other things: machines, buildings, trucks, computers, data centers. Labor and capital are the two main physical inputs to production in a modern economy. Financial capital is the money used to buy, rent, or build physical capital. In everyday speech "capital" can also just mean wealth, and Edwards' slide traces the word back to the Latin for "head" of cattle, one of the earliest forms of wealth.

On the Fall 2025 final, Q1(c) asked what "capital" means, with these options: productive physical assets, financial assets like a bank account or a mutual fund, and accumulated savings that can be invested across international borders. The key answer was all of the above. The word covers both the machine and the money that pays for it.

Two more definitions from the slides. Investment is spending on new capital; it is the flow of new capital into the capital stock, and it is one of the parts of national spending (the I in C + I + G + NX, which stands for consumption plus investment plus government purchases plus net exports, and which you will use in the Keynesian cross unit). The interest rate works as the "price" of capital, because borrowing to buy a machine costs interest, and using your own money costs the interest you could have earned instead.

Renting capital: demand is MRPK = MR × MPK

Start with a firm deciding how many machines to rent for a year. It uses the same marginal logic as hiring workers in the labor unit. One more machine adds output, called the marginal product of capital (MPK). Each extra unit of output brings in marginal revenue (MR), which equals the price of the output for a competitive firm. So one more machine adds revenue equal to its marginal revenue product of capital:

MRPK = MR × MPK. The firm rents machines until MRPK equals the rental price. Because MPK falls as the firm adds more machines (diminishing returns), MRPK falls too, so the demand curve for rental capital slopes down.

The market demand for one type of rental capital adds up all the firms' demands, so it also slopes down. Edwards then assumes the supply of that capital is perfectly inelastic in the short run: there are only so many cranes or excavators in the area today, whatever the rental price. Supply is a vertical line at K1, and the rental price is set where demand crosses it.

The housing bubble: a shift in MR shifts MRPK

Edwards' example links two markets. During the housing bubble, demand for construction services rose. In the construction market that raised both the price and the quantity of construction (from P1, Q1 to P2, Q2). The higher construction price is a higher MR for every builder, so the MRPK of each piece of construction equipment rose. That shifts the demand for rental equipment to the right. With a vertical supply of equipment, the whole effect lands on the rental price: it rises from P1 to P2, and the quantity of equipment stays at K1.

This is the same move as the soybean labor market on the Fall 2025 final, Q4(c), run in reverse. There, the trade war cut the price of soybeans, which cut MR, so labor demand MRPL = MR × MPL shifted in. Whenever the output price changes, the marginal revenue product curve of every input (MRPL for labor, MRPK for capital) shifts in the same direction as the price.

Buying capital: durability and time preference

Many machines are specific to one firm's product, so the firm has to buy them instead of renting. A key feature of capital is that it is durable: the firm pays for the machine today, but the machine produces output and revenue for many years. To compare a price paid today with revenue that arrives later, you need one more idea.

Time preference means people prefer things today to things tomorrow. That is why banks pay interest: to get you to give up money now, they must promise you more money later. Interest is the extra amount you receive above what you put in the bank if you leave it there for a period of time.

Present value of one future payment

Present value (PV), also called present discounted value, is what a payment received in the future is worth to you today. The question it answers: how much would I have to put in the bank today, at interest rate i, to end up with the future payment?

Edwards' first example: what is $100 one year from now worth today at a 3% interest rate? If you deposit x today, after one year you have x × (1 + 0.03). Set that equal to $100: x × 1.03 = 100, so x = 100 ÷ 1.03 = $97.09. At 8% the same $100 is worth 100 ÷ 1.08 = $92.59. For $100 two years away at 3%, the deposit grows twice, x × 1.03 × 1.03 = 100, so x = 100 ÷ 1.032 = $94.26.

PV = F ÷ (1 + i)t, where F is the future payment, i is the interest rate written as a decimal (3% is 0.03), and t is the number of years until the payment arrives. A higher interest rate or a longer wait makes the present value smaller.

Two patterns to remember, both visible in the graph. First, raising the interest rate lowers the present value of a fixed future payment, because a smaller deposit today grows into the same F. Second, the further away the payment is, the smaller its present value, because it is divided by (1 + i) once for every year of waiting. His slide also shows the "math kids" version F × e−it, and says it will not be on exams.

Present value of a stream of payments

Most assets pay more than once. The present value of a stream is the sum of the present values of each payment, each discounted by its own number of years:

PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3 + … + Ft ÷ (1 + i)t. F1 is the payment one year from now, F2 the payment two years from now, and so on. This is exactly the formula printed in Fall 2025 final Q2.

His slide example: $1,000 at the end of each of the next three years at 3%. The three pieces are 1,000 ÷ 1.03 = 970.87, 1,000 ÷ 1.032 = 942.60, and 1,000 ÷ 1.033 = 915.14. The total is $2,828.61. (The Fall 2025 slide prints the first term as 970.09 and the total as $2,827.83. That first term is a typo: 1,000 ÷ 1.03 is 970.87. The other two terms on the slide are right.) The stream pays $3,000 in total, but it is worth less than that today, because every dollar after the first year is worth less than a dollar now.

Bonds: price is the present value of fixed payments

Firms raise financial capital in two ways. They can issue bonds, which means borrowing money in return for a promise to repay later with interest. Or they can issue stocks, which means selling shares of the company; shareholders are then entitled to a share of future profits, paid out as dividends.

A bond's payments F1, F2, F3 are fixed in dollars when the bond is issued. What a buyer will pay for it is its present value at today's interest rate. So the bond price is the PV formula above with the bond's fixed payments plugged in.

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. When the interest rate i rises, every denominator (1 + i)t gets bigger, the fixed payments are worth less today, and the bond's price falls. When i falls, the bond's price rises.

The intuition without algebra: suppose you hold an old bond that pays $100 a year. Then interest rates rise and newly issued bonds pay more. Nobody will pay the old price for your bond when a new one pays more, so the only way to sell yours is at a lower price. The lower price is what makes its return match the new, higher market rate.

The special case i = 0

If the interest rate is zero, then 1 + i = 1, and 1 raised to any power is still 1. Every denominator disappears, and the present value is just the sum of the payments:

At i = 0, PV = F1 + F2 + F3. This was the full answer to Fall 2025 final Q2(d). For any positive interest rate, the PV is smaller than this sum.

The intuition: with no interest, there is no reward for waiting, so a dollar next year is worth the same as a dollar today, and you can just add the dollars up.

The price of a unit of capital

Now put the pieces together. A machine is a stream of future revenue: in each year it adds MRPKt = MRt × MPKt to the firm's revenue. So the machine is worth the present value of that stream:

Value of a machine = MRPK1 ÷ (1 + i) + MRPK2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + … + MRPKt ÷ (1 + i)t, with MRPKt = MRt × MPKt. A profit-maximizing firm buys machines up to the point where this present value equals the price of the machine.

A bond and a machine have the same formula. The difference is that a bond's payments are fixed, while a machine's payments are MRPKs, which depend on future marginal revenue and future marginal product. Fall 2025 final Q2 said exactly this: "A bond's payout Ft is fixed. For a unit of capital, Ft is its marginal revenue product," which it then wrote as MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t). So the price of capital can change for two kinds of reasons: the interest rate changes, or expected future MRPKs change.

The Q2(e) story deserves a sentence of its own. Machines and workers work together, so the marginal product of a machine depends on how much labor is there to use it. If the economy expects less labor in the future (the Fall 2025 final's story pointed to immigration enforcement removing workers), each machine's future MPK falls. Lower future MPK means lower future MRPK, which lowers the present value, which is the price of a unit of capital.

The investment demand curve

Each firm compares the PV of a machine's MRPKs with the machine's price. When the interest rate rises, the PV falls, so some machines that were worth buying no longer are, and firms buy fewer machines. Buying fewer machines raises the MPK (and so the MRPK) of the machines that are bought, which brings PV back up to the price at a new equilibrium. When the interest rate falls, the PV rises and firms buy more machines.

The investment demand curve has the interest rate i on the vertical axis and investment I on the horizontal axis, and it slopes down. A change in the interest rate is a movement along the curve. A change in anything else that affects the PV of future MRPKs, or the price of the machine, shifts the whole curve.

Edwards' two shift examples:

  • An investment tax credit shifts investment demand right. The government pays back part of the purchase price, so the machine is effectively cheaper. At any interest rate, more machines now have a PV of MRPK at least as large as their (lower) effective price, so firms invest more.
  • Pessimism about future MRPK shifts investment demand left. If firms expect lower future output prices or lower future productivity, the PV of every machine falls, so at any interest rate firms invest less.

Stock prices are present values too

The same formula prices a share of stock. A share entitles you to future dividends, so

Stock price = PV(stream of expected future dividends). Stock prices rise when interest rates fall or expected dividends rise, and fall when interest rates rise or expected dividends fall.

The slides give two reasons lower interest rates go with higher stock prices: the usual negative effect of the interest rate on present value, and the fact that lower rates tend to raise expected economic activity, which raises expected profits. His chart of the S&P 500 stock index and the federal funds rate (the Federal Reserve's short-term interest rate) from 2018 to early 2023 shows the stock index falling in 2022 as the Fed raised rates quickly.

Expected dividends can change for one firm or for all firms. The slides show the S&P 500 during the pandemic and the stock prices of Zoom, Carnival Cruise, and Peloton: the pandemic raised expected future profits for some firms (video calls, home exercise) and cut them for others (cruises). The last slide shows the 1920s stock market bubble, with the Dow peaking around September 1929 and then collapsing. A bubble is a price that has run far above any reasonable present value of future dividends.

Real and nominal interest rates, and saving (from the textbook)

The Class 14 slides do not cover these, but the Fall 2025 final Q2 said "the nominal interest rate," so know the distinction. The textbook defines the nominal interest rate as the rate in dollars, the one a bank or bond quotes, and the real interest rate as the nominal rate minus the inflation rate: r = i − π, where π is inflation. The real rate measures how much more purchasing power you get back, not just how many more dollars. Bond payments are fixed in dollars, so you discount them with the nominal rate.

The textbook also ties capital to saving: income that households do not spend. Saving is what supplies the funds (financial capital) that firms borrow to buy physical capital. Time preference is why savers must be paid interest to wait, and a higher interest rate rewards saving more. Keep this as background; the slides and the Fall 2025 key put the weight on present value, bond prices, and the price of capital.

Worked examples

Example: the three PV numbers from his slides

Find the present value of $100 one year from now at 3% and at 8%, and of $100 two years from now at 3%.

  1. One year at 3%: PV = 100 ÷ 1.03 = 97.087, which rounds to $97.09.
  2. One year at 8%: PV = 100 ÷ 1.08 = 92.593, which rounds to $92.59.
  3. Two years at 3%: 1.032 = 1.0609, so PV = 100 ÷ 1.0609 = 94.260, which rounds to $94.26.
  4. Check by growing forward: $97.09 × 1.03 = $100.00, and $94.26 × 1.0609 = $100.00.

Answer: $97.09, $92.59, and $94.26. A higher rate (8% instead of 3%) and a longer wait (two years instead of one) both lower the present value.

Example: a three-year stream, and the slide typo

Find the present value of $1,000 paid at the end of each of the next three years at 3%.

  1. Year 1: 1,000 ÷ 1.03 = 970.87.
  2. Year 2: 1,000 ÷ 1.0609 = 942.60.
  3. Year 3: 1.033 = 1.092727, so 1,000 ÷ 1.092727 = 915.14.
  4. Add: 970.87 + 942.60 + 915.14 = 2,828.61.

Answer: $2,828.61. His slide shows 970.09 for year 1 and a total of $2,827.83; the year 1 term is a typo. If an exam repeats this example, compute it yourself.

Example: one bond at three interest rates

A bond pays $100 at the end of each of the next three years. Find its price at i = 0, i = 5%, and i = 10%.

  1. i = 0: every denominator is 1, so the price is 100 + 100 + 100 = $300.
  2. i = 5%: 100 ÷ 1.05 = 95.2381; 100 ÷ 1.1025 = 90.7029; 100 ÷ 1.157625 = 86.3838. Sum = 272.3248, so $272.32.
  3. i = 10%: 100 ÷ 1.1 = 90.9091; 100 ÷ 1.21 = 82.6446; 100 ÷ 1.331 = 75.1315. Sum = 248.6852, so $248.69. (Carry four decimals before rounding, or the cents can come out one off.)

Answer: $300, $272.32, and $248.69. As the interest rate rises from 0 to 5% to 10%, the price falls, which is the Q2(c) answer in numbers.

Example: a coupon bond priced at exactly its face value

A bond pays $50 at the end of years 1 and 2, and $1,050 at the end of year 3 (a $1,000 loan paying 5% a year). Find its price at 5% and at 10%.

  1. At 5%: 50 ÷ 1.05 = 47.619; 50 ÷ 1.1025 = 45.351; 1,050 ÷ 1.157625 = 907.029. Sum = 999.999, so $1,000.00.
  2. At 10%: 50 ÷ 1.1 = 45.455; 50 ÷ 1.21 = 41.322; 1,050 ÷ 1.331 = 788.881. Sum = 875.658, so $875.66.

Answer: $1,000.00 at 5% and $875.66 at 10%. When the market rate equals the bond's own 5% rate, it sells for exactly the amount lent. When the market rate rises to 10%, the same fixed payments are worth $124.34 less.

Example: should the firm buy the machine?

A bakery can buy an oven for $2,500. The oven lasts three years, then is worthless. Each year it adds 100 loaves (MPK = 100), and each loaf sells for $10 (MR = $10). Should the bakery buy it at an interest rate of 5%? At 10%? What if, at 5%, the oven's MPK were only 90 loaves?

  1. MRPK = MR × MPK = $10 × 100 = $1,000 a year for three years.
  2. At 5%: 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = 952.38; 1,000 ÷ 1.1025 = 907.03; 1,000 ÷ 1.157625 = 863.84. PV = $2,723.25, more than the $2,500 price, so buy.
  3. At 10%: 909.09 + 826.45 + 751.31 = $2,486.85, less than $2,500, so do not buy. The higher interest rate alone flips the decision.
  4. At 5% with MPK = 90: MRPK = $900. PV = 857.14 + 816.33 + 777.45 = $2,450.92, less than $2,500, so do not buy. Lower future MPK alone also flips the decision.
  5. At i = 0 the oven would be worth 1,000 + 1,000 + 1,000 = $3,000.

Answer: Buy at 5% (PV $2,723.25 > $2,500). Do not buy at 10% (PV $2,486.85) or if MPK falls to 90 (PV $2,450.92). This is the investment demand curve in miniature: higher i, or lower expected MRPK, means fewer machines bought.

Example: Fall 2025 final Q2(c) to (e), in full

The exam gave the three-year formula PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3, said a bond's Ft is fixed, and said a unit of capital's Ft is MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

  1. (c) The interest rate rises. The payments are fixed and every denominator grows, so the right-hand side shrinks: the bond's price falls.
  2. (d) i = 0. Then 1 + i = 1, so PV = F1 + F2 + F3.
  3. (e) Capital's future marginal product falls (less labor expected). Then MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t) falls in future years, so the PV, which is the price of a unit of capital, falls.

Answer: falls; F1 + F2 + F3; falls. Each key answer was one or two sentences that named the direction and pointed to the part of the formula that moved.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying a higher interest rate raises a bond's price because "the bond pays more interest."

Correct: An existing bond's payments are fixed. A higher market rate makes those fixed payments worth less today, so its price falls. Price and interest rate move in opposite directions.

Mistake: Writing 3% as 3 in the formula, so 100 ÷ (1 + 3) = 25.

Correct: Use the decimal: 100 ÷ 1.03 = $97.09.

Mistake: Discounting every payment in a stream by (1 + i) once.

Correct: The payment t years away is divided by (1 + i)t. Year 2 gets (1 + i)2, year 3 gets (1 + i)3.

Mistake: Answering Q2(d) with "PV = 0" or "the bond is worthless" when i = 0.

Correct: At i = 0 the denominators are 1, not 0. PV = F1 + F2 + F3, the largest the PV can be for positive i.

Mistake: Treating a machine like a bond with fixed payments, so only the interest rate can change its price.

Correct: A machine pays MRPKt = MRt × MPKt. Its price also moves when expected future output prices (MR) or productivity (MPK) change.

Mistake: Shifting the investment demand curve when the interest rate changes.

Correct: The interest rate is on the vertical axis, so a change in i is a movement along the curve. Tax credits and changes in expected MRPK shift it.

Mistake: Showing a rise in rental demand with a vertical capital supply as a rise in the quantity of capital.

Correct: With perfectly inelastic supply, only the rental price rises; K stays at K1.

Mistake: Picking only "productive physical assets" for what "capital" means.

Correct: On the Fall 2025 final, physical assets, financial assets, and savings invested across borders were all correct: "all of the above."

How it shows up on the final

Q2 short answers (2 points each). The Fall 2025 final printed the three-year PV formula, defined Ft for a bond (fixed) and for capital (MRPK = MR × MPK), and then asked three questions in a row: interest rate up (bond price falls), i = 0 (PV = F1 + F2 + F3), and future MPK down (price of capital falls). Expect the same setup with the direction flipped or a new cause: a lower interest rate, higher future output prices, a new technology, an investment tax credit, or a machine that lasts longer.

Q1 multiple choice. Q1(c) asked what "capital" means. Definitions from this unit (present value, physical versus financial capital, investment) are natural 2-point items.

Links to the long questions. The MRP logic is the same one Q4(c) used for labor: a lower output price lowers MR and shifts labor demand in. Investment I is a piece of planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) in the Keynesian cross, and Q3's story blamed a possible AI data-center bust for lower investment. When i rises, firms buy fewer machines, so I and PAE fall; that is how monetary policy reaches spending later in the course.

Answer in the key's pattern: name the direction (rises or falls), then name which part of the formula changed and why. For example: "The price of capital falls, because lower future MPK lowers future MRPK = MR × MPK, which lowers the present value." The answer box is small and Edwards says less is more.

Practice questions

The first four are 2-point multiple choice like Q1. The rest are Q2-style short answers and multi-part stories. Use a calculator and show the formula you used.

2 points If market interest rates rise, the price of an existing bond with fixed payments

(i) Rises, because new bonds pay more interest
(ii) Falls
(iii) Stays the same, because its payments are fixed
(iv) We cannot tell

Show answer

(ii) Falls. The bond's price is the present value of its fixed payments. A higher interest rate raises every denominator (1 + i)t, so the present value, and the price, falls. Option (iii) is the trap: the payments stay fixed, but what they are worth today does not.

Full credit: circle (ii).

(i) $0
(ii) $94.26
(iii) $100
(iv) $200

(iii) $100. PV = 100 ÷ (1 + 0)2 = 100 ÷ 1 = $100. With no interest there is no reward for waiting, so the future dollar is worth a dollar today. $94.26 is the answer at 3%.

Full credit: circle (iii).

(i) A fall in the interest rate
(ii) An investment tax credit
(iii) Pessimism about the future marginal revenue product of capital
(iv) All of the above

(ii) An investment tax credit. It lowers the effective price of a machine, so more machines are worth buying at every interest rate. A fall in the interest rate raises investment too, but as a movement along the curve, since i is on the vertical axis. Pessimism shifts the curve left.

(c) With i = 0, 1 + i = 1, so PV = F1 + F2 + F3 = 100 + 100 + 100 = $300.

Full credit: $272.32; $248.69 with "price falls because the denominators rise while payments are fixed"; F1 + F2 + F3 = $300.

6 points Fall 2025 final, Q2(c) to (e). Use the same formula as above. A bond's payout Ft is fixed. For a unit of capital, Ft is its marginal revenue product, MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

(a) (2 points) Suppose the interest rate i increases. What happens to the price of this bond?

(b) (2 points) Suppose i = 0. Express the price of this bond in terms of F1, F2, and F3.

(c) (2 points) What happens to the price of a unit of capital when capital's future marginal product falls, for example because of an expected future reduction in labor?

Show answer

(a) The price falls. The payments are fixed, and the right-hand side gets smaller when i rises because every denominator gets bigger.

(b) 1 + i = 1, so p = PV = F1 + F2 + F3.

(c) With less labor to work with, each machine produces less, so future MPK falls, future MRPK = MR × MPK falls, and the present value, which is the price of a unit of capital, falls.

Full credit: modeled on the official key: "falls" with the denominator reason; F1 + F2 + F3; "falls" with the chain lower MPK, lower MRPK, lower PV and price.

8 points The data-center bet. A cloud company is deciding whether to buy an AI server for $5,000. The server lasts three years. Each year it handles 2,000 extra customer jobs (MPK = 2,000), and each job earns $1 (MR = $1). The interest rate is 5%.

(a) (2 points) What is the server's MRPK each year? Explain what it means.

(b) (2 points) What is the present value of the server's MRPKs? Should the company buy it? Show your work.

(c) (2 points) Competition among AI providers pushes the price per job down to $0.80 in all three years. Recompute the PV. Should the company still buy the server? Explain.

(d) (2 points) If firms across the industry share this pessimism about future MRPK, what happens to the investment demand curve? Describe the graph and what it means for investment at today's interest rate.

Show answer

(a) MRPK = MR × MPK = $1 × 2,000 = $2,000 a year. It is the extra revenue the server adds each year.

(b) 2,000 ÷ 1.05 + 2,000 ÷ 1.1025 + 2,000 ÷ 1.157625 = 1,904.76 + 1,814.06 + 1,727.68 = $5,446.50. That is more than the $5,000 price, so buy.

(c) MRPK = $0.80 × 2,000 = $1,600. PV = 1,523.81 + 1,451.25 + 1,382.14 = $4,357.20, less than $5,000, so do not buy. Lower future MR lowers every future MRPK, which lowers the present value below the price.

(d) Investment demand shifts left. Draw i on the vertical axis and I on the horizontal axis, a downward-sloping I1, and a parallel I2 to its left. At the same interest rate, investment falls from I1* to I2*: fewer servers are worth their price. (This is Edwards' "pessimism about future MRPK" slide, and it is the kind of investment drop that Q3 of the Fall 2025 final fed into the Keynesian cross.)

Full credit: $2,000; $5,446.50 and buy; $4,357.20 and do not buy, with "lower MR lowers MRPK and PV"; a left shift of investment demand, with axes labeled i and I, and lower I at the same i.

6 points Tractors and farm labor. A farm is pricing a new harvester that will be used for three years. Farmers expect fewer seasonal workers in the coming years because of stricter immigration enforcement. A harvester needs workers to drive it, haul crops, and run the packing line.

(a) (2 points) What happens to the harvester's future marginal product of capital? Explain.

(b) (2 points) What happens to the price farmers are willing to pay for a harvester today? Explain fully using the present value formula.

(c) (2 points) Now suppose, separately, that crop prices are expected to rise sharply. What happens to the harvester's price? Which part of MRPK changed?

Show answer

(a) It falls. Capital and labor work together, so with fewer workers each harvester gets used less and adds less output.

(b) The price falls. The harvester's value is the PV of future MRPKt = MRt × MPKt. Lower MPK means lower MRPK in each future year, so every numerator in the PV sum shrinks, and the present value, which is what a farmer will pay, falls.

(c) The price rises. Higher crop prices raise MR, so MRPK = MR × MPK rises in each future year, and the PV rises.

Full credit: MPK falls because labor and capital are used together; price falls because lower MRPK lowers PV; price rises in (c) because MR rose.

6 points Build or wait? A bakery can buy an oven for $2,500 that lasts three years and adds $1,000 of revenue (MRPK) each year.

(a) (2 points) Should it buy at 5%? At 10%? Show the PV each time.

(b) (2 points) Congress passes a 10% investment tax credit, so the bakery gets $250 back on the oven. At 10%, should it buy now? Explain.

(c) (2 points) A cheaper oven costs $1,800 but lasts only two years, with the same $1,000 MRPK each year. At 5%, should the bakery buy it?

Show answer

(a) At 5%: 952.38 + 907.03 + 863.84 = $2,723.25 > $2,500, so buy. At 10%: 909.09 + 826.45 + 751.31 = $2,486.85 < $2,500, so do not buy.

(b) The effective price is now $2,500 − $250 = $2,250. The PV at 10% is still $2,486.85, which is now above the effective price, so buy. This is why a tax credit shifts investment demand right: at the same interest rate, more machines pass the test.

(c) PV = 952.38 + 907.03 = $1,859.41 > $1,800, so buy. A shorter life means fewer terms in the sum, but the lower price more than makes up for it here.

Full credit: $2,723.25 buy, $2,486.85 do not; buy after the credit because PV exceeds the $2,250 effective price; $1,859.41 and buy.

6 points Boom in building. A wave of new apartment projects raises the demand for construction services in a city. The supply of rentable cranes in the city is fixed this year.

(a) (2 points) What happens to the price and quantity of construction services? Explain.

(b) (4 points) Draw the rental market for cranes before and after. Label the axes, both demand curves, the supply curve, and the equilibrium rental prices and quantities. Explain what shifted and why.

Show answer

(a) Demand for construction services shifts right along an upward-sloping supply curve, so both the price and quantity rise.

(b) Axes: rental price on the vertical, quantity of cranes K on the horizontal. Supply is a vertical line at K1. Demand is MRPK = MR × MPK and slopes down. The higher construction price is a higher MR for builders, so MRPK rises and crane demand shifts right from D1 to D2. Because supply is vertical, the rental price rises from P1 to P2 and the quantity stays at K1.

Full credit: labeled axes, vertical supply at K1, D1 and D2 = MRPK with D2 to the right, P1 and P2 marked, the quantity unchanged, and the reason "higher output price raises MR, so MRPK rises."

4 points Stocks and the Fed. A company is expected to pay a $6 dividend one year from now, and you expect to be able to sell the share right after that for $100.

(a) (2 points) What is the most you should pay for the share today if the interest rate is 6%? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Explain two separate reasons why a Fed decision to cut interest rates tends to raise stock prices, according to his slides.

Show answer

(a) You receive $6 + $100 = $106 in one year. PV = 106 ÷ 1.06 = $100.00.

(b) First, a lower interest rate raises the present value of any given stream of future dividends, because the denominators shrink. Second, lower rates tend to boost expected economic activity, which raises expected future profits and dividends, the numerators.

Full credit: $100.00 with the PV formula; both reasons, one about the denominator (discounting) and one about expected dividends.

4 points Investment and the interest rate.

(a) (2 points) Draw an investment demand curve. If the interest rate falls from 6% to 3%, show what happens to investment. Is this a shift or a movement along the curve?

(b) (2 points) Explain fully why investment responds to the interest rate this way.

Show answer

(a) Interest rate i on the vertical axis, investment I on the horizontal, a downward-sloping curve labeled I. Mark i1 = 6% with I1, and i2 = 3% with a larger I2. It is a movement along the curve.

(b) A machine's value is the PV of its future MRPKs. A lower interest rate raises that PV, so more machines are worth at least their price, and firms buy more. As they buy more, each machine's MPK falls until PV equals the price again.

Full credit: labeled axes, a downward-sloping curve, larger I at lower i, "movement along," and the PV of MRPK reasoning.

2 points Real versus nominal (textbook). A savings account pays a 6% nominal interest rate, and inflation is 4%. What is the real interest rate, and what does it mean?

Show answer

Real rate = nominal rate − inflation = 6% − 4% = 2%. Your balance grows 6% in dollars, but prices rise 4%, so what your money can buy grows only about 2% a year.

Full credit: 2% and the meaning: growth in purchasing power, not in dollars.

One-minute review

  • Capital means physical assets, financial assets, and savings that fund them (Q1(c): all of the above). Investment is the flow of new capital.
  • Rental demand for capital is MRPK = MR × MPK and slopes down. With vertical supply, a demand shift changes only the rental price.
  • PV = F ÷ (1 + i)t. Use the decimal for i. Higher i or a longer wait means lower PV.
  • A stream: add Ft ÷ (1 + i)t for each year. $1,000 a year for three years at 3% is $2,828.61 (the slide's $2,827.83 has a typo).
  • Bond prices move opposite to interest rates, because the payments are fixed and the denominators grow.
  • At i = 0, PV = F1 + F2 + F3.
  • Price of capital = PV of future MRPKs. It falls when i rises, future MPK falls, or future MR falls, and rises in the opposite cases.
  • Investment demand slopes down in (I, i) space. A change in i moves along it; a tax credit shifts it right; pessimism shifts it left.
  • Stock price = PV of expected dividends: lower rates and better expected profits raise it.
