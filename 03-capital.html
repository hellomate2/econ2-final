Class 14 · Capital and Interest

Capital, interest, and present value

Capital is the second big factor input next to labor, and because a machine pays off over many years, its value depends on interest rates through present value. On the Fall 2025 final this was worth 8 points by itself: Q1(c) asked what "capital" means, and Q2(c) to (e) asked what a rising interest rate does to a bond's price, what the price is when the interest rate is zero, and what happens to the price of capital when its future marginal product falls.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

This unit is about what something that pays you over many years is worth today. A machine, a bond, and a share of stock are all priced the same way: add up what each will pay you, but count every future payment for less the longer you have to wait and the higher the interest rate is. The one thing to remember for the final is that a higher interest rate or smaller expected future payments mean a lower price today, and that with a zero interest rate the price is just the sum of the payments.

What economists mean by capital

Physical capital is physical stuff that helps produce other things: machines, buildings, trucks, computers, data centers. Labor and capital are the two main physical inputs to production in a modern economy. Financial capital is the money used to buy, rent, or build physical capital. In everyday speech "capital" can also just mean wealth. Edwards' slide traces the word back to the Latin for "head" of cattle, one of the earliest forms of wealth.

In plain words: capital is the stuff that helps make other stuff, like machines and buildings, plus the money people use to get that stuff.

On the Fall 2025 final, Q1(c) asked what "capital" means, with these options: productive physical assets, financial assets like a bank account or a mutual fund, and accumulated savings that can be invested across international borders. The key answer was all of the above. The word covers both the machine and the money that pays for it.

Two more definitions from the slides. Investment is spending on new capital. It is the flow of new capital into the capital stock, which is the total amount of capital that already exists. Investment is also one of the parts of national spending: it is the I in C + I + G + NX. That expression stands for consumption plus investment plus government purchases plus net exports, and you will use it in the Keynesian cross unit.

The interest rate works as the "price" of capital. If a firm borrows to buy a machine, it pays interest on the loan. If it uses its own money, it gives up the interest it could have earned by leaving that money in the bank. Either way, the interest rate is the cost of tying money up in a machine.

In plain words: investment means buying new machines and buildings. The interest rate is what it costs you to have your money stuck in them instead of earning interest somewhere else.

Renting capital: demand is MRPK = MR × MPK

Start with a firm deciding how many machines to rent for a year. It uses the same marginal logic as hiring workers in the labor unit. One more machine adds output, called the marginal product of capital (MPK). Each extra unit of output brings in marginal revenue (MR), which equals the price of the output for a competitive firm. So one more machine adds revenue equal to its marginal revenue product of capital:

MRPK = MR × MPK. The firm rents machines until MRPK equals the rental price. Because MPK falls as the firm adds more machines (diminishing returns), MRPK falls too, so the demand curve for rental capital slopes down.

In plain words: a firm keeps renting machines as long as the next machine brings in at least as much money as its rent. Each extra machine adds a little less than the one before, so the higher the rent, the fewer machines the firm wants.

The market demand for one type of rental capital adds up all the firms' demands, so it also slopes down. Edwards then assumes the supply of that capital is perfectly inelastic in the short run. That means there are only so many cranes or excavators in the area today, whatever the rental price. Supply is a vertical line at K1, and the rental price is set where demand crosses it.

In plain words: if the number of machines in town is fixed, a change in demand can only move the rent. The number of machines rented stays the same.

The housing bubble: a shift in MR shifts MRPK

Edwards' example links two markets. During the housing bubble, demand for construction services rose. In the construction market that raised both the price and the quantity of construction (from P1, Q1 to P2, Q2). The higher construction price is a higher MR for every builder, so the MRPK of each piece of construction equipment rose. That shifts the demand for rental equipment to the right. With a vertical supply of equipment, the whole effect lands on the rental price: it rises from P1 to P2, and the quantity of equipment stays at K1.

This is the same move as the soybean labor market on the Fall 2025 final, Q4(c), run in reverse. There, the trade war cut the price of soybeans, which cut MR, so labor demand MRPL = MR × MPL shifted in. Whenever the output price changes, the marginal revenue product curve of every input (MRPL for labor, MRPK for capital) shifts in the same direction as the price.

Buying capital: durability and time preference

Many machines are specific to one firm's product, so the firm has to buy them instead of renting. A key feature of capital is that it is durable: the firm pays for the machine today, but the machine produces output and revenue for many years. To compare a price paid today with revenue that arrives later, you need one more idea.

Time preference means people prefer things today to things tomorrow. That is why banks pay interest: to get you to give up money now, they must promise you more money later. Interest is the extra amount you receive above what you put in the bank if you leave it there for a period of time.

In plain words: a dollar now is worth more to you than a dollar later, so a bank has to pay you something extra to wait.

Present value of one future payment

Present value (PV), also called present discounted value, is what a payment received in the future is worth to you today. The question it answers: how much would I have to put in the bank today, at interest rate i, to end up with the future payment?

Edwards' first example: what is $100 one year from now worth today at a 3% interest rate? If you deposit x today, after one year you have x × (1 + 0.03). Set that equal to $100: x × 1.03 = 100, so x = 100 ÷ 1.03 = $97.09. At 8% the same $100 is worth 100 ÷ 1.08 = $92.59. For $100 two years away at 3%, the deposit grows twice, x × 1.03 × 1.03 = 100, so x = 100 ÷ 1.032 = $94.26.

PV = F ÷ (1 + i)t, where F is the future payment, i is the interest rate written as a decimal (3% is 0.03), and t is the number of years until the payment arrives. A higher interest rate or a longer wait makes the present value smaller.

In plain words: present value asks how much money you would have to put in the bank today to end up with the future payment. The higher the interest rate, or the longer the wait, the less you need to put in today, so the future payment is worth less today.

Two patterns to remember, both visible in the graph. First, raising the interest rate lowers the present value of a fixed future payment, because a smaller deposit today grows into the same F. Second, the further away the payment is, the smaller its present value, because it is divided by (1 + i) once for every year of waiting. His slide also shows the "math kids" version F × e−it, and says it will not be on exams.

Present value of a stream of payments

Most assets pay more than once. The present value of a stream is the sum of the present values of each payment, each discounted by its own number of years:

PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3 + … + Ft ÷ (1 + i)t. F1 is the payment one year from now, F2 the payment two years from now, and so on. This is exactly the formula printed in Fall 2025 final Q2.

In plain words: shrink each payment according to how long you wait for it, then add up the shrunk payments.

His slide example: $1,000 at the end of each of the next three years at 3%. The three pieces are 1,000 ÷ 1.03 = 970.87, 1,000 ÷ 1.032 = 942.60, and 1,000 ÷ 1.033 = 915.14. The total is $2,828.61. (The Fall 2025 slide prints the first term as 970.09 and the total as $2,827.83. That first term is a typo: 1,000 ÷ 1.03 is 970.87. The other two terms on the slide are right.) The stream pays $3,000 in total, but it is worth less than that today, because every dollar after the first year is worth less than a dollar now.

Bonds: price is the present value of fixed payments

Firms raise financial capital in two ways. They can issue bonds, which means borrowing money in return for a promise to repay later with interest. Or they can issue stocks, which means selling shares of the company; shareholders are then entitled to a share of future profits, paid out as dividends.

A bond's payments F1, F2, F3 are fixed in dollars when the bond is issued. What a buyer will pay for it is its present value at today's interest rate. So the bond price is the PV formula above with the bond's fixed payments plugged in.

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. When the interest rate i rises, every denominator (1 + i)t gets bigger, the fixed payments are worth less today, and the bond's price falls. When i falls, the bond's price rises.

In plain words: a bond's payments never change after it is sold. So when interest rates go up, an old bond is worth less, and when interest rates go down, it is worth more.

The intuition without algebra: suppose you hold an old bond that pays $100 a year. Then interest rates rise and newly issued bonds pay more. Nobody will pay the old price for your bond when a new one pays more, so the only way to sell yours is at a lower price. The lower price is what makes its return match the new, higher market rate.

The special case i = 0

If the interest rate is zero, then 1 + i = 1, and 1 raised to any power is still 1. Every denominator disappears, and the present value is just the sum of the payments:

At i = 0, PV = F1 + F2 + F3. This was the full answer to Fall 2025 final Q2(d). For any positive interest rate, the PV is smaller than this sum.

In plain words: with no interest, waiting costs you nothing, so you just add up the payments.

The intuition: with no interest, there is no reward for waiting, so a dollar next year is worth the same as a dollar today, and you can just add the dollars up.

The price of a unit of capital

Now put the pieces together. A machine is a stream of future revenue: in each year it adds MRPKt = MRt × MPKt to the firm's revenue. So the machine is worth the present value of that stream:

Value of a machine = MRPK1 ÷ (1 + i) + MRPK2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + … + MRPKt ÷ (1 + i)t, with MRPKt = MRt × MPKt. A profit-maximizing firm buys machines up to the point where this present value equals the price of the machine.

In plain words: a machine is worth the extra money it will bring in over its life, shrunk for the wait. A firm buys the machine if that amount is at least as big as the price tag.

A bond and a machine have the same formula. The difference is that a bond's payments are fixed, while a machine's payments are MRPKs, which depend on future marginal revenue and future marginal product. Fall 2025 final Q2 said exactly this: "A bond's payout Ft is fixed. For a unit of capital, Ft is its marginal revenue product," which it then wrote as MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t). So the price of capital can change for two kinds of reasons: the interest rate changes, or expected future MRPKs change.

The Q2(e) story deserves a sentence of its own. Machines and workers work together, so the marginal product of a machine depends on how much labor is there to use it. If the economy expects less labor in the future (the Fall 2025 final's story pointed to immigration enforcement removing workers), each machine's future MPK falls. Lower future MPK means lower future MRPK, which lowers the present value, which is the price of a unit of capital.

The investment demand curve

Each firm compares the PV of a machine's MRPKs with the machine's price. When the interest rate rises, the PV falls, so some machines that were worth buying no longer are, and firms buy fewer machines. Buying fewer machines raises the MPK (and so the MRPK) of the machines that are bought, which brings PV back up to the price at a new equilibrium. When the interest rate falls, the PV rises and firms buy more machines.

The investment demand curve has the interest rate i on the vertical axis and investment I on the horizontal axis, and it slopes down. A change in the interest rate is a movement along the curve. A change in anything else that affects the PV of future MRPKs, or the price of the machine, shifts the whole curve.

In plain words: when interest rates fall, firms buy more machines, and when interest rates rise, they buy fewer. Only something other than the interest rate, such as a tax break or gloomier expectations, moves the whole curve.

Edwards' two shift examples:

  • An investment tax credit shifts investment demand right. The government pays back part of the purchase price, so the machine is effectively cheaper. At any interest rate, more machines now have a PV of MRPK at least as large as their (lower) effective price, so firms invest more.
  • Pessimism about future MRPK shifts investment demand left. If firms expect lower future output prices or lower future productivity, the PV of every machine falls, so at any interest rate firms invest less.

Stock prices are present values too

The same formula prices a share of stock. A share entitles you to future dividends, so

Stock price = PV(stream of expected future dividends). Stock prices rise when interest rates fall or expected dividends rise, and fall when interest rates rise or expected dividends fall.

In plain words: a share is worth the profits it will pay you in the future, shrunk for the wait. Better expected profits or lower interest rates push the price up.

The slides give two reasons lower interest rates go with higher stock prices: the usual negative effect of the interest rate on present value, and the fact that lower rates tend to raise expected economic activity, which raises expected profits. His chart of the S&P 500 stock index and the federal funds rate (the Federal Reserve's short-term interest rate) from 2018 to early 2023 shows the stock index falling in 2022 as the Fed raised rates quickly.

Expected dividends can change for one firm or for all firms. The slides show the S&P 500 during the pandemic and the stock prices of Zoom, Carnival Cruise, and Peloton: the pandemic raised expected future profits for some firms (video calls, home exercise) and cut them for others (cruises). The last slide shows the 1920s stock market bubble, with the Dow peaking around September 1929 and then collapsing. A bubble is a price that has run far above any reasonable present value of future dividends.

Real and nominal interest rates, and saving (from the textbook)

The Class 14 slides do not cover these, but the Fall 2025 final Q2 said "the nominal interest rate," so know the distinction. The textbook defines the nominal interest rate as the rate in dollars, the one a bank or bond quotes, and the real interest rate as the nominal rate minus the inflation rate: r = i − π, where π is inflation. The real rate measures how much more purchasing power you get back, not just how many more dollars. Bond payments are fixed in dollars, so you discount them with the nominal rate.

In plain words: the nominal rate counts how many extra dollars you get back. The real rate counts how much extra stuff those dollars can buy once rising prices are taken out.

The textbook also ties capital to saving: income that households do not spend. Saving is what supplies the funds (financial capital) that firms borrow to buy physical capital. Time preference is why savers must be paid interest to wait, and a higher interest rate rewards saving more. Keep this as background; the slides and the Fall 2025 key put the weight on present value, bond prices, and the price of capital.

Worked examples

Example: the three PV numbers from his slides

Find the present value of $100 one year from now at 3% and at 8%, and of $100 two years from now at 3%.

  1. One year at 3%: PV = 100 ÷ 1.03 = 97.087, which rounds to $97.09.
  2. One year at 8%: PV = 100 ÷ 1.08 = 92.593, which rounds to $92.59.
  3. Two years at 3%: 1.032 = 1.0609, so PV = 100 ÷ 1.0609 = 94.260, which rounds to $94.26.
  4. Check by growing forward: $97.09 × 1.03 = $100.00, and $94.26 × 1.0609 = $100.00.

Answer: $97.09, $92.59, and $94.26. A higher rate (8% instead of 3%) and a longer wait (two years instead of one) both lower the present value.

Example: a three-year stream, and the slide typo

Find the present value of $1,000 paid at the end of each of the next three years at 3%.

  1. Year 1: 1,000 ÷ 1.03 = 970.87.
  2. Year 2: 1,000 ÷ 1.0609 = 942.60.
  3. Year 3: 1.033 = 1.092727, so 1,000 ÷ 1.092727 = 915.14.
  4. Add: 970.87 + 942.60 + 915.14 = 2,828.61.

Answer: $2,828.61. His slide shows 970.09 for year 1 and a total of $2,827.83; the year 1 term is a typo. If an exam repeats this example, compute it yourself.

Example: one bond at three interest rates

A bond pays $100 at the end of each of the next three years. Find its price at i = 0, i = 5%, and i = 10%.

  1. i = 0: every denominator is 1, so the price is 100 + 100 + 100 = $300.
  2. i = 5%: 100 ÷ 1.05 = 95.2381; 100 ÷ 1.1025 = 90.7029; 100 ÷ 1.157625 = 86.3838. Sum = 272.3248, so $272.32.
  3. i = 10%: 100 ÷ 1.1 = 90.9091; 100 ÷ 1.21 = 82.6446; 100 ÷ 1.331 = 75.1315. Sum = 248.6852, so $248.69. (Carry four decimals before rounding, or the cents can come out one off.)

Answer: $300, $272.32, and $248.69. As the interest rate rises from 0 to 5% to 10%, the price falls, which is the Q2(c) answer in numbers.

Example: a coupon bond priced at exactly its face value

A bond pays $50 at the end of years 1 and 2, and $1,050 at the end of year 3 (a $1,000 loan paying 5% a year). Find its price at 5% and at 10%.

Two words first. The coupon is the fixed interest payment the bond makes each year, here $50. The face value is the amount the borrower repays at the end, here $1,000. So the last payment is the final $50 coupon plus the $1,000 face value, which is $1,050.

  1. At 5%: 50 ÷ 1.05 = 47.619; 50 ÷ 1.1025 = 45.351; 1,050 ÷ 1.157625 = 907.029. Sum = 999.999, so $1,000.00.
  2. At 10%: 50 ÷ 1.1 = 45.455; 50 ÷ 1.21 = 41.322; 1,050 ÷ 1.331 = 788.881. Sum = 875.658, so $875.66.

Answer: $1,000.00 at 5% and $875.66 at 10%. When the market rate equals the bond's own 5% rate, it sells for exactly the amount lent. When the market rate rises to 10%, the same fixed payments are worth $124.34 less.

Example: should the firm buy the machine?

A bakery can buy an oven for $2,500. The oven lasts three years, then is worthless. Each year it adds 100 loaves (MPK = 100), and each loaf sells for $10 (MR = $10). Should the bakery buy it at an interest rate of 5%? At 10%? What if, at 5%, the oven's MPK were only 90 loaves?

  1. MRPK = MR × MPK = $10 × 100 = $1,000 a year for three years.
  2. At 5%: 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = 952.38; 1,000 ÷ 1.1025 = 907.03; 1,000 ÷ 1.157625 = 863.84. PV = $2,723.25, more than the $2,500 price, so buy.
  3. At 10%: 909.09 + 826.45 + 751.31 = $2,486.85, less than $2,500, so do not buy. The higher interest rate alone flips the decision.
  4. At 5% with MPK = 90: MRPK = $900. PV = 857.14 + 816.33 + 777.45 = $2,450.92, less than $2,500, so do not buy. Lower future MPK alone also flips the decision.
  5. At i = 0 the oven would be worth 1,000 + 1,000 + 1,000 = $3,000.

Answer: Buy at 5% (PV $2,723.25 > $2,500). Do not buy at 10% (PV $2,486.85) or if MPK falls to 90 (PV $2,450.92). This is the investment demand curve in miniature: higher i, or lower expected MRPK, means fewer machines bought.

Example: Fall 2025 final Q2(c) to (e), in full

The exam gave the three-year formula PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3, said a bond's Ft is fixed, and said a unit of capital's Ft is MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

  1. (c) The interest rate rises. The payments are fixed and every denominator grows, so the right-hand side shrinks: the bond's price falls.
  2. (d) i = 0. Then 1 + i = 1, so PV = F1 + F2 + F3.
  3. (e) Capital's future marginal product falls (less labor expected). Then MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t) falls in future years, so the PV, which is the price of a unit of capital, falls.

Answer: falls; F1 + F2 + F3; falls. Each key answer was one or two sentences that named the direction and pointed to the part of the formula that moved.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying a higher interest rate raises a bond's price because "the bond pays more interest."

Correct: An existing bond's payments are fixed. A higher market rate makes those fixed payments worth less today, so its price falls. Price and interest rate move in opposite directions.

Mistake: Writing 3% as 3 in the formula, so 100 ÷ (1 + 3) = 25.

Correct: Use the decimal: 100 ÷ 1.03 = $97.09.

Mistake: Discounting every payment in a stream by (1 + i) once.

Correct: The payment t years away is divided by (1 + i)t. Year 2 gets (1 + i)2, year 3 gets (1 + i)3.

Mistake: Answering Q2(d) with "PV = 0" or "the bond is worthless" when i = 0.

Correct: At i = 0 the denominators are 1, not 0. PV = F1 + F2 + F3, the largest the PV can be for positive i.

Mistake: Treating a machine like a bond with fixed payments, so only the interest rate can change its price.

Correct: A machine pays MRPKt = MRt × MPKt. Its price also moves when expected future output prices (MR) or productivity (MPK) change.

Mistake: Shifting the investment demand curve when the interest rate changes.

Correct: The interest rate is on the vertical axis, so a change in i is a movement along the curve. Tax credits and changes in expected MRPK shift it.

Mistake: Showing a rise in rental demand with a vertical capital supply as a rise in the quantity of capital.

Correct: With perfectly inelastic supply, only the rental price rises; K stays at K1.

Mistake: Picking only "productive physical assets" for what "capital" means.

Correct: On the Fall 2025 final, physical assets, financial assets, and savings invested across borders were all correct: "all of the above."

Mistake: Discounting dollar payments with the real interest rate, or payments measured in today's purchasing power with the nominal rate.

Correct: Match them. A bond's payments are fixed in dollars, so discount them with the nominal rate i. Use the real rate r = i − π only for payments already adjusted for inflation.

Mistake: Counting the purchase of stocks or bonds as investment.

Correct: In macro, investment means new physical capital: equipment, structures, new housing, and additions to inventories. Buying a share or a bond moves existing financial assets from one owner to another. It is financial capital, and it can fund investment, but it is not investment itself.

Mistake: Mixing up flows and stocks, for example calling a household's savings account balance its "saving."

Correct: Saving is a flow: income not spent during a year. Wealth is a stock: everything owned at a point in time. Saving adds to wealth each year.

Mistake: Getting the sign of public saving wrong when the government runs a deficit.

Correct: Public saving = T − G. If the government collects 3,500 and spends 4,000, public saving is −500, and national saving is private saving minus 500.

Mistake: Thinking a machine that is exactly as productive as before is still just as worth buying after interest rates rise.

Correct: A higher i lowers the PV of the same MRPKs, because the money could earn more elsewhere. The machine has not changed, but its opportunity cost has, so fewer machines pass the test and investment falls.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Write the formula first: PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3. Then circle the piece the question changed. Most Q2-style answers are one sentence about that piece.
  • Sort the change. If the interest rate moved, it is a denominator story, and the price moves the opposite way. If MR, MPK, the output price, the amount of labor, or technology moved, it is a numerator story for capital, and the price moves the same way. A bond's numerators never move.
  • For any curve question, look at the axes. If the thing that changed is on an axis (the interest rate on the investment demand graph), it is a movement along the curve. Anything else shifts the curve.
  • If you forget which way a bond moves, tell yourself the story: new bonds now pay more, so nobody pays full price for an old bond. The old bond's price falls.
  • Sanity-check every number. With a positive interest rate, the PV must be less than the plain sum of the payments, and a payment far in the future must be worth less today than a payment of the same size that comes sooner. If not, check that you wrote 5% as 0.05 and used the right power of (1 + i).
  • If i = 0, stop calculating and add the payments.

How it shows up on the final

Q2 short answers (2 points each). The Fall 2025 final printed the three-year PV formula, defined Ft for a bond (fixed) and for capital (MRPK = MR × MPK), and then asked three questions in a row: interest rate up (bond price falls), i = 0 (PV = F1 + F2 + F3), and future MPK down (price of capital falls). Expect the same setup with the direction flipped or a new cause: a lower interest rate, higher future output prices, a new technology, an investment tax credit, or a machine that lasts longer.

Q1 multiple choice. Q1(c) asked what "capital" means. Definitions from this unit (present value, physical versus financial capital, investment) are natural 2-point items.

Links to the long questions. The MRP logic is the same one Q4(c) used for labor: a lower output price lowers MR and shifts labor demand in. Investment I is a piece of planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) in the Keynesian cross, and Q3's story blamed a possible AI data-center bust for lower investment. When i rises, firms buy fewer machines, so I and PAE fall; that is how monetary policy reaches spending later in the course.

Answer in the key's pattern: name the direction (rises or falls), then name which part of the formula changed and why. For example: "The price of capital falls, because lower future MPK lowers future MRPK = MR × MPK, which lowers the present value." The answer box is small and Edwards says less is more.

Words that change the answer: "existing" or "old" bond means its payments are fixed, so a higher rate lowers its price. "Shift" and "movement along" matter: a change in i is a movement along investment demand, while everything else shifts it. "Future" or "expected" means the change hits the numerators of the PV sum, even if nothing has changed today. "Rental" means the vertical-supply graph, and "purchase" or "buy" means present value. "Price of a unit of capital" in Q2(e) means what the machine is worth, its PV. "Price of new capital goods" in Problem Set 3 means the sticker price the firm pays, and a higher sticker price shifts investment demand left. "Nominal" rate means dollars, and "real" rate means purchasing power. "Forever" means you can use the shortcut PV = F ÷ i. "End of each year" means the first payment is divided by (1 + i) once, not zero times.

Show answer

Saving rises. The new products raise the marginal utility of spending in the future. To keep the marginal utility per dollar equal across time (the rational spending rule), the household cuts spending now, which means saving more, and moves that spending to the future when the new products arrive.

Full credit: saving rises, because future spending is now worth more at the margin than spending today.

6 points A machine that never wears out. A machine costs $40,000. It adds $5,000 a year to the firm's revenue (its MRPK) and costs $1,000 a year to run, so it adds $4,000 a year net, forever. Adapted from the textbook (the decision to buy capital)

(a) (2 points) Should the firm buy it at i = 8%? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) At i = 12%?

(c) (2 points) What is the highest interest rate at which buying is worthwhile? Explain why a higher interest rate is a reason not to buy, even though the machine is exactly as productive as before.

Show answer

(a) The net payment lasts forever, so PV = 4,000 ÷ 0.08 = $50,000, more than the $40,000 price. Buy. The textbook checks it another way: the yearly cost of owning the machine is the interest given up on $40,000 plus the running cost, 0.08 × 40,000 + 1,000 = $4,200, which is less than the $5,000 it brings in.

Full credit: $50,000 > $40,000 (or $4,200 < $5,000), so buy.

(b) PV = 4,000 ÷ 0.12 = $33,333.33, less than $40,000. Do not buy. (Yearly cost: 0.12 × 40,000 + 1,000 = $5,800, more than $5,000.)

Full credit: $33,333.33 < $40,000, so do not buy.

(c) Buying is worthwhile while 4,000 ÷ i ≥ 40,000, so up to i = 10%. The $40,000 could instead sit in the bank earning i. A higher i means the firm gives up more by tying the money up in the machine, which is the same as saying the machine's future payments are worth less today.

Full credit: 10%, and a higher i raises the opportunity cost of the money (lowers the PV of the machine's payments).

4 points Who saves? An economy has output Y = 20,000, consumption C = 13,000, government purchases G = 4,000, and net taxes T = 3,500. Adapted from the textbook (saving chapter)

(a) (2 points) Find private saving (Y − T − C) and public saving (T − G).

(b) (2 points) Find national saving two ways. What does a negative public saving number mean?

Show answer

(a) Private saving = 20,000 − 3,500 − 13,000 = 3,500. Public saving = 3,500 − 4,000 = −500.

Full credit: 3,500 and −500.

(b) National saving = private + public = 3,500 − 500 = 3,000, and also Y − C − G = 20,000 − 13,000 − 4,000 = 3,000. Negative public saving means the government spends more than it collects: a budget deficit of 500, which uses up part of private saving.

Full credit: 3,000 both ways, and negative public saving is a deficit.

2 points A stock is expected to pay a $4 dividend and sell for $104 one year from now. The safe interest rate is 3%, and because the stock is risky, buyers want an extra 1% (a risk premium). What should it sell for today? Adapted from the textbook (stock prices)

Show answer

Discount the $4 + $104 = $108 received in a year at the safe rate plus the risk premium: 108 ÷ (1 + 0.03 + 0.01) = 108 ÷ 1.04 = $103.85. A higher risk premium works like a higher interest rate: it lowers the price today.

Full credit: $103.85, discounting at 4%.

2 points The price of existing 10-year Treasury bonds fell sharply this month. The most likely cause is that

(i) Market interest rates fell
(ii) Market interest rates rose
(iii) The government cut the bonds' fixed payments
(iv) We cannot learn anything about interest rates from bond prices

Show answer

(ii) Market interest rates rose. This is Fall 2025 Q2(c) run backward. A bond's price is the present value of its fixed payments at today's interest rate. A higher i makes every denominator (1 + i)t larger, so the price falls. Option (i) has the direction backward: lower rates raise bond prices. Option (iii) is wrong because a bond's payments are fixed when it is issued; the price can fall with no change in the payments. Option (iv) is wrong because price and rate are tied together by the PV formula, so a falling price tells you the rate rose.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points In this course, which of these counts as investment? Based on Fall 2025 slides, Class 14, slide 5

(i) Buying 100 shares of Apple stock
(ii) A firm building a new data center
(iii) A household putting $5,000 in a savings account
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(ii) A firm building a new data center. His slide says "Investment is the flow of new capital into the capital stock." A new data center is new physical capital. Option (i) is wrong because buying shares moves an existing financial asset from one owner to another; no new machine or building is created. Option (iii) is saving. It supplies financial capital that someone may later borrow to invest, but the deposit itself is not investment. Option (iv) is wrong because (i) and (iii) are wrong.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points New tariffs raise the price of imported machine tools and other new capital goods. The expected MRPKs of the machines do not change. At any given interest rate, the investment demand curve Adapted from Edwards' Problem Set 3, Q4(a)

(i) Shifts right, because machines are now worth more
(ii) Shifts left
(iii) Does not shift; investment moves along the curve
(iv) Does not change, because MRPK did not change

Show answer

(ii) Shifts left. His shift slides use the rule PV(stream of MRPKs) = purchase price (Class 14, slides 32 to 35). The left side did not change, but the purchase price rose. So at every interest rate, fewer machines have a PV at least as large as their price, and firms buy fewer. This is the opposite of an investment tax credit, which lowers the effective price and shifts the curve right. Option (i) is wrong because the machines earn the same MRPK, so they are not worth more; they just cost more. Option (iii) is wrong because only a change in the interest rate, which is on the vertical axis, is a movement along the curve. Option (iv) is wrong because the price side of the comparison changed even though MRPK did not.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points Which of these can change the price of a unit of capital but cannot change the price of a bond?

(i) A rise in the interest rate
(ii) A fall in the expected future price of the good the machine produces
(iii) The interest rate falling to zero
(iv) None of the above; bond prices and capital prices always move together

Show answer

(ii). Both prices use the same PV formula, but the payments differ. A bond's payments Ft are fixed. A machine's payments are MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t). A lower expected output price lowers future MR, so it lowers every future MRPK and the machine's price. The bond's fixed payments are unaffected. Option (i) is wrong because a higher i raises the denominators for both, so both prices fall. Option (iii) is wrong because i = 0 changes the denominators for both, turning each price into the plain sum of its payments. Option (iv) is wrong because (ii) moves only the price of capital.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points Explain why $100 received one year from now is worth less to you than $100 today. Use the ideas of time preference and present value, then find what the $100 is worth today if the interest rate is 4%. Based on Fall 2025 slides, Class 14, slides 18 and 19

Show answer

People prefer things today to things tomorrow (time preference), which is why banks must pay interest to get you to wait. Present value is "what a payment to be received in the future is worth to you today." Because money in the bank grows, you need less than $100 today to end up with $100 in a year. At 4%: PV = 100 ÷ 1.04 = $96.15. Check by growing it forward: 96.1538 × 1.04 = $100.00.

Full credit: time preference (or "you could earn interest on it"), his definition of PV, and $96.15.

6 points Working backward from the price. A one-year bond pays $1,050 one year from now and nothing else.

(a) (2 points) It sells today for $1,000. What interest rate does that price imply? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) A week later its price falls to $980. What interest rate does the new price imply? Did interest rates rise or fall?

(c) (2 points) Suppose the change in (b) happened to interest rates across the whole economy. What happens to planned investment and to planned aggregate expenditure (PAE)? Explain.

Show answer

(a) The price is the present value: 1,000 = 1,050 ÷ (1 + i). Multiply both sides by (1 + i) and divide by 1,000: 1 + i = 1,050 ÷ 1,000 = 1.05. So i = 5%. Check: 1,050 ÷ 1.05 = $1,000.

Full credit: 5%, with the formula solved for i.

(b) 1 + i = 1,050 ÷ 980 = 1.0714, so i ≈ 7.14%. Interest rates rose. Another way to see it: a buyer who pays $980 and gets $1,050 earns $70, and 70 ÷ 980 = 7.14%. A lower price for the same fixed payment means a higher return.

Full credit: about 7.14%, and "rates rose."

(c) Planned investment falls, and PAE falls. A higher interest rate lowers the present value of every machine's future MRPKs, so fewer machines are worth their price and firms buy fewer (Class 14, slide 30). This is a movement up along the investment demand curve. Lower I lowers PAE = C + I + G + NX at every level of income, so the PAE line in the Keynesian cross shifts down, and short-run output falls.

Full credit: I falls because the PV of MRPK falls; PAE falls (the line shifts down).

4 points Short bonds and long bonds. A bank holds two U.S. Treasury bonds. Bond A pays $1,000 one year from now. Bond B pays $1,000 ten years from now. Neither pays anything else. Based on Fall 2025 slides, Class 22, slide 28 (Silicon Valley Bank)

(a) (2 points) Find the price of each bond when i = 2% and when i = 5%. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Which bond lost the larger share of its value when rates rose? Explain why, and connect it to Silicon Valley Bank in 2023.

Show answer

(a) Bond A at 2%: 1,000 ÷ 1.02 = $980.39. At 5%: 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = $952.38. Bond B at 2%: 1.0210 = 1.21899, so 1,000 ÷ 1.21899 = $820.35. At 5%: 1.0510 = 1.62889, so 1,000 ÷ 1.62889 = $613.91.

Full credit: $980.39 and $952.38; $820.35 and $613.91.

(b) Bond A lost 980.39 − 952.38 = $28.01, which is 28.01 ÷ 980.39 = 2.86% of its value. Bond B lost 820.35 − 613.91 = $206.44, which is 206.44 ÷ 820.35 = 25.16%. The ten-year bond lost far more, because its payment is divided by (1 + i) ten times, so a rise in i is compounded ten times. His slide uses this to explain Silicon Valley Bank: many of its assets were long-term Treasury bonds, and when interest rates rose, the market price of those bonds fell ("this is the concept of 'present value' at work").

Full credit: about 2.86% versus 25.16%, the long bond loses more because its payment is discounted over more years, and SVB held long-term bonds whose prices fell when rates rose.

4 points Downtime rules. New safety regulations require more downtime, so each machine now produces less output than before. Assume demand for the product is elastic enough that firms' marginal revenue barely changes. Adapted from Edwards' Problem Set 3, Q4(b)

(a) (2 points) What happens to the MRPK of a machine and to the investment demand curve? Describe the graph you would draw.

(b) (2 points) At the same time, the Fed lowers the interest rate. How does this show up on the same graph, and can you tell for sure whether investment rises or falls?

Show answer

(a) Each machine produces less, so MPK falls. With MR about the same, MRPK = MR × MPK falls. The PV of a machine's MRPKs falls at every interest rate, so fewer machines are worth their price, and investment demand shifts left. The graph: interest rate i on the vertical axis, investment I on the horizontal axis, a downward-sloping curve I1 and a second curve I2 to its left. At the same interest rate, investment falls from I1* to I2*.

Full credit: lower MPK lowers MRPK, a left shift, labeled axes, and less investment at the same i.

(b) The lower interest rate is a movement down along the new curve I2, because i is on the vertical axis. The two forces push investment in opposite directions: the shift lowers it and the lower rate raises it. Without knowing how big each change is, you cannot tell whether investment ends up higher or lower than at the start.

Full credit: a movement along the shifted curve, and "cannot tell without the sizes."

4 points Harvesters for rent. In a farm county, the number of harvesters available to rent this season is fixed.

(a) (2 points) Farmers lose a big export market, so the price of their crop falls. Describe the harvester rental market before and after: the axes, the curves, and what happens to the rental price and the number of harvesters rented.

(b) (2 points) Separately, an equipment dealer brings 50 extra harvesters into the county to rent. Which curve moves this time, and what happens to the rental price and quantity?

Show answer

(a) Axes: rental price on the vertical, quantity of harvesters K on the horizontal. Supply is a vertical line at K1. Demand is MRPK = MR × MPK and slopes down. A lower crop price is a lower MR for every farmer, so MRPK falls and demand shifts left from D1 to D2. Because supply is vertical, the rental price falls from P1 to P2, and the quantity stays at K1. This is the housing-bubble graph in reverse, and the same move as the soybean labor market on Fall 2025 Q4(c).

Full credit: labeled axes, vertical supply, demand shifts left because lower MR lowers MRPK, a lower rental price, and an unchanged quantity.

(b) This time supply moves: the vertical line shifts right from K1 to K2 = K1 + 50. Demand does not move. The new equilibrium is further down the same MRPK curve, so the rental price falls and the quantity rented rises. The extra harvesters add less than the earlier ones (diminishing MPK), so farmers will only rent them at a lower price.

Full credit: supply shifts right, the rental price falls, and the quantity rises along the same demand curve.

One-minute review

Quick check

Interest rates fall. What happens to the price of an existing bond?

It rises, because its fixed payments are divided by smaller (1 + i)t terms.

At i = 0, what is a bond paying $50, $50, and $1,050 worth?

$1,150, because every denominator is 1, so you just add the payments.

The interest rate falls. Is that a shift of investment demand or a movement along it?

A movement along it, because the interest rate is on the vertical axis.

Expected future MPK of capital falls. What happens to the price of a unit of capital?

It falls, because lower MPK lowers future MRPK = MR × MPK, which lowers the present value.

Rental demand for cranes rises and the supply of cranes is fixed. What changes?

Only the rental price rises; the quantity stays at K1 because supply is vertical.

Rates rise. Which loses more value, a one-year bond or a ten-year bond?

The ten-year bond, because its payment is divided by (1 + i) ten times instead of once.

  • Capital means physical assets, financial assets, and savings that fund them (Q1(c): all of the above). Investment is the flow of new capital.
  • Rental demand for capital is MRPK = MR × MPK and slopes down. With vertical supply, a demand shift changes only the rental price.
  • PV = F ÷ (1 + i)t. Use the decimal for i. Higher i or a longer wait means lower PV.
  • A stream: add Ft ÷ (1 + i)t for each year. $1,000 a year for three years at 3% is $2,828.61 (the slide's $2,827.83 has a typo).
  • Bond prices move opposite to interest rates, because the payments are fixed and the denominators grow.
  • At i = 0, PV = F1 + F2 + F3.
  • Price of capital = PV of future MRPKs. It falls when i rises, future MPK falls, or future MR falls, and rises in the opposite cases.
  • Investment demand slopes down in (I, i) space. A change in i moves along it; a tax credit shifts it right; pessimism shifts it left.
  • Stock price = PV of expected dividends: lower rates and better expected profits raise it.
