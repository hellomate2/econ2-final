Class 15 · International Trade

Gains from international trade

This unit takes comparative advantage from the midterm and applies it to whole countries: Edwards' U.S. and China example with soybeans and washing machines, the terms of trade (the world relative price), consumption possibilities curves that lie outside the production possibilities curves, and specialization when the production possibilities curve (PPC) is curved. On the Fall 2025 final it showed up as Q1(b), a 2-point multiple choice on what comparative advantage implies, and as the background for Q4, the soybean trade war, where part (d) asked who won and who lost.

The ideas, explained

Why trade, and what this unit adds to the midterm

Edwards' first chart shows that U.S. exports plus imports have risen as a share of national income (GDP, gross domestic product) since 1929. To explain why countries trade, economists use the same tool you used for Bill and Chris on the island: comparative advantage. What is new here is the price at which countries trade. On the island the trading price was a number of fish per coconut. Between countries it is the terms of trade, a world relative price, and it decides whether both countries want to trade and how the gains are split. His own warning on the learning objectives slide: numerators and denominators get confusing here, so set up every ratio carefully.

Comparative advantage between countries

A country has a comparative advantage in a good if it is the low-opportunity-cost producer of that good. Absolute advantage means producing more of a good per worker. As on the island, comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, decides who should specialize in what.

The opportunity cost of one unit of a good is what you give up to make it. With output per worker per day, the rule is the same one you used for the midterm:

Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = (units of Y per worker per day) ÷ (units of X per worker per day). The opportunity cost of Y is the reciprocal. Whoever has the lower opportunity cost of a good has the comparative advantage in it.

The Fall 2025 final, Q1(b), asked what the principle of comparative advantage implies. The key answer was "I should specialize in doing the task that I do best," meaning best relative to my own other tasks, which is the task where my opportunity cost is lowest. The distractor "I should specialize in all tasks that I do better than others" describes absolute advantage, and it is wrong.

Where comparative advantage comes from

Factor abundance. A "factor" is just an input to production. A country tends to have a comparative advantage in goods that use the inputs it has a lot of. Edwards' examples: mineral wealth and early U.S. industrialization (his slides show the U.S. share of world mineral output in 1913 and of manufacturing exports from 1879 to 1929, from the economic historian Gavin Wright); climate and soil deciding where coffee grows; capital and skilled labor deciding what the U.S. has a comparative advantage in today; and many developing countries having lots of less-skilled labor and a comparative advantage in low-tech manufactured goods.

His table of top U.S. goods exports in December 2021 fits that last point: pharmaceutical preparations led the list at $9,733 million for the month, followed by crude oil ($7,449 million) and chemicals ($7,441 million), with semiconductors, civilian aircraft engines, and medical equipment also on it. Those are goods that use a lot of capital, skilled labor, and natural resources.

Dynamic comparative advantage. Some comparative advantage is acquired, not inherent. By doing something or getting an early start, a country can become the low-opportunity-cost producer of a good, because its workers and firms learn and build up skills and suppliers. The slide asks "Examples?" and leaves it open. Any industry where a country got in early and built up know-how makes a reasonable answer, as long as you explain the early-start mechanism.

The real data on his next slide point the same way. World soybean production in 2022/23 (U.S. Department of Agriculture, million metric tons): Brazil 152.0, United States 118.3, Argentina 49.5, China 18.4, out of a world total of 391.2.

The two PPCs

A production possibilities curve (PPC) shows every combination of the two goods a country can produce. Edwards draws it per worker per day, with soybeans on the horizontal axis and washing machines on the vertical axis. One U.S. worker can make 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines, or any straight-line mix between them. One Chinese worker can make 1 ton or 2 machines.

The slope of a PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis, in units of the good on the vertical axis. With soybeans across and washing machines up, the U.S. PPC has slope −1 and China's has slope −2. The flatter PPC belongs to the country with the lower opportunity cost of soybeans.

The terms of trade

The terms of trade are the terms at which goods trade in the world market. Technically, a country's terms of trade is the relative price of its exports. The U.S. exports soybeans, so its terms of trade are measured in washing machines per ton of soybeans. Because it depends on world prices, it is also called the world relative price.

Terms of trade (for the soybean exporter) = Psoybeans ÷ Pwashing machines, in washing machines per ton. With soybeans at $400 a ton and washing machines at $300, the terms of trade are 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ washing machines per ton.

Getting the ratio upside down is the classic error. A quick check: the answer should be in "washing machines per ton," so a ton of soybeans buys how many machines? $400 buys 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines. China exports washing machines, so China's own terms of trade are the reciprocal, 300 ÷ 400 = ¾ ton of soybeans per washing machine.

When both countries want to trade

Both countries gain only if the terms of trade lie between the two countries' opportunity costs. In the example, a ton of soybeans must trade for more than 1 washing machine (the U.S. cost) and fewer than 2 (China's cost).

Why: the U.S. sells soybeans. If a ton fetches less than 1 machine, the U.S. would do better making machines itself, since a ton costs it exactly 1 machine. China buys soybeans. If a ton costs more than 2 machines, China would do better growing soybeans itself. Between 1 and 2, the U.S. gets machines more cheaply than it could make them, and China gets soybeans more cheaply than it could grow them.

Market forces push the world price into that range. Edwards' example: soybeans at $200 and washing machines at $300 means a ton trades for only ⅔ of a machine. China would love to buy U.S. soybeans at that price, but the U.S. would not want to sell, because each ton costs it a whole machine to grow. There is excess demand for soybeans in the world market, and that pushes up the relative price of soybeans. The reverse also works: if a ton fetched more than 2 machines, the U.S. would want to sell lots of soybeans, China would want to buy none, and the excess supply would push the relative price back down.

Where in the range the price ends up decides how the gains are split. At exactly 1 machine per ton, the U.S. gets nothing from trade and China gets all the gain. At exactly 2, China gets nothing and the U.S. gets all of it. The closer the terms of trade are to a country's own opportunity cost, the smaller that country's gain.

The consumption possibilities curve (CPC)

The consumption possibilities curve (CPC) shows the combinations of the two goods a country can have once it can trade. Edwards assumes the world relative price is 1⅓ washing machines per ton.

U.S.: a worker specializes completely in soybeans and grows 2 tons. Each ton can be traded for 1⅓ machines, so trading all of it would bring 2 × 1⅓ = 2⅔ machines. The CPC runs from 2 tons of soybeans (keep everything) to 2⅔ washing machines (trade everything), a straight line with slope −1⅓. Without trade, the most a U.S. worker could have was 2 machines.

China: a worker specializes completely in washing machines and makes 2. One machine buys ¾ ton of soybeans (since 1 ton costs 1⅓ machines), so trading both would bring 2 × ¾ = 1½ tons. The CPC runs from 2 machines to 1½ tons, also with slope −1⅓. Without trade, the most a Chinese worker could have was 1 ton.

The gains from trade show up as the CPC lying outside the PPC. Both countries' CPCs have slope equal to minus the terms of trade (here −1⅓), and each starts from the point where the country produces only its comparative advantage good.

A numeric way to state the gain: a U.S. worker who wants 1 ton of soybeans can have 1⅓ washing machines with trade but only 1 without, a gain of ⅓ machine. A Chinese worker who wants 1 washing machine can have ¾ ton of soybeans with trade but only ½ ton without, a gain of ¼ ton. Both gain.

How the terms of trade move the CPC

The U.S. CPC always starts at its production point, 2 tons of soybeans, and its slope is minus the terms of trade. So a change in the terms of trade pivots the CPC around that point. A higher relative price of soybeans swings it out (bigger U.S. gains). A lower one swings it in. At 1 machine per ton the CPC lies right on top of the U.S. PPC, and the U.S. gains nothing from trade.

This is the trade-war logic behind Fall 2025 final Q4. When China's tariffs shut out U.S. soybeans, the price U.S. soybeans could fetch fell, so the U.S. terms of trade worsened and the U.S. CPC pivoted in toward its PPC. This picture is the long-run view, where farmers can choose what to produce. The final itself used the short-run view from Class 16: the harvest was already planted and could not be stored, so the price fell all the way down the U.S. demand curve. The next unit draws that graph.

Why straight-line PPCs give complete specialization

With a straight PPC, a line with slope equal to minus the terms of trade touches the PPC only at one of its endpoints, so each country produces only one good. That is why the U.S. grows only soybeans and China makes only washing machines in this example.

Curved PPCs: produce at the tangency, trade to the CPC

Two limitations of the straight-line model. A country's PPC is almost surely curved (bowed out), because the opportunity cost of making more of a good rises as more is produced: the land and workers best suited to soybeans switch first, and later switches cost more machines per ton. And countries rarely specialize completely. The curved PPC explains both.

Keep the world price at $400 a ton and $300 a machine (1⅓ machines per ton), and assume the world price does not change as the country produces more. The country picks the production point on its PPC that has the highest value at world prices. That is the point A where a line with slope −1⅓ is just tangent to the PPC. A point inside the PPC would waste resources; any other point on the PPC is worth less at world prices.

With a curved PPC, the country produces at the tangency point A, where the slope of the PPC equals minus the terms of trade. The CPC is the straight line through A with that slope. The country then trades along the CPC to any consumption point such as B, which lies outside the PPC. Moving from A to B, it exports the good it produces more of than it consumes and imports the other.

Check with values: at A the output is worth $400 × 4 million + $300 × 3 million = $2.5 billion at world prices. Other points on this PPC are worth less, for example 3 million tons and 4 million machines is worth $1.2 billion + $1.2 billion = $2.4 billion. B is also worth $400 × 2.5 million + $300 × 5 million = $2.5 billion, which is why it is affordable: exports of 1.5 million tons × $400 = $600 million pay for imports of 2 million machines × $300 = $600 million. The U.S. still makes some washing machines (3 million) but exports soybeans, which is the "rarely specialize completely" point.

Who wins and who loses

Between countries, the model says both countries gain as long as the terms of trade lie strictly between their opportunity costs, and the world price splits the gain. Edwards' slide summary: trade theory predicts gains from specialization when countries differ in opportunity cost, and world prices encourage trade and make sure both countries benefit.

Inside each country, the textbook adds that the gains are not shared evenly. Producers and workers in the export industry gain, because the world market lets them sell more at a better price; buyers of the imported good gain, because it gets cheaper. Producers and workers in the import-competing industry lose, because they now face cheaper foreign competition. In the example, U.S. soybean farmers and U.S. buyers of washing machines win, and U.S. washing machine makers lose; in China it is the reverse. The total gains are larger than the losses, so the winners could in principle compensate the losers. The next unit (Class 16) shows these winners and losers as areas on a supply and demand graph with a world price.

A trade war hits the export side. Edwards' current-events slide cited 2025 reporting that U.S. soybean farmers were panicking over the loss of Chinese buyers, and noted that in the first Trump term, when Chinese soybean imports also plummeted, the U.S. government paid $23 billion to U.S. soybean farmers, with plans for $10 billion at the time of the slide. On the Fall 2025 final, Q4(d) added a later one-time payment of $12 billion and China's record trade surplus, and the key concluded that the U.S. lost that round and China won, because the U.S. had to compensate its farmers while China's durable manufactured goods found new buyers.

Does trade raise growth? The evidence

The slides end with evidence. Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner compared eight economies that were always open to trade with forty that were always closed from 1966 to 1990, and the open group grew faster over most of the period. But a simple correlation has problems:

  • Reverse causation. Maybe being rich makes a country want to trade more, so growth causes trade instead of the other way around.
  • Omitted influences. Countries that adopt free trade may also adopt other growth-friendly policies, and those could be causing the growth.

Jeffrey Frankel and David Romer addressed this by using only the part of trade that geography predicts (being close to big markets or the sea), which income cannot cause. Their chart still shows a positive relationship between that geographic component of trade and income per worker. David Dollar and Aart Kraay found that "globalisers," developing countries that started opening to trade from the late 1970s to the 1980s, grew substantially more afterward. Edwards' summary: much evidence suggests trade helps growth.

Worked examples

Example: Edwards' U.S. and China numbers, start to finish

A U.S. worker makes 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines a day; a Chinese worker makes 1 ton or 2 machines. Soybeans sell for $400 a ton and machines for $300.

  1. Opportunity costs of 1 ton: U.S. 2 ÷ 2 = 1 machine; China 2 ÷ 1 = 2 machines. Of 1 machine: U.S. 1 ton; China ½ ton.
  2. Comparative advantage: the U.S. in soybeans (1 < 2 machines), China in washing machines (½ < 1 ton).
  3. Range for trade: between 1 and 2 machines per ton.
  4. Terms of trade: 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton, inside the range, so both want to trade.
  5. U.S. CPC: produce 2 tons; trade all for 2 × 1⅓ = 2⅔ machines. Intercepts 2 tons and 2⅔ machines, slope −1⅓.
  6. China CPC: produce 2 machines; trade all for 2 ÷ 1⅓ = 1½ tons. Intercepts 2 machines and 1½ tons, slope −1⅓.

Answer: The U.S. specializes in soybeans and China in washing machines. Both CPCs have slope −1⅓ and lie outside the PPCs: the U.S. can reach 2⅔ machines instead of 2, China 1½ tons instead of 1.

Example: who gets the gains at different world prices

Same countries. Suppose a U.S. worker keeps 1 ton of soybeans and trades the other ton. How many washing machines does she end up with at terms of trade of 1, 1⅓, 1½, and 2?

  1. Without trade, a U.S. worker with 1 ton has 1 machine (PPC: 2 − 1 = 1).
  2. At 1: she gets 1 machine. Gain 0, so the U.S. has no reason to trade.
  3. At 1⅓: 1⅓ machines. Gain ⅓.
  4. At 1½: 1½ machines. Gain ½.
  5. At 2: 2 machines. Gain 1. But at this price China gains nothing, since a ton costs it exactly 2 machines to grow.

Answer: 1, 1⅓, 1½, and 2 machines. A higher relative price of soybeans moves gains toward the U.S. and away from China.

Example: the curved PPC and the most valuable output

A country's PPC is a quarter circle: (soybeans)2 + (machines)2 = 25, in millions. World prices are $400 a ton and $300 a machine. Which of these production points should it choose: (5 tons, 0), (4, 3), (3, 4), (0, 5)? Where can it consume?

  1. All four points are on the PPC (for example 42 + 32 = 25).
  2. Values at world prices (in millions of dollars): (5, 0): 2,000. (4, 3): 1,600 + 900 = 2,500. (3, 4): 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400. (0, 5): 1,500.
  3. The best is A = (4, 3), worth $2.5 billion. It is where the PPC is tangent to a line with slope −1⅓.
  4. The CPC is every bundle worth $2,500 million: intercepts 2,500 ÷ 400 = 6¼ million tons and 2,500 ÷ 300 = 8⅓ million machines.
  5. Consuming 2.5 million tons: machines = (2,500 − 1,000) ÷ 300 = 5 million. Exports 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 million tons; imports 5 − 3 = 2 million machines.

Answer: Produce at A = (4, 3) and trade to B = (2.5, 5). B lies outside the PPC (2.52 + 52 = 31.25 > 25), which is the gain from trade.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Giving the U.S. both goods because it is at least as productive in both.

Correct: Specialization follows opportunity cost. China's opportunity cost of a washing machine (½ ton) is lower than the U.S. cost (1 ton), so China makes machines.

Mistake: Writing the terms of trade upside down: PWM ÷ PS = ¾ "washing machines per ton."

Correct: For the soybean exporter it is PS ÷ PWM = 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton. Check the units: how many machines does one ton buy?

Mistake: Starting the CPC at the PPC's vertical intercept for the U.S.

Correct: The CPC starts where the country actually produces, its comparative advantage good. For the U.S. that is 2 tons of soybeans on the horizontal axis; the CPC then rises to 2⅔ machines.

Mistake: Saying any world price makes both countries better off.

Correct: Only prices strictly between the two opportunity costs (between 1 and 2 machines per ton) give both a gain. At an endpoint one country gains nothing; outside the range one country refuses to trade.

Mistake: Concluding that everyone in a country gains from trade.

Correct: The country as a whole gains, but import-competing producers and workers lose. Exporters and buyers of imports gain.

Mistake: With a curved PPC, putting the production point at an endpoint or inside the curve.

Correct: Produce at the tangency A, where the PPC's slope equals minus the terms of trade. Consume at a different point B on the CPC.

Mistake: Treating the Sachs and Warner chart as proof that trade causes growth.

Correct: It is a correlation. Name reverse causation and omitted policies as problems, and mention the geography-based evidence of Frankel and Romer.

How it shows up on the final

Multiple choice. Fall 2025 Q1(b) asked what comparative advantage implies (specialize in the task you do best relative to your other tasks). The final's multiple choice reaches back to midterm comparative advantage, so expect items on opportunity cost, terms of trade ranges, and who specializes in what.

Short answers. A two-country table like the U.S. and China one is a natural Q2-style item: compute opportunity costs, name who specializes, give the range for the terms of trade, compute the terms of trade from two dollar prices.

Long trade story. Fall 2025 Q4 was a 12-point trade story built on the soybean trade war, using supply and demand with a world price (next unit) and the labor market. Its part (d) asked who won and lost; this unit gives you the language for it: the export sector loses when its terms of trade fall, and governments compensate the losers. A graph question here would be "draw the PPC and CPC for each country, label intercepts and slopes."

For any PPC or CPC drawing: label both axes with the good names, label both intercepts with numbers, write the slope on each line, and say which point the country produces at. Edwards' key answers reward the labels.

On your cheat sheet, write the terms of trade as "P of the export ÷ P of the import, in imports per unit of export," and the rule "terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs."

Practice questions

The first five are Q1-style multiple choice. Then come Edwards-style stories with (a), (b), (c) parts. Draw the graphs on paper with labeled intercepts and slopes.

2 points (Fall 2025 final, Q1(b)) The principle of comparative advantage implies that

(i) I should specialize in doing the task that I do best
(ii) I should specialize in all tasks that I do better than others
(iii) I should specialize in doing the task that is respected most by others
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(i). "Best" means best relative to my other tasks, where my opportunity cost is lowest. Option (ii) is absolute advantage: being better than others at a task does not mean you should do it.

Full credit: circle (i), the official key answer.

2 points Soybeans sell for $400 a ton and washing machines for $300 in world markets. The U.S. exports soybeans. The U.S. terms of trade are

(i) ¾ washing machine per ton of soybeans
(ii) 1⅓ washing machines per ton of soybeans
(iii) $100 per ton
(iv) 1⅓ tons of soybeans per washing machine

Show answer

(ii). A country's terms of trade is the relative price of its exports: PS ÷ PWM = 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ washing machines per ton. Option (i) is China's terms of trade with the wrong units; China's is ¾ ton per machine.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points When two countries with straight-line PPCs trade at terms of trade strictly between their opportunity costs,

(i) Each country specializes completely in its comparative advantage good
(ii) Each country produces some of both goods
(iii) Only the country with absolute advantage gains
(iv) Neither country gains

Show answer

(i). A line with slope equal to minus the terms of trade touches a straight PPC only at an endpoint, so each country produces only one good. Producing some of both happens with curved PPCs.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points A developing country has a large supply of less-skilled labor and little capital. Factor abundance predicts it has a comparative advantage in

(i) Pharmaceuticals
(ii) Civilian aircraft engines
(iii) Low-tech manufactured goods
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(iii). Countries tend to have a comparative advantage in goods that use the inputs they have in abundance. Pharmaceuticals and aircraft engines use capital and skilled labor, which is where the U.S. has its comparative advantage.

Full credit: circle (iii).

2 points In Edwards' example (U.S.: 2 tons or 2 machines; China: 1 ton or 2 machines), suppose a ton of soybeans traded for 2½ washing machines in world markets. Then

(i) Both countries gain more than before
(ii) China would not want to buy soybeans, and the relative price of soybeans would fall
(iii) The U.S. would stop growing soybeans
(iv) Nothing changes

Show answer

(ii). A ton costs China only 2 machines to grow itself, so it will not pay 2½. The U.S. would want to sell lots of soybeans, China would want none, and that excess supply pushes the relative price of soybeans down into the 1 to 2 range.

Full credit: circle (ii).

14 points Beans for machines. Suppose new seed technology means a typical U.S. farm worker can now grow 4 tons of soybeans a day, or could instead make 2 washing machines. A typical Chinese worker can grow 1 ton of soybeans or make 2 washing machines. World prices are $300 a ton for soybeans and $400 per washing machine.

(a) (2 points) Find each country's opportunity cost of a ton of soybeans and of a washing machine. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Which country has an absolute advantage in each good? Which has a comparative advantage in each good? Explain.

(c) (2 points) Between what values must the terms of trade lie for both countries to want to trade? State the units.

(d) (2 points) Compute the terms of trade for the U.S. at these world prices. Will both countries trade?

(e) (4 points) Draw the U.S. PPC and CPC per worker per day, soybeans on the horizontal axis. Label intercepts and slopes.

(f) (2 points) A U.S. worker wants to consume 2 tons of soybeans. How many washing machines can she have with trade and without trade?

Show answer

(a) U.S.: a ton costs 2 ÷ 4 = ½ washing machine; a machine costs 4 ÷ 2 = 2 tons. China: a ton costs 2 ÷ 1 = 2 machines; a machine costs 1 ÷ 2 = ½ ton.

(b) Absolute advantage: the U.S. in soybeans (4 tons against 1); neither in washing machines (2 each). Comparative advantage: the U.S. in soybeans (½ machine against 2) and China in washing machines (½ ton against 2 tons), because each is the low-opportunity-cost producer of that good.

(c) Between the two opportunity costs of a ton: more than ½ and less than 2 washing machines per ton of soybeans.

(d) Terms of trade = PS ÷ PWM = 300 ÷ 400 = ¾ washing machine per ton. That is between ½ and 2, so both countries trade: the U.S. exports soybeans and China exports washing machines.

(e) PPC: from 2 machines on the vertical axis to 4 tons on the horizontal axis, slope −½. CPC: the U.S. produces 4 tons and could trade them all for 4 × ¾ = 3 machines, so the CPC runs from 4 tons to 3 machines, slope −¾, outside the PPC.

(f) With trade: keep 2 tons and trade 2 tons for 2 × ¾ = 1.5 machines. Without trade: on the PPC, 2 tons leaves 2 − ½ × 2 = 1 machine. The gain is 0.5 machine per worker per day.

Full credit: all four opportunity costs; absolute and comparative advantage correctly assigned with the opportunity cost reason; ½ to 2 machines per ton; ¾ and "yes, inside the range"; a labeled PPC (2, 4, slope −½) and CPC (3, 4, slope −¾) lying outside; 1.5 against 1 machine.

6 points China's side of the deal. Use Edwards' original numbers: a Chinese worker makes 1 ton of soybeans or 2 washing machines a day, and the world price is 1⅓ washing machines per ton of soybeans.

(a) (2 points) What does China specialize in, and what are its terms of trade (the relative price of its exports)?

(b) (2 points) Describe China's PPC and CPC: intercepts and slopes.

(c) (2 points) A Chinese worker keeps 1 washing machine. How many tons of soybeans can she have with trade and without?

Show answer

(a) China specializes in washing machines, its low-opportunity-cost good (½ ton each). Its terms of trade are the reciprocal of the U.S. ones: 1 ÷ 1⅓ = ¾ ton of soybeans per washing machine.

(b) PPC: 2 machines on the vertical axis to 1 ton on the horizontal, slope −2. CPC: starts at 2 machines and reaches 2 × ¾ = 1½ tons, slope −1⅓, outside the PPC.

(c) With trade: trade 1 machine for ¾ ton. Without trade: making 1 machine uses half the day, leaving time for ½ ton. The gain is ¼ ton.

Full credit: washing machines and ¾ ton per machine; PPC (2, 1, slope −2) and CPC (2, 1½, slope −1⅓); ¾ against ½ ton.

8 points Soybeans in a trade war. Use Edwards' original numbers (U.S. worker: 2 tons or 2 machines). Before the trade war, a ton of U.S. soybeans traded for 1⅓ washing machines. Then China placed steep tariffs on U.S. soybeans, and the best price U.S. farmers could get fell to 1 washing machine per ton.

(a) (2 points) What happens to the U.S. terms of trade and to the U.S. CPC? Describe the graph.

(b) (2 points) A U.S. worker who keeps 1 ton of soybeans: how many washing machines could she get before and after? What is the U.S. gain from trade now?

(c) (2 points) Inside the U.S., who is hurt most, and what did the U.S. government do about it in the first Trump term according to Edwards' slide?

(d) (2 points) If the price fell further, below 1 washing machine per ton, what would the U.S. do? Explain.

Show answer

(a) The U.S. terms of trade worsen from 1⅓ to 1 machine per ton. The CPC pivots in around the production point (2 tons): its vertical intercept falls from 2⅔ machines to 2, and its slope from −1⅓ to −1, so it now lies on top of the PPC.

(b) Before: 1⅓ machines. After: 1 machine, the same as without trade. The U.S. gain from trade falls to zero, a loss of ⅓ machine per worker per day at that consumption point.

(c) U.S. soybean farmers (and their workers), because they are the export sector whose price fell. Edwards' slide says the U.S. government paid $23 billion to U.S. soybean farmers when Chinese imports plummeted in the first Trump term.

(d) Below 1 machine per ton, a ton fetches less than it costs the U.S. to grow (1 machine), so the U.S. would stop exporting soybeans and make washing machines itself. The price is now outside the range where the U.S. wants to trade. This is a planting decision for the next season. A harvest that is already in the ground cannot be switched, which is the short-run case on the Fall 2025 final (see the next unit).

Full credit: terms of trade worsen and the CPC pivots in to the PPC; 1⅓ then 1 machine and zero gain; soybean farmers, $23 billion; stop exporting because the price is below the U.S. opportunity cost.

8 points A curved PPC. A country's PPC is a quarter circle: (soybeans)2 + (washing machines)2 = 100, in millions. World prices are $300 a ton and $400 per washing machine, and they do not change with how much this country produces.

(a) (2 points) Compute the world-market value of each production point: (10, 0), (8, 6), (6, 8), (0, 10), with soybeans first. Which should the country produce?

(b) (2 points) What are the terms of trade, in washing machines per ton? What is the slope of the CPC?

(c) (2 points) Find the CPC's two intercepts.

(d) (2 points) The country wants to consume 10 million tons of soybeans. How many washing machines can it consume? What does it export and import, and how much?

Show answer

(a) (10, 0): $3,000 million. (8, 6): 2,400 + 2,400 = $4,800 million. (6, 8): 1,800 + 3,200 = $5,000 million. (0, 10): $4,000 million. Produce at A = (6, 8), the highest value.

(b) 300 ÷ 400 = ¾ washing machine per ton, so the CPC slope is −¾. At A the PPC is tangent to a line with that slope.

(c) The CPC is every bundle worth $5,000 million: 5,000 ÷ 300 = 16⅔ million tons and 5,000 ÷ 400 = 12½ million machines.

(d) Machines = (5,000 − 300 × 10) ÷ 400 = 2,000 ÷ 400 = 5 million. It produced 6 million tons and consumes 10, so it imports 4 million tons of soybeans. It produced 8 million machines and consumes 5, so it exports 3 million machines. Check: exports 3 × $400 = $1,200 million pay for imports 4 × $300 = $1,200 million.

Full credit: the four values with (6, 8) chosen; ¾ and slope −¾; 16⅔ and 12½; 5 million machines, imports 4 million tons, exports 3 million machines.

4 points Where world prices land. Use Edwards' original numbers.

(a) (2 points) Soybeans sell for $200 a ton and washing machines for $300. Explain what each country wants to do and what happens to the relative price.

(b) (2 points) Now soybeans sell for $700 and machines for $300. Explain again.

Show answer

(a) A ton trades for 200 ÷ 300 = ⅔ machine, below the U.S. cost of 1 machine. China would love to buy U.S. soybeans, but the U.S. would rather make machines itself. Excess demand for soybeans pushes the relative price of soybeans up.

(b) A ton trades for 700 ÷ 300 = 2⅓ machines, above China's cost of 2. The U.S. would love to sell soybeans, but China would rather grow its own. Excess supply pushes the relative price of soybeans down. Either way the price is pushed into the 1 to 2 range.

Full credit: ⅔ and 2⅓ computed; who refuses in each case; excess demand raises and excess supply lowers the relative price.

6 points Does trade cause growth? A news story shows that countries that trade more are richer and conclude that trade made them rich.

(a) (2 points) Name and explain two problems with that conclusion.

(b) (2 points) How did Frankel and Romer try to get around these problems, and what did they find?

(c) (2 points) What does the evidence on "globalisers" from Dollar and Kraay suggest?

Show answer

(a) Reverse causation: being rich may make a country trade more, so income could be causing trade. Omitted influences: countries that adopt free trade may also adopt other growth-friendly policies, and those could explain the growth.

(b) They used only the part of trade predicted by geography (location relative to other countries), which a country's income cannot cause. That geographic component of trade is still positively associated with income per worker.

(c) Developing countries that opened to trade from the late 1970s to the 1980s grew substantially more afterward, which supports the view that trade helps growth.

Full credit: reverse causation and omitted variables, each explained; geography-predicted trade still linked to higher income; globalisers grew faster after opening.

4 points Sources of comparative advantage.

(a) (2 points) Pharmaceutical preparations topped the list of U.S. goods exports in Edwards' December 2021 table. Which source of comparative advantage best explains this? Explain.

(b) (2 points) Explain dynamic comparative advantage, and how it differs from comparative advantage based on climate.

Show answer

(a) Factor abundance. The U.S. has a lot of capital and skilled labor, and pharmaceuticals use those inputs heavily, so the U.S. is a low-opportunity-cost producer of them.

(b) Dynamic comparative advantage is acquired: by doing something or getting an early start, a country builds skills and know-how and becomes the low-opportunity-cost producer. Climate-based advantage (like where coffee grows) is inherent and comes from natural conditions.

Full credit: factor abundance with capital and skilled labor; acquired through an early start versus inherent natural conditions.

4 points Winners and losers. The U.S. and China trade as in Edwards' example: the U.S. exports soybeans and imports washing machines.

(a) (2 points) Does the U.S. as a whole gain? How can you tell on a graph?

(b) (2 points) Name one group in the U.S. that gains and one that loses, and explain why.

Show answer

(a) Yes, as long as the terms of trade are strictly between 1 and 2 machines per ton. On the graph, the U.S. CPC lies outside its PPC.

(b) Gain: U.S. soybean farmers, who sell more at a better price, and U.S. buyers of washing machines, who pay less. Lose: U.S. washing machine makers and their workers, who face cheaper imports. The country's total gain is larger than these losses.

Full credit: yes, CPC outside PPC; one export-side or consumer winner and the import-competing loser, each with a reason.

One-minute review

  • Comparative advantage means the lower opportunity cost. Opportunity cost of X = (Y per worker) ÷ (X per worker).
  • Edwards' example: U.S. 2 tons or 2 machines, China 1 ton or 2 machines. The U.S. makes soybeans (costs 1 machine against 2), China makes machines (costs ½ ton against 1).
  • Sources: factor abundance (inputs a country has a lot of) and dynamic comparative advantage (an early start).
  • PPC slope = minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal good: U.S. −1, China −2.
  • Terms of trade = relative price of a country's exports. For the U.S., PS ÷ PWM = 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton. China's is ¾ ton per machine.
  • Both gain only if the terms of trade lie between the opportunity costs (1 and 2 machines per ton). Market forces push prices into that range.
  • The CPC starts at the specialized production point and has slope minus the terms of trade. U.S.: 2 tons to 2⅔ machines. China: 2 machines to 1½ tons. CPC outside PPC = gains from trade.
  • Curved PPC: produce at the tangency A (highest world value), consume at B on the CPC; export one good, import the other; specialization is incomplete.
  • Countries gain overall, but import-competing producers lose. A trade war worsens the exporter's terms of trade and pivots its CPC in.
  • Trade and growth: positive correlation, but watch reverse causation and omitted policies; geography-based evidence still points to trade helping growth.
