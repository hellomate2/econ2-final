Class 15 · International Trade

Gains from international trade

This unit takes comparative advantage from the midterm and applies it to whole countries: Edwards' U.S. and China example with soybeans and washing machines, the terms of trade (the world relative price), consumption possibilities curves that lie outside the production possibilities curves, and specialization when the production possibilities curve (PPC) is curved. On the Fall 2025 final it showed up as Q1(b), a 2-point multiple choice on what comparative advantage implies, and as the background for Q4, the soybean trade war, where part (d) asked who won and who lost.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

Each country should make the good it gives up the least to make, and buy the other good from abroad. When both countries do that and trade at a price between their two costs, both end up with more than they could make alone. For the final, remember one rule: compare opportunity costs, never raw output, and the trading price has to land between the two countries' opportunity costs.

Why trade, and what this unit adds to the midterm

Edwards' first chart shows that U.S. exports plus imports have risen as a share of national income (GDP, gross domestic product) since 1929. To explain why countries trade, economists use the same tool you used for Bill and Chris on the island: comparative advantage.

What is new here is the price at which countries trade. On the island the trading price was a number of fish per coconut. Between countries it is the terms of trade, a world relative price. A relative price is the price of one good counted in units of another good instead of in dollars, such as "1⅓ washing machines per ton of soybeans." The terms of trade decide whether both countries want to trade and how the gains are split. His own warning on the learning objectives slide: numerators and denominators get confusing here, so set up every ratio carefully.

Comparative advantage between countries

A country has a comparative advantage in a good if it is the low-opportunity-cost producer of that good. Absolute advantage means producing more of a good per worker. As on the island, comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, decides who should specialize in what.

In plain words: you have a comparative advantage in whatever you give up the least to make. Being faster or making more is a different thing, called absolute advantage, and it does not decide who makes what.

The opportunity cost of one unit of a good is what you give up to make it. With output per worker per day, the rule is the same one you used for the midterm:

Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = (units of Y per worker per day) ÷ (units of X per worker per day). The opportunity cost of Y is the reciprocal. Whoever has the lower opportunity cost of a good has the comparative advantage in it.

Why the formula works, step by step for the U.S.: a U.S. worker can spend the day making 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines. So 2 tons cost 2 machines, and dividing both sides by 2, 1 ton costs 2 ÷ 2 = 1 machine. The good you want the cost of always goes in the denominator.

In plain words: to find what a ton of soybeans really costs a country, ask how many washing machines its worker could have built in the time it takes to grow that ton.

The Fall 2025 final, Q1(b), asked what the principle of comparative advantage implies. The key answer was "I should specialize in doing the task that I do best," meaning best relative to my own other tasks, which is the task where my opportunity cost is lowest. The distractor "I should specialize in all tasks that I do better than others" describes absolute advantage, and it is wrong.

Where comparative advantage comes from

Factor abundance. A "factor" is just an input to production. A country tends to have a comparative advantage in goods that use the inputs it has a lot of. Edwards gives four examples:

  • Mineral wealth and early U.S. industrialization. His slides show the U.S. share of world mineral output in 1913 and of manufacturing exports from 1879 to 1929, from the economic historian Gavin Wright.
  • Climate and soil deciding where coffee grows.
  • Capital and skilled labor deciding what the U.S. has a comparative advantage in today.
  • Many developing countries, which have lots of less-skilled labor and a comparative advantage in low-tech manufactured goods.

In plain words: countries tend to sell the world things made with what they have plenty of. A country with lots of oil sells oil, a country with the right weather sells coffee, and a country with lots of machines and trained workers sells high-tech goods.

His table of top U.S. goods exports in December 2021 fits that last point: pharmaceutical preparations led the list at $9,733 million for the month, followed by crude oil ($7,449 million) and chemicals ($7,441 million), with semiconductors, civilian aircraft engines, and medical equipment also on it. Those are goods that use a lot of capital, skilled labor, and natural resources.

Dynamic comparative advantage. Some comparative advantage is acquired, not inherent. By doing something or getting an early start, a country can become the low-opportunity-cost producer of a good, because its workers and firms learn and build up skills and suppliers. The slide asks "Examples?" and leaves it open. Any industry where a country got in early and built up know-how makes a reasonable answer, as long as you explain the early-start mechanism.

In plain words: some advantages come from nature, like soil and weather. Others are earned by starting early and getting good at something before anyone else does.

The two PPCs

A production possibilities curve (PPC) shows every combination of the two goods a country can produce. Edwards draws it per worker per day, with soybeans on the horizontal axis and washing machines on the vertical axis. One U.S. worker can make 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines, or any straight-line mix between them. One Chinese worker can make 1 ton or 2 machines.

The slope of a PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis, in units of the good on the vertical axis. With soybeans across and washing machines up, the U.S. PPC has slope −1 and China's has slope −2. The flatter PPC belongs to the country with the lower opportunity cost of soybeans.

Where the slope comes from: slope is rise over run. Going from the top of the U.S. line (0 tons, 2 machines) to the bottom (2 tons, 0 machines), the line falls 2 machines while it runs 2 tons, so the slope is −2 ÷ 2 = −1. China's line falls 2 machines over a run of only 1 ton, so its slope is −2 ÷ 1 = −2.

In plain words: a flatter line means the country gives up fewer washing machines for each extra ton of soybeans, so soybeans are cheap for that country to make.

The terms of trade

The terms of trade are the terms at which goods trade in the world market. Technically, a country's terms of trade is the relative price of its exports. The U.S. exports soybeans, so its terms of trade are measured in washing machines per ton of soybeans. Because it depends on world prices, it is also called the world relative price.

Terms of trade (for the soybean exporter) = Psoybeans ÷ Pwashing machines, in washing machines per ton. With soybeans at $400 a ton and washing machines at $300, the terms of trade are 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ washing machines per ton.

In plain words: the terms of trade tell you how much of the other good one unit of your export buys on the world market. Put the price of what you sell on top.

Getting the ratio upside down is the classic error. A quick check: the answer should be in "washing machines per ton," so a ton of soybeans buys how many machines? $400 buys 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines. China exports washing machines, so China's own terms of trade are the reciprocal, 300 ÷ 400 = ¾ ton of soybeans per washing machine.

When both countries want to trade

Both countries gain only if the terms of trade lie between the two countries' opportunity costs. In the example, a ton of soybeans must trade for more than 1 washing machine (the U.S. cost) and fewer than 2 (China's cost).

In plain words: each country has to get a better deal by trading than by making the good itself. That only happens when the world price sits between what the good costs each country to make.

Why: the U.S. sells soybeans. If a ton fetches less than 1 machine, the U.S. would do better making machines itself, since a ton costs it exactly 1 machine. China buys soybeans. If a ton costs more than 2 machines, China would do better growing soybeans itself. Between 1 and 2, the U.S. gets machines more cheaply than it could make them, and China gets soybeans more cheaply than it could grow them.

Market forces push the world price into that range. Edwards' example: soybeans at $200 and washing machines at $300 means a ton trades for only ⅔ of a machine. China would love to buy U.S. soybeans at that price, but the U.S. would not want to sell, because each ton costs it a whole machine to grow. There is excess demand for soybeans in the world market, and that pushes up the relative price of soybeans. The reverse also works: if a ton fetched more than 2 machines, the U.S. would want to sell lots of soybeans, China would want to buy none, and the excess supply would push the relative price back down.

Where in the range the price ends up decides how the gains are split. At exactly 1 machine per ton, the U.S. gets nothing from trade and China gets all the gain. At exactly 2, China gets nothing and the U.S. gets all of it. The closer the terms of trade are to a country's own opportunity cost, the smaller that country's gain.

In plain words: a country that trades at a price close to its own cost gets little out of the deal. The country whose cost is far from the world price gets most of the gain.

The consumption possibilities curve (CPC)

The consumption possibilities curve (CPC) shows the combinations of the two goods a country can have once it can trade. Edwards assumes the world relative price is 1⅓ washing machines per ton.

U.S.: a worker specializes completely in soybeans and grows 2 tons. Each ton can be traded for 1⅓ machines, so trading all of it would bring 2 × 1⅓ = 2⅔ machines. The CPC runs from 2 tons of soybeans (keep everything) to 2⅔ washing machines (trade everything), a straight line with slope −1⅓. Without trade, the most a U.S. worker could have was 2 machines.

China: a worker specializes completely in washing machines and makes 2. One ton costs 1⅓ machines, so one machine buys 1 ÷ 1⅓ = 1 ÷ (4/3) = ¾ ton of soybeans. Trading both machines would bring 2 × ¾ = 1½ tons. The CPC runs from 2 machines to 1½ tons, also with slope −1⅓. Without trade, the most a Chinese worker could have was 1 ton.

The gains from trade show up as the CPC lying outside the PPC. Both countries' CPCs have slope equal to minus the terms of trade (here −1⅓), and each starts from the point where the country produces only its comparative advantage good.

In plain words: with trade, a country can end up with more of both goods than it could ever make alone. That extra is the gain from trade.

A numeric way to state the gain: a U.S. worker who wants 1 ton of soybeans can have 1⅓ washing machines with trade but only 1 without, a gain of ⅓ machine. A Chinese worker who wants 1 washing machine can have ¾ ton of soybeans with trade but only ½ ton without, a gain of ¼ ton. Both gain.

How the terms of trade move the CPC

The U.S. CPC always starts at its production point, 2 tons of soybeans, and its slope is minus the terms of trade. So a change in the terms of trade pivots the CPC around that point. A higher relative price of soybeans swings it out (bigger U.S. gains). A lower one swings it in. At 1 machine per ton the CPC lies right on top of the U.S. PPC, and the U.S. gains nothing from trade.

To find the new top of the CPC, multiply the 2 tons the U.S. produces by the terms of trade: 2 × 1 = 2 machines, 2 × 1⅓ = 2⅔ machines, and 2 × 2 = 4 machines.

In plain words: the better the price a country gets for its exports, the more imports it can buy, so its trade line swings outward. A worse price swings it back in.

This is the trade-war logic behind Fall 2025 final Q4. China's tariffs shut out U.S. soybeans. The price U.S. soybeans could fetch fell, so the U.S. terms of trade worsened, and the U.S. CPC pivoted in toward its PPC. This picture is the long-run view, where farmers can choose what to produce. The final itself used the short-run view from Class 16. There, the harvest was already planted and could not be stored, so the price fell all the way down the U.S. demand curve. The next unit draws that graph.

Why straight-line PPCs give complete specialization

With a straight PPC, a line with slope equal to minus the terms of trade touches the PPC only at one of its endpoints, so each country produces only one good. That is why the U.S. grows only soybeans and China makes only washing machines in this example.

The reason in steps: on a straight PPC the opportunity cost is the same for every ton. The U.S. cost is always 1 machine per ton, and a ton sells for 1⅓ machines. So each ton grown in place of 1 machine brings in 1⅓ machines through trade, ⅓ machine more than it cost. That gain is the same for the first ton and the last, so the U.S. keeps moving workers until nobody is left making machines.

In plain words: when making a good costs the same no matter how much you make, it pays to go all the way into the good you are cheapest at.

Curved PPCs: produce at the tangency, trade to the CPC

Two limitations of the straight-line model. A country's PPC is almost surely curved (bowed out), because the opportunity cost of making more of a good rises as more is produced: the land and workers best suited to soybeans switch first, and later switches cost more machines per ton. And countries rarely specialize completely. The curved PPC explains both.

Keep the world price at $400 a ton and $300 a machine (1⅓ machines per ton), and assume the world price does not change as the country produces more. The country picks the production point on its PPC that has the highest value at world prices. That is the point A where a line with slope −1⅓ is just tangent to the PPC. A point inside the PPC would waste resources; any other point on the PPC is worth less at world prices.

With a curved PPC, the country produces at the tangency point A, where the slope of the PPC equals minus the terms of trade. The CPC is the straight line through A with that slope. The country then trades along the CPC to any consumption point such as B, which lies outside the PPC. Moving from A to B, it exports the good it produces more of than it consumes and imports the other.

In plain words: move resources into your export good until one more unit costs you as much as it sells for on the world market. Stop there, then trade for the mix of goods you actually want.

Check with values: at A the output is worth $400 × 4 million + $300 × 3 million = $2.5 billion at world prices. Other points on this PPC are worth less, for example 3 million tons and 4 million machines is worth $1.2 billion + $1.2 billion = $2.4 billion. B is also worth $400 × 2.5 million + $300 × 5 million = $2.5 billion, which is why it is affordable: exports of 1.5 million tons × $400 = $600 million pay for imports of 2 million machines × $300 = $600 million. The U.S. still makes some washing machines (3 million) but exports soybeans, which is the "rarely specialize completely" point.

Who wins and who loses

Between countries, the model says both countries gain as long as the terms of trade lie strictly between their opportunity costs, and the world price splits the gain. Edwards' slide summary: trade theory predicts gains from specialization when countries differ in opportunity cost, and world prices encourage trade and make sure both countries benefit.

Inside each country, the textbook adds that the gains are not shared evenly. Producers and workers in the export industry gain, because the world market lets them sell more at a better price; buyers of the imported good gain, because it gets cheaper. Producers and workers in the import-competing industry lose, because they now face cheaper foreign competition. In the example, U.S. soybean farmers and U.S. buyers of washing machines win, and U.S. washing machine makers lose; in China it is the reverse. The total gains are larger than the losses, so the winners could in principle compensate the losers. The next unit (Class 16) shows these winners and losers as areas on a supply and demand graph with a world price.

In plain words: trade makes the country richer overall, but the people who compete with the new imports can lose profits or jobs.

A trade war hits the export side. Edwards' current-events slide cited 2025 reporting that U.S. soybean farmers were panicking over the loss of Chinese buyers, and noted that in the first Trump term, when Chinese soybean imports also plummeted, the U.S. government paid $23 billion to U.S. soybean farmers, with plans for $10 billion at the time of the slide. On the Fall 2025 final, Q4(d) added a later one-time payment of $12 billion and China's record trade surplus, and the key concluded that the U.S. lost that round and China won, because the U.S. had to compensate its farmers while China's durable manufactured goods found new buyers.

Does trade raise growth? The evidence

The slides end with evidence. Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner compared eight economies that were always open to trade with forty that were always closed from 1966 to 1990, and the open group grew faster over most of the period. But a simple correlation has problems:

  • Reverse causation. Maybe being rich makes a country want to trade more, so growth causes trade instead of the other way around.
  • Omitted influences. Countries that adopt free trade may also adopt other growth-friendly policies, and those could be causing the growth.

In plain words: seeing that countries that trade more are richer does not prove trade made them rich. Being rich could lead to more trade, or some third thing could cause both.

Jeffrey Frankel and David Romer addressed this by using only the part of trade that geography predicts (being close to big markets or the sea), which income cannot cause. Their chart still shows a positive relationship between that geographic component of trade and income per worker. David Dollar and Aart Kraay found that "globalisers," developing countries that started opening to trade from the late 1970s to the 1980s, grew substantially more afterward. Edwards' summary: much evidence suggests trade helps growth.

Worked examples

Example: Edwards' U.S. and China numbers, start to finish

A U.S. worker makes 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines a day; a Chinese worker makes 1 ton or 2 machines. Soybeans sell for $400 a ton and machines for $300.

  1. Opportunity costs of 1 ton: U.S. 2 ÷ 2 = 1 machine; China 2 ÷ 1 = 2 machines. Of 1 machine: U.S. 1 ton; China ½ ton.
  2. Comparative advantage: the U.S. in soybeans (1 < 2 machines), China in washing machines (½ < 1 ton).
  3. Range for trade: between 1 and 2 machines per ton.
  4. Terms of trade: 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton, inside the range, so both want to trade.
  5. U.S. CPC: produce 2 tons; trade all for 2 × 1⅓ = 2⅔ machines. Intercepts 2 tons and 2⅔ machines, slope −1⅓.
  6. China CPC: produce 2 machines; trade all for 2 ÷ 1⅓ = 1½ tons. Intercepts 2 machines and 1½ tons, slope −1⅓.

Answer: The U.S. specializes in soybeans and China in washing machines. Both CPCs have slope −1⅓ and lie outside the PPCs: the U.S. can reach 2⅔ machines instead of 2, China 1½ tons instead of 1.

Example: who gets the gains at different world prices

Same countries. Suppose a U.S. worker keeps 1 ton of soybeans and trades the other ton. How many washing machines does she end up with at terms of trade of 1, 1⅓, 1½, and 2?

  1. Without trade, a U.S. worker with 1 ton has 1 machine (PPC: 2 − 1 = 1).
  2. At 1: she gets 1 machine. Gain 0, so the U.S. has no reason to trade.
  3. At 1⅓: 1⅓ machines. Gain ⅓.
  4. At 1½: 1½ machines. Gain ½.
  5. At 2: 2 machines. Gain 1. But at this price China gains nothing, since a ton costs it exactly 2 machines to grow.

Answer: 1, 1⅓, 1½, and 2 machines. A higher relative price of soybeans moves gains toward the U.S. and away from China.

Example: the curved PPC and the most valuable output

A country's PPC is a quarter circle: (soybeans)2 + (machines)2 = 25, in millions. World prices are $400 a ton and $300 a machine. Which of these production points should it choose: (5 tons, 0), (4, 3), (3, 4), (0, 5)? Where can it consume?

  1. All four points are on the PPC (for example 42 + 32 = 25).
  2. Values at world prices (in millions of dollars): (5, 0): 2,000. (4, 3): 1,600 + 900 = 2,500. (3, 4): 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400. (0, 5): 1,500.
  3. The best is A = (4, 3), worth $2.5 billion. It is where the PPC is tangent to a line with slope −1⅓.
  4. The CPC is every bundle worth $2,500 million: intercepts 2,500 ÷ 400 = 6¼ million tons and 2,500 ÷ 300 = 8⅓ million machines.
  5. Consuming 2.5 million tons: machines = (2,500 − 1,000) ÷ 300 = 5 million. Exports 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 million tons; imports 5 − 3 = 2 million machines.

Answer: Produce at A = (4, 3) and trade to B = (2.5, 5). B lies outside the PPC (2.52 + 52 = 31.25 > 25), which is the gain from trade.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Giving the U.S. both goods because it is at least as productive in both.

Correct: Specialization follows opportunity cost. China's opportunity cost of a washing machine (½ ton) is lower than the U.S. cost (1 ton), so China makes machines.

Mistake: Writing the terms of trade upside down: PWM ÷ PS = ¾ "washing machines per ton."

Correct: For the soybean exporter it is PS ÷ PWM = 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton. Check the units: how many machines does one ton buy?

Mistake: Starting the CPC at the PPC's vertical intercept for the U.S.

Correct: The CPC starts where the country actually produces, its comparative advantage good. For the U.S. that is 2 tons of soybeans on the horizontal axis; the CPC then rises to 2⅔ machines.

Mistake: Saying any world price makes both countries better off.

Correct: Only prices strictly between the two opportunity costs (between 1 and 2 machines per ton) give both a gain. At an endpoint one country gains nothing; outside the range one country refuses to trade.

Mistake: Concluding that everyone in a country gains from trade.

Correct: The country as a whole gains, but import-competing producers and workers lose. Exporters and buyers of imports gain.

Mistake: With a curved PPC, putting the production point at an endpoint or inside the curve.

Correct: Produce at the tangency A, where the PPC's slope equals minus the terms of trade. Consume at a different point B on the CPC.

Mistake: Treating the Sachs and Warner chart as proof that trade causes growth.

Correct: It is a correlation. Name reverse causation and omitted policies as problems, and mention the geography-based evidence of Frankel and Romer.

Mistake: Using the output-table formula on a table of hours per unit, so the opportunity costs come out upside down.

Correct: With hours per unit, the opportunity cost of a good is its own hours divided by the other good's hours. If wheat takes 2 hours and cloth takes 4, a bushel of wheat costs 2 ÷ 4 = ½ bolt of cloth. With hours, the country needing fewer hours has the absolute advantage.

Mistake: Saying that a country with an absolute advantage in both goods has nothing to gain from trade.

Correct: Gains come from different opportunity costs. If Alpha's phone costs 2 shirts and Beta's costs 3, specializing still raises world output, even when Alpha is better at both goods.

Mistake: Giving one country a comparative advantage in both goods.

Correct: The opportunity cost of one good is the reciprocal of the other's, so a low cost of soybeans means a high cost of machines. Each country has a comparative advantage in only one good. If the opportunity costs are equal, neither has one and there is no gain from trade.

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Copy the numbers into a small table: one row per country, one column per good. Then write all four opportunity costs as "other good ÷ this good" (or "this good's hours ÷ other good's hours" if the table is in hours) before answering anything.
  • Read every ratio aloud with its units, such as "washing machines per ton." If the units do not match what the question asks, flip the fraction.
  • For any "range" or "will they trade" question, the answer is the two countries' opportunity costs of the same good, in the same units. Write them down and check whether the price sits strictly between them.
  • For a CPC drawing, start at the intercept of the good the country exports. Get the other intercept by multiplying that output by the terms of trade. Label both intercepts and the slope.
  • With a curved PPC and no clear picture, compute the world-market value of each candidate production point (price times quantity, added up) and pick the largest.
  • If a definition will not come, write "the low-opportunity-cost producer" and say what is given up. That phrase is Edwards' own definition (Class 15, slide 7).

How it shows up on the final

Multiple choice. Fall 2025 Q1(b) asked what comparative advantage implies (specialize in the task you do best relative to your other tasks). The final's multiple choice reaches back to midterm comparative advantage, so expect items on opportunity cost, terms of trade ranges, and who specializes in what.

Short answers. A two-country table like the U.S. and China one is a natural Q2-style item: compute opportunity costs, name who specializes, give the range for the terms of trade, compute the terms of trade from two dollar prices.

Long trade story. Fall 2025 Q4 was a 12-point trade story built on the soybean trade war, using supply and demand with a world price (next unit) and the labor market. Its part (d) asked who won and lost; this unit gives you the language for it: the export sector loses when its terms of trade fall, and governments compensate the losers. A graph question here would be "draw the PPC and CPC for each country, label intercepts and slopes."

For any PPC or CPC drawing: label both axes with the good names, label both intercepts with numbers, write the slope on each line, and say which point the country produces at. Edwards' key answers reward the labels.

On your cheat sheet, write the terms of trade as "P of the export ÷ P of the import, in imports per unit of export," and the rule "terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs."

The table gives hours per unit instead of output per workerWhether you can flip the formula for hours.Opportunity cost of a good = its own hours ÷ the other good's hours. Fewer hours means absolute advantage.
"Country A is better at both goods. Can trade still help?"Absolute advantage in both is a trap.Yes, as long as the opportunity costs differ. Show the two costs and that A's lower one is in only one good.
Both countries have the same opportunity costs (Problem Set 1 Q2 had two people with equal costs)Whether you know gains need different opportunity costs.Neither has a comparative advantage, so there is no gain from specializing and trading. Show the two equal ratios.
"New technology doubles one country's output of both goods. Does specialization change?" (the fishhook, Fall 2025 midterm Q1(e))Whether the ratios change when both numbers scale up.Recompute. A proportional change leaves every opportunity cost the same, so nothing changes. If only one good improves, the ranking can flip, so check.
"Between what values must the terms of trade lie for both countries to want to trade?"The range rule (Class 15, slide 25).The two countries' opportunity costs of the same good, in units of the other good, such as "between 1 and 2 washing machines per ton of soybeans." Always state units.
"Soybeans sell for $400 and washing machines for $300. What are the U.S. terms of trade?"Building a relative price from two dollar prices.Price of the export ÷ price of the import: 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton. The units are "imports per unit of export."
"What are China's terms of trade?" (the other country)Taking the reciprocal for the other country.China exports machines, so flip it: 300 ÷ 400 = ¾ ton of soybeans per machine.
"A country's terms of trade improved (or worsened)." What happened?Its export price moved relative to its import price.Improve: each unit of exports buys more imports, and the CPC pivots out. Worsen: each unit buys fewer imports, and the CPC pivots in toward the PPC.
"The world price is X, which lies outside the range. What happens?" (Class 15, slide 26)Which country refuses to trade, and which way the price moves.Below the exporter's cost: the exporter refuses, excess demand pushes the price up. Above the importer's cost: the importer refuses, excess supply pushes the price down.
"At a world price of X, which country gets most of the gains?"Where the price sits inside the range.The country whose own opportunity cost is far from the price gains more. At an endpoint, the country whose cost equals the price gains nothing.
"Draw and label each country's PPC and CPC." (graph)Intercepts and slopes.PPC: both intercepts from the table, slope = minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal good. CPC: starts at the export good's intercept and has slope = minus the terms of trade. Write "the CPC lies outside the PPC" (slide 31).
"A worker wants to consume X of one good. How much of the other can she have with and without trade?"The size of the gain from trade.With trade: trade the rest of the export at the terms of trade. Without trade: read the PPC at the same amount. Subtract.
Reverse: "The CPC reaches 3 washing machines. What are the terms of trade, and what is the price of soybeans?"Work back from the graph to the price.Terms of trade = vertical intercept ÷ horizontal intercept of the CPC. Then price of the export = terms of trade × price of the import.
Reverse: "Country A exports wheat at a price of 3, and both gain. Which opportunity costs fit?"The range rule read backward.The exporter's cost must be below the price, and the importer's cost must be above it. Equal to the price means that country gains nothing.
"With a curved PPC, where does the country produce? Where does it consume?"Tangency and trade (slides 35 to 41).Produce where a line with slope minus the terms of trade touches the PPC; that point has the highest world-market value. Consume anywhere else on that line, outside the PPC.
"The country is a net importer of X. Show where it consumes." (graph)Which side of the production point consumption sits on.Consumption sits on the CPC with more X than production. The X gap is imports, the other gap is exports, and their values are equal.
"The world price of one good rises. How does production change?" or "The PPC shifts out at fixed prices. The country stops shifting toward its export good once its rising cost meets the world price, so it still makes some of both.

"Who gains and who loses from trade inside each country?" or "Who won the trade war?" Words that change the answer: "absolute" means more output or fewer hours, while "comparative" means lower opportunity cost. "Per worker" tables use other ÷ own, but "hours per unit" tables use own ÷ other. "Its terms of trade" means the price of that country's export on top, so the U.S. and China have reciprocal terms of trade. "Strictly between" gives both countries a gain, while a price equal to one country's cost gives that country nothing. "Improve" means the export buys more imports. "Straight-line PPC" means complete specialization, while "bowed-out" or "curved" means producing at a tangency and making some of both. "The country as a whole" gains, but "everyone" does not. "Planted and cannot be stored" switches you to the short-run supply and demand graph in the next unit. "Correlation" or "is associated with" is not proof of cause.

It consumes fewer goods than it produces, and the difference is its exports of goods. C lies outside the PPC, which is the gain from trade.

Full credit: production at the tangency, consumption on the CPC up and to the left of it and outside the PPC; vertical gap labeled imports of services and horizontal gap labeled exports of goods.

2 points A country's PPC between food and machines is bowed out from the origin. What does that shape say about opportunity cost, and why does it happen? Adapted from the textbook (Frank, Bernanke, Antonovics, Heffetz), trade chapter

Show answer

The opportunity cost of machines rises as the country makes more of them, so the PPC gets steeper as you move toward more machines. It happens because resources differ. The economy first moves the workers and land that are best at making machines and worst at food, so the first machines cost little food. Later it has to move resources that are good at food, so each extra machine costs more food. The textbook calls this the principle of increasing opportunity cost, or the low-hanging-fruit principle.

Full credit: rising opportunity cost, explained by resources that are not equally suited to both goods being moved in order.

One question for each row of the "Every way this can be asked" table that the questions above do not already cover.

2 points In a day, a worker in Norland catches 6 tons of fish or builds 3 computers. A worker in Sudland catches 4 tons of fish or builds 2 computers. Which is true?

(i) Norland has a comparative advantage in both goods
(ii) Sudland has a comparative advantage in computers
(iii) Neither country has a comparative advantage, so there is no gain from specializing and trading
(iv) Norland should export both goods to Sudland

Show answer

(iii). A computer costs Norland 6 ÷ 3 = 2 tons of fish and costs Sudland 4 ÷ 2 = 2 tons of fish. A ton of fish costs each country ½ computer. The opportunity costs are equal, so neither country is the low-opportunity-cost producer of either good. The only price "between" the two costs is exactly 2 tons per computer, which equals each country's own cost, so neither gains by trading.

Why the others are wrong: (i) Norland has an absolute advantage in both goods (6 against 4, 3 against 2), but no country can have a comparative advantage in both, because one good's cost is the reciprocal of the other's. (ii) Sudland's cost of a computer, 2 tons, is the same as Norland's, so it is not lower. (iv) A country that exports both goods gets nothing back, so that is not trade. With equal costs there is nothing to gain anyway.

Full credit: circle (iii), with the equal costs of 2 tons per computer shown.

4 points Technology shocks. Start from Edwards' numbers: a U.S. worker makes 2 tons of soybeans or 2 washing machines a day, and a Chinese worker makes 1 ton or 2 machines.

(a) (2 points) New equipment doubles U.S. output of both goods, so a U.S. worker makes 4 tons or 4 machines. Does the pattern of specialization change? Explain.

(b) (2 points) Instead, a new factory process raises U.S. output of washing machines only, so a U.S. worker makes 2 tons or 5 machines. Now who has the comparative advantage in each good, and between what values must the terms of trade lie?

Show answer

(a) No change. The U.S. cost of a ton is now 4 ÷ 4 = 1 machine, the same as before, and China's is still 2 ÷ 1 = 2 machines. Doubling both numbers leaves the ratio the same, so the U.S. still specializes in soybeans and China in washing machines. The range for the terms of trade is still 1 to 2 machines per ton. This is the same lesson as the fishhook on the Fall 2025 midterm.

(b) The pattern flips. The U.S. cost of a ton is now 5 ÷ 2 = 2½ machines, higher than China's 2. The U.S. cost of a machine is 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4 ton, lower than China's ½ ton. So the U.S. now has the comparative advantage in washing machines and China in soybeans, even though the U.S. is still better at both. Both gain if a ton of soybeans trades for more than 2 and fewer than 2½ washing machines, which is the same as 0.4 to 0.5 ton of soybeans per washing machine.

Full credit: (a) 1 against 2 machines per ton, unchanged, so no change; (b) 2½ against 2 machines per ton (or 0.4 against ½ ton per machine), U.S. machines and China soybeans, range 2 to 2½ machines per ton.

2 points In Edwards' example (U.S.: 2 tons or 2 machines; China: 1 ton or 2 machines), the world price settles at 1.9 washing machines per ton of soybeans. Which is true?

(i) The U.S. gets most of the gains, because 1.9 is close to China's opportunity cost of 2
(ii) China gets most of the gains, because China is the country that buys soybeans
(iii) Neither country gains, because 1.9 is so close to 2
(iv) Both countries gain equally, because any price inside the range splits the gains evenly

Show answer

(i). Take a U.S. worker who keeps 1 ton and trades the other. She gets 1 × 1.9 = 1.9 machines, against 1 machine without trade, a gain of 0.9 machine. A Chinese worker who keeps 1 machine and trades the other gets 1 ÷ 1.9 = about 0.53 ton of soybeans, against ½ ton without trade, a gain of only about 0.03 ton. The price is close to China's own cost, so China gets little out of the deal.

Why the others are wrong: (ii) being the buyer does not decide the split; the position of the price does. (iii) 1.9 is still strictly below 2, so China gains a little and the U.S. gains a lot. China would gain nothing only at exactly 2. (iv) The split is even only by coincidence; it shifts toward the country whose cost is far from the price.

Full credit: circle (i).

6 points Reading the price off the graph. Use Edwards' numbers (U.S. worker: 2 tons or 2 machines; Chinese worker: 1 ton or 2 machines). A graph shows the U.S. CPC per worker running from 2 tons of soybeans on the horizontal axis up to 3 washing machines on the vertical axis.

(a) (2 points) What are the U.S. terms of trade, and what is the slope of the CPC?

(b) (2 points) A washing machine sells for $300 in world markets. What is the world price of a ton of soybeans? Will both countries trade?

(c) (2 points) Describe China's CPC at this price. A Chinese worker who keeps 1 machine: how many tons of soybeans can she have with trade and without?

Show answer

(a) The U.S. produces 2 tons and can trade them all for 3 machines, so 1 ton buys 3 ÷ 2 = 1½ washing machines per ton. The slope of the CPC is minus the terms of trade: −1½.

(b) Terms of trade = PS ÷ PWM, so PS = 1½ × $300 = $450 a ton. Check: 450 ÷ 300 = 1½. The price of 1½ lies strictly between 1 and 2 machines per ton, so both countries trade.

(c) China produces 2 machines. One machine buys 1 ÷ 1½ = ⅔ ton, so trading both brings 2 × ⅔ = 1⅓ tons. China's CPC runs from 2 machines to 1⅓ tons, slope −1½, outside its PPC (which reaches only 1 ton). Keeping 1 machine and trading 1 gives ⅔ ton with trade, against ½ ton without, a gain of ⅙ ton.

Full credit: 1½ machines per ton and slope −1½; $450, inside the 1 to 2 range so both trade; China's CPC from 2 machines to 1⅓ tons; ⅔ against ½ ton.

2 points Country A exports wheat to Country B at a world price of 3 bolts of cloth per bushel of wheat, and both countries gain from the trade. Which pair could be their opportunity costs of a bushel of wheat?

(i) A: 4 bolts of cloth; B: 2 bolts
(ii) A: 2 bolts; B: 5 bolts
(iii) A: 2 bolts; B: 2½ bolts
(iv) A: 3 bolts; B: 4 bolts

Show answer

(ii). The price must lie strictly between the two costs, with the exporter's cost below it and the importer's above it. A sells a bushel for 3 bolts that costs it only 2, and B buys for 3 bolts a bushel that would cost it 5 to grow. Both gain.

Why the others are wrong: (i) is reversed. B would be the low-cost wheat producer, so B would export wheat, not A. (iii) B's cost of 2½ is below the price of 3, so B would rather grow its own wheat than pay 3. (iv) A's cost equals the price, so A gains nothing, which breaks "both countries gain."

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points An economist can clean her apartment in 2 hours. A housekeeper would take 3 hours. The economist earns $100 an hour doing paid economic analysis, and the housekeeper charges $25 an hour. Should the economist clean her own apartment? Explain.

Show answer

No, she should hire the housekeeper. Cleaning herself takes 2 hours that she could spend on paid work, so it costs her 2 × $100 = $200 in lost earnings. Hiring the housekeeper costs 3 × $25 = $75. She has the absolute advantage in cleaning (2 hours against 3), but her comparative advantage is in economic analysis, where she is relatively more productive. This is Lesson 2 on Edwards' final review slides (Fall 2025 final review slides, slide 16): even if you clean faster than a housekeeper, you should hire one if you are relatively more productive at paid economic analysis.

Full credit: "hire the housekeeper," with the $200 opportunity cost against the $75 fee, or the absolute versus comparative advantage reason.

2 points A news report says that a country's terms of trade improved last year. This means

(i) The price of its exports rose relative to the price of its imports, so each unit of exports buys more imports
(ii) The country exported a larger quantity of goods
(iii) The price of its imports rose relative to the price of its exports
(iv) The country signed a new trade agreement

Show answer

(i). A country's terms of trade is the relative price of its exports (Class 15, slide 23): the price of exports divided by the price of imports. "Improved" means that ratio rose, so each ton of soybeans, say, now buys more washing machines, and the CPC pivots outward around the production point.

Why the others are wrong: (ii) the terms of trade are a price, not a quantity; a country can export more at a worse price. (iii) is the definition of terms of trade getting worse. (iv) an agreement might change prices, but the terms of trade measure the prices themselves.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points In Edwards' example, the U.S. exports soybeans and China exports washing machines. Once trade opens, which group in China is most likely to lose?

(i) Chinese soybean farmers and their workers
(ii) Chinese washing machine makers and their workers
(iii) Chinese households that buy soybeans
(iv) Nobody in China, because China as a whole gains

Show answer

(i). Soybeans are China's import-competing industry. Cheaper U.S. soybeans come in, so Chinese soybean farmers sell less at a lower price, and their workers can lose jobs or wages.

Why the others are wrong: (ii) washing machines are China's export industry, which gains a bigger market. (iii) buyers of the imported good gain, because soybeans get cheaper. (iv) China as a whole gains, but the gain is not shared evenly; import-competing producers lose. It is the mirror image of the U.S., where soybean farmers win and washing machine makers lose.

Full credit: circle (i).

6 points Reading two PPCs. A graph shows two straight-line PPCs per worker per day, with wheat (bushels) on the horizontal axis and cloth (bolts) on the vertical axis. Country A's PPC runs from 6 bolts of cloth on the vertical axis to 2 bushels of wheat on the horizontal axis. Country B's PPC runs from 4 bolts to 4 bushels.

(a) (2 points) Find the slope of each PPC. Which country has the comparative advantage in wheat, and how can you tell from the graph?

(b) (2 points) Between what values, in bolts of cloth per bushel of wheat, must the terms of trade lie for both to trade?

(c) (2 points) The world price is 2 bolts per bushel. Describe B's CPC and A's CPC: intercepts and slopes.

Show answer

(a) A's PPC falls 6 bolts over a run of 2 bushels, so its slope is −6 ÷ 2 = −3. B's falls 4 bolts over 4 bushels, so its slope is −4 ÷ 4 = −1. The slope is minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal good, so a bushel of wheat costs A 3 bolts and costs B 1 bolt. B has the comparative advantage in wheat, which you can see because B's PPC is flatter. A has it in cloth (⅓ bushel per bolt against B's 1 bushel).

(b) Between the two costs of a bushel: more than 1 and less than 3 bolts of cloth per bushel.

(c) B produces 4 bushels of wheat and could trade them all for 4 × 2 = 8 bolts, so B's CPC runs from 4 bushels to 8 bolts, slope −2. A produces 6 bolts of cloth; one bolt buys 1 ÷ 2 = ½ bushel, so A's CPC runs from 6 bolts to 3 bushels, also slope −2. Both lie outside their PPCs: B could make at most 4 bolts alone, and A at most 2 bushels.

Full credit: slopes −3 (A) and −1 (B), B flatter so B has wheat; 1 to 3 bolts per bushel; B's CPC 4 bushels to 8 bolts and A's 6 bolts to 3 bushels, both with slope −2 and outside the PPCs.

2 points In Edwards' straight-line example, the U.S. grows only soybeans once it trades. A U.S. with a bowed-out PPC, facing the same world price of 1⅓ washing machines per ton, still makes some washing machines. Explain why the two cases differ.

Show answer

On a straight PPC the opportunity cost of a ton is the same at every point (1 machine for the U.S.). Every ton sells for 1⅓ machines, more than it costs, so the U.S. moves all of its workers into soybeans, and it ends at an endpoint of the PPC (Class 15, slide 39). On a bowed-out PPC the opportunity cost of soybeans rises as the country grows more, because the land and workers best suited to soybeans move first (slide 32). The country moves resources into soybeans only until the cost of one more ton rises to 1⅓ machines, the world price. Past that point, another ton would cost more machines than it sells for, so the country stops and keeps making some machines. That stopping point is the tangency A.

Full credit: constant opportunity cost gives complete specialization at an endpoint; rising opportunity cost means stopping where the PPC's slope equals minus the terms of trade, so both goods are produced.

One-minute review

Quick check

A U.S. worker makes 3 tons of soybeans or 6 machines; a Chinese worker makes 1 ton or 4 machines. Who has the comparative advantage in soybeans?

The U.S.: a ton costs it 6 ÷ 3 = 2 machines, against 4 ÷ 1 = 4 machines in China.

Soybeans sell for $500 a ton and washing machines for $250. What are the U.S. terms of trade?

500 ÷ 250 = 2 washing machines per ton, because the export's price goes on top.

The two opportunity costs of a ton are 1 and 2 machines. Can the world price stay at 2½ machines per ton?

No: China would grow its own soybeans, and the excess supply pushes the price back below 2.

Where does the U.S. CPC start, and what is its slope at terms of trade of 1⅓?

At its production point, 2 tons of soybeans, with slope −1⅓, minus the terms of trade.

With a curved PPC, where does the country produce?

At the tangency, where the PPC's slope equals minus the terms of trade, because that bundle has the highest world-market value.

Does everyone in a country gain when it opens to trade?

No: the country gains overall, but import-competing producers and their workers lose.

  • Comparative advantage means the lower opportunity cost. Opportunity cost of X = (Y per worker) ÷ (X per worker).
  • Edwards' example: U.S. 2 tons or 2 machines, China 1 ton or 2 machines. The U.S. makes soybeans (costs 1 machine against 2), China makes machines (costs ½ ton against 1).
  • Sources: factor abundance (inputs a country has a lot of) and dynamic comparative advantage (an early start).
  • PPC slope = minus the opportunity cost of the horizontal good: U.S. −1, China −2.
  • Terms of trade = relative price of a country's exports. For the U.S., PS ÷ PWM = 400 ÷ 300 = 1⅓ machines per ton. China's is ¾ ton per machine.
  • Both gain only if the terms of trade lie between the opportunity costs (1 and 2 machines per ton). Market forces push prices into that range.
  • The CPC starts at the specialized production point and has slope minus the terms of trade. U.S.: 2 tons to 2⅔ machines. China: 2 machines to 1½ tons. CPC outside PPC = gains from trade.
  • Curved PPC: produce at the tangency A (highest world value), consume at B on the CPC; export one good, import the other; specialization is incomplete.
  • Countries gain overall, but import-competing producers lose. A trade war worsens the exporter's terms of trade and pivots its CPC in.
  • Trade and growth: positive correlation, but watch reverse causation and omitted policies; geography-based evidence still points to trade helping growth.
