Question 1: multiple choice (12 points)

The title was "Multiple choices cheer multiple majors? Well, cheers!" The instruction was to circle the one correct answer. Each part is worth 2 points, and there is no partial credit on a circled letter, so read every option before circling.

2 points (a) A central element of economics is how markets help participants match the level of their marginal benefit to the level of

(i) Marginal utility
(ii) Marginal product
(iii) Marginal cost
(iv) Marginal derivative

Show answer

(iii) Marginal cost. The core rule of the course is to keep doing something until the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit equals its marginal cost (MC). In a market, demand is the MB curve and supply is the MC curve, so the equilibrium quantity is exactly where MB = MC. Marginal utility is a buyer's MB measured in utility, so it sits on the same side as MB and cannot be the match. Marginal product is extra output from one more worker, which is a production idea. "Marginal derivative" is a made-up distractor.

Full credit: circle (iii) Marginal cost.

What it tests: the MB = MC rule from the midterm half. Review it in Midterm material refresher.

2 points (b) The principle of comparative advantage implies that

(i) I should specialize in doing the task that I do best
(ii) I should specialize in all tasks that I do better than others
(iii) I should specialize in doing the task that is respected most by others
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(i) I should specialize in doing the task that I do best. Comparative advantage means having the lower opportunity cost of a task. Even a person who is faster at everything (absolute advantage in everything) gains by focusing on the one task where their edge is largest, which is the task they do best relative to everything else they could do. Option (ii) describes absolute advantage, which is the classic trap: doing every task you are better at would mean doing everything yourself and giving up the gains from trade. Option (iii) has nothing to do with costs. Since (ii) and (iii) are wrong, (iv) is wrong too.

Full credit: circle (i).

What it tests: comparative versus absolute advantage. Review in Midterm material refresher and, for countries, Gains from international trade.

2 points (c) In economics, the word "capital" means

(i) Productive physical assets like durable equipment and structures
(ii) Financial assets like a bank account or a mutual fund investment
(iii) Accumulated savings that can be invested across international borders
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(iv) All of the above. In the capital and interest class, capital means physical capital such as machines and buildings, but the same word also covers the financial claims that fund those machines, such as bonds, stocks, and bank deposits. In the international macro class, "capital flows" means savings moving across borders to buy assets in another country. Every option is a meaning the course uses, so the answer is all of them.

Full credit: circle (iv).

What it tests: the definition of capital. Taught in Capital, interest, and present value and Exchange rates and international macro.

2 points (d) If the yen-dollar exchange rate falls from ¥160 per U.S. dollar to ¥150 per U.S. dollar, then we say

(i) The yen has appreciated and the dollar has depreciated
(ii) The yen has depreciated and the dollar has appreciated
(iii) Nothing has happened
(iv) We cannot tell based on this information

Show answer

(i) The yen has appreciated and the dollar has depreciated. The rate is quoted as yen per dollar, which is the price of one dollar in yen. One dollar used to buy 160 yen and now buys only 150 yen, so the dollar is worth less: it depreciated. The flip side is that each yen now buys more of a dollar (1/150 of a dollar instead of 1/160), so the yen appreciated. The trick is that a smaller number means a weaker dollar here, because the number is the dollar's price in yen.

Full credit: circle (i).

What it tests: reading an exchange rate quote. Taught in Exchange rates and international macro.

2 points (e) Which of these is an important aspect of "social infrastructure," the basic building blocks of a society that allows markets to function well?

(i) The rule of law
(ii) Property rights
(iii) Protection from government
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(iv) All of the above. His final review slides list exactly these three as the institutions markets need: rule of law, property rights, and protection from government. People invest and trade only when contracts are enforced, when what they build stays theirs, and when the government itself will not seize it. The option that looks odd, "protection from government," is on his list on purpose.

Full credit: circle (iv).

What it tests: social infrastructure as a source of long-run growth. Taught in Long-run growth.

2 points (f) Compared to perfect competition, a monopolist

(i) Raises price and raises quantity
(ii) Reduces price and raises quantity
(iii) Reduces price and reduces quantity
(iv) Raises price and reduces quantity

Show answer

(iv) Raises price and reduces quantity. A monopolist produces where marginal revenue (MR) equals MC. Because it must cut the price on every unit to sell one more, MR lies below the demand curve, so MR hits MC at a smaller quantity than demand does. With a smaller quantity, the price read off the demand curve is higher. A competitive market would produce out to where demand meets MC, which is more output at a lower price.

Full credit: circle (iv).

What it tests: the monopoly result from the midterm half. Review in Midterm material refresher.

Question 2: short answers (10 points)

The title was "To optimize, set Marginal Short Questions = Marginal Short Answers." Each part is worth 2 points and has a small answer box.

Setup for (a) and (b): In 2021, The Economist reported that the average price of a McDonald's Big Mac in the U.S. was $1.60 in 1986 and $5.65 in 2021, or about 3.5 times higher. The consumer price index (CPI, with 1982 to 1984 = 100) was about 110 in 1986 and roughly 271 in 2021, or about 2.5 times higher.

2 points (a) Has the price of a Big Mac increased, decreased, or stayed the same in inflation-adjusted real terms? That is, if your income increased at the rate of inflation, could you buy fewer, more, or the same numbers of Big Macs today vs. 1986? Explain.

Show answer

Increased. The CPI measures how much the general price level rose, so it tells you how much an income that kept up with inflation grew: about 2.5 times. The Big Mac's price grew about 3.5 times. Because the burger's price grew faster than prices in general, it costs more in real terms, and an inflation-matched income buys fewer Big Macs today than in 1986.

Extra arithmetic (not in the key), to see the size: $1.60 × (271 ÷ 110) = $3.94. That is what the 1986 Big Mac would cost in 2021 if it had only kept up with inflation. The actual price was $5.65, which is about 43% more than $3.94. You do not need this number for credit.

Full credit (key): "The CPI rose by a factor of 2.5, while the Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5. Therefore the real price of a Big Mac has increased." State the direction word and compare the two growth factors.

What it tests: real versus nominal prices using the CPI. Taught in GDP, prices, and unemployment.

2 points (b) Is the production and serving of a Big Mac something that could be automated in factories, or is it something that is done by human workers? Discuss the implications for marginal costs over time, given that factory automation has often driven falling costs.

Show answer

Mostly done by human workers, so its marginal cost has not fallen much. A Big Mac is cooked and served to order in a restaurant by workers, with a building, a grill, and a counter. That kind of work cannot be moved onto a factory line the way cars or electronics can. Factory automation lowers the marginal cost (MC) of goods because machines raise output per worker. Without that, the MC of a Big Mac stays tied to wages and rent, which rise over time. This is part of why its real price rose in (a).

Full credit (key): "No," it "is not something that can be automated. It requires workers and a facility. This means that mechanization cannot reduce costs much." Say no, name the workers and facility, and connect it to MC not falling.

What it tests: MC reasoning from the midterm half, linked to productivity. Review MC in Midterm material refresher.

Setup for (c), (d), and (e): The present value (PV) of a bond or a claim to capital that pays Ft in year t over 3 years, when the nominal interest rate is i, is

PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3

A bond's payouts Ft are fixed. For a unit of capital, Ft is its marginal revenue product of capital, which is marginal revenue times the marginal product of capital: MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

2 points (c) Suppose the interest rate i increases. What happens to the price of this bond?

Show answer

The bond's price falls. The price of a bond equals the present value of its payments. Each payment is divided by (1 + i) raised to a power. When i rises, every denominator gets bigger, so every term gets smaller, and the PV falls. In words, a fixed future dollar is worth less today when you could earn more interest on money instead.

An extra example (not in the key): a bond that pays $100 in each of 3 years is worth $300.00 at i = 0, $272.32 at i = 5%, and $248.69 at i = 10%.

Full credit (key): the price of a bond with fixed nominal payments "must fall because the right-hand side decreases when the interest rate i increases."

What it tests: bond prices move opposite to interest rates. Taught in Capital, interest, and present value.

2 points (d) Suppose i = 0. Express the price of this bond in terms of F1, F2, and F3.

Show answer

Price = F1 + F2 + F3. With i = 0, every denominator is 1 + 0 = 1, and 1 raised to any power is still 1. So every payment counts at face value and the price is just the sum of the payments.

Full credit (key): "If the interest rate i is zero, then 1 + i = 1 and we are left with p = PV = F1 + F2 + F3." Show that 1 + i = 1, then write the sum.

What it tests: plugging into the PV formula. Taught in Capital, interest, and present value.

2 points (e) What happens to the price of a unit of capital when capital's future marginal product falls, for example because of an expected future reduction in labor?

Show answer

The price of capital falls. A machine is worth the present value of the income it will earn, and that income each year is MRPK = MR × MPK. Capital is more productive when it has more workers to use it, so fewer future workers means a lower future marginal product of capital (MPK). A lower MPK lowers each future Ft, which lowers the PV, which is the price of the capital.

Full credit (key): less future labor means the future marginal product of capital "would fall, and it would pull down the present value and price of a unit of capital."

What it tests: the price of capital as a PV of MRPK, linked to the immigration story. Taught in Capital, interest, and present value.

Question 3: the Keynesian cross (24 points)

The title was "Risks to the U.S. economy in 2025: We Keynesian Cross Ourselves." This is the biggest question on the exam, 40% of the points. Planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) is total planned spending: PAE = C + I + G + NX, where C is consumption, I is investment, G is government purchases, and NX is net exports. Y is real GDP (output). The marginal propensity to consume (mpc) is the share of each extra dollar of income that households spend.

Setup: In 2025, U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion. A reasonable estimate of the mpc is 1/3, and the following system models PAE in trillions of dollars:

C = 10 + (1/3)Y   I = 6   G = 5   NX = −1 (net exports are negative $1 trillion)

2 points (a) Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find the level of PAE when Y = 0, which is the vertical intercept in the Keynesian cross diagram. Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Add the four pieces: PAE = (10 + (1/3)Y) + 6 + 5 + (−1).
  2. Add the numbers that do not depend on Y: 10 + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20.
  3. So PAE = 20 + (1/3)Y. At Y = 0, PAE = 20.

The 20 is autonomous expenditure, the spending that happens even at zero income. It is where the PAE line starts on the vertical axis.

Full credit (key): "PAE = C + I + G + NX = 10 + 1/3 Y + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20 + 1/3 Y. When Y = 0, we have PAE = 20, and that is the vertical intercept." Write the combined line, then the intercept.

2 points (b) Using your answer to part (a), set PAE = Y and solve to find the short-run equilibrium level of Y, which is the horizontal coordinate of the spot where PAE crosses the 45° line. Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Set spending equal to output: 20 + (1/3)Y = Y.
  2. Subtract (1/3)Y from both sides: 20 = (2/3)Y.
  3. Multiply both sides by 3/2: Y = 30.

Check: at Y = 30, PAE = 20 + 30/3 = 20 + 10 = 30, which equals Y. Equilibrium output is $30 trillion, which matches the story's "U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion."

Full credit (key): "20 + 1/3 Y = Y. Combining terms at far right, we find that 20 = 2/3 Y, which means that Y = 30."

4 points (c) Graph the PAE line and the 45-degree line on the axes below, and show the equilibrium level of total output in the short run without any shocks. Label intercepts, etc. (The exam's axes ran from 0 to 35 on both, marked every 5.)

Show answer
  1. Draw the 45-degree line from the origin through (35, 35). Every point on it has PAE = Y. Use a ruler and check that it passes through (10, 10) and (30, 30).
  2. Draw PAE: start at 20 on the vertical axis. It has slope 1/3, so it passes through (15, 25) and (30, 30). Label it with its equation.
  3. Mark the crossing at (30, 30) with a dot and draw dotted lines down to Y = 30 and across to PAE = 30.

Full credit (key): the key's graph shows the 45° line from the origin, the PAE line starting at 20 and flatter than the 45° line, both labeled, and dotted lines from the crossing to 30 on each axis.

Setup for (d) to (j): At the end of 2025, there were concerns about the U.S. economy. Tariffs may raise domestic prices and reduce consumption. Investment in data centers fueled recent GDP growth, but the AI boom could be a bubble that bursts. Immigration enforcement detained and removed immigrant workers, and the net effects remain murky. Suppose these headwinds produce this situation in early 2026:

C' = 8 + (1/3)Y   I' = 5   G = 5   NX' = 0   PAE' = C' + I' + G + NX'

2 points (d) Combine the expenditure terms in PAE' and find the level of PAE' when Y = 0, which is the vertical intercept. Show your work.

Show answer
  1. PAE' = (8 + (1/3)Y) + 5 + 5 + 0.
  2. Autonomous part: 8 + 5 + 5 + 0 = 18.
  3. So PAE' = 18 + (1/3)Y, and at Y = 0, PAE' = 18.

Full credit (key): "PAE' = C' + I' + G + NX' = 8 + 1/3 Y + 5 + 5 + 0 = 18 + 1/3 Y. When Y = 0, we have PAE' = 18, and that is the vertical intercept."

2 points (e) Using your answer to part (d), set PAE' = Y and solve to find the short-run equilibrium level of Y. Show your work.

Show answer
  1. 18 + (1/3)Y = Y.
  2. 18 = (2/3)Y.
  3. Y = 18 × 3/2 = 27.

Check: 18 + 27/3 = 18 + 9 = 27.

Full credit (key): "18 + 1/3 Y = Y. Combining terms at far right, we find that 18 = 2/3 Y, which means that Y = 27."

4 points (f) On the axes below, graph the original PAE line from part (a), the new PAE' line from part (d), and the 45-degree line. Show equilibria and label intercepts, etc.

Show answer
  1. Draw the 45-degree line and the original PAE1 = 20 + (1/3)Y exactly as in (c).
  2. Draw PAE2 = 18 + (1/3)Y starting at 18 with the same slope. It passes through (27, 27) and (33, 29). Keep it parallel: the gap between the lines is 2 at every Y.
  3. Mark both equilibria with dots and dotted lines to the axes: 30 and 30 for the old one, 27 and 27 for the new one.
  4. Label both intercepts (20 and 18), both lines with their equations, and add a downward arrow between the lines.

Full credit (key): the key's graph shows the 45° line, PAE1 labeled "PAE = 20 + 1/3 Y" and PAE2 labeled "PAE' = 18 + 1/3 Y" as parallel lines, intercepts 20 and 18, dotted lines at 30 and 27 on both axes, and downward arrows showing the shift.

2 points (g) By how much did PAE change between (a) and (d)? Express your answer in trillions of dollars and describe it as an increase, decrease, or no change. Explain fully, making clear what the arithmetic actually means about planned aggregate spending, and describing how it appears in the graph.

Show answer

PAE decreased by $2 trillion. Go piece by piece. Autonomous consumption fell from 10 to 8, a drop of 2, which is the tariffs hitting households. Investment fell from 6 to 5, a drop of 1, which is the data-center boom cooling. Net exports rose from −1 to 0, an increase of 1. Government purchases did not change. The net change is −2 − 1 + 1 = −2. On the graph this is a parallel downward shift of the PAE line by 2 at every level of Y, which you see as the intercept moving from 20 to 18.

"Change in PAE" here means the shift of the line at a given Y, which is the change in autonomous spending. Do not read PAE at the two equilibria (30 and 27) and answer 3. That 3 is the answer to (h).

Full credit (key): PAE "decreased by $2 trillion" because autonomous consumption "fell by $2 trillion, and investment fell by $1 trillion, while net exports actually rose by $1 trillion," a net reduction of $2 trillion, "which is a vertical drop in PAE as shown in the diagram." Give the number, the direction, the three components, and the vertical shift.

2 points (h) By how much did GDP change between (a) and (d)? Express your answer in trillions of dollars and describe it as an increase, decrease, or no change. Explain fully, making clear what the arithmetic actually means about GDP, and describing how it appears in the graph.

Show answer

GDP decreased by $3 trillion, from 30 to 27. The PAE line dropped by 2. Because the PAE line slopes upward with slope 1/3, its crossing with the 45-degree line slides down and to the left by more than the vertical drop. On the graph the equilibrium moves from (30, 30) to (27, 27), a 3-unit move to the left on the Y axis.

Full credit (key): GDP "decreased by $3 trillion" because PAE decreased by $2 trillion, a vertical drop in the PAE curve, "and because the PAE line slopes upward, its intersection with the 45° line falls and/or shifts left by $3 trillion."

2 points (i) Are your answers to (g) and (h) the same, or are they different? If they are the same, state as much and explain why. If they are different, identify which one is larger in magnitude, and explain why. Explain fully, referencing aspects of the model.

Show answer

Different. The change in GDP ($3 trillion) is larger than the change in PAE ($2 trillion). The reason is the marginal propensity to consume. When spending falls by 2, incomes fall by 2. Households then cut their own consumption by 1/3 of that lost income, which cuts other people's incomes, who cut their spending too, and so on. Adding up all the rounds gives the multiplier, 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 1 ÷ (2/3) = 1.5. So the change in GDP is 1.5 × 2 = 3.

Full credit (key): the answers are "different"; the change in GDP is "larger at $3 trillion" because of "the nonzero marginal propensity to consume out of income in the consumption function, which is equal to 1/3 here. A nonzero mpc creates a multiplier equal to 1/(1−mpc)." Name the mpc, give the multiplier formula, and say which is larger.

2 points (j) Suppose Congress could pass a temporary spending bill in 2026 that could quickly change G by enough to fully counteract the impacts that you described in parts (d) to (f). How big of a change in G would that be? Would it be a decrease, an increase, or no change in G? Explain fully.

Show answer

Increase G by $2 trillion. The shock lowered autonomous PAE by 2. Raising G by 2 puts the intercept back at 18 + 2 = 20, so the PAE line returns to exactly where it was, and equilibrium GDP returns to 30. You do not need to raise G by 3, because the multiplier works on the increase in G just as it worked on the shock: 1.5 × 2 = 3 of extra GDP, which undoes the 3 that was lost.

Full credit (key): "The reduction in PAE was $2 trillion, and thus to offset the effect of that shock, Congress would like to raise G by $2 trillion. As long as the marginal propensity to consume remains the same across these offsetting shocks, then government could match the decrease in PAE with an equal and opposite increase in PAE deriving from G."

What Q3 tests as a whole: building PAE, solving PAE = Y, drawing the cross to scale, shifting it, and the multiplier. Taught in Recessions and the Keynesian cross; part (j) is the fiscal policy logic in Fiscal policy.

Question 4: soybeans and the trade war (12 points)

The title was "Oy, Soybeans! So you been exporting?" It mixes the trade graph from Class 16 with the labor market from Class 12.

4 points (a) The supply and demand diagram shows the domestic U.S. market for soybeans. Adapt the diagram to show roughly half of the quantity supplied as exports to the world market, meaning the domestic quantity supplied exceeds the domestic quantity demanded, at a world price that compensates suppliers for their marginal costs. Show the world price and the quantity of exports. Briefly explain what you drew.

Show answer
  1. Draw a horizontal world price line Pw above the domestic equilibrium price P1. A country exports only when the world pays more than the home market would.
  2. Where Pw hits demand, drop a dotted line to the axis: that is domestic quantity demanded, QD.
  3. Where Pw hits supply, drop a dotted line: that is domestic quantity supplied, QS.
  4. Bracket the horizontal gap QS − QD and label it "exports." Place Pw high enough that the gap is about half of QS.

Full credit (key): "In a market for an exported good, the world price Pw must be higher than the price that equates domestic supply and demand. The horizontal gap at the world price shows exports." Label Pw, QD, QS, and the exports gap.

Setup for (b): In spring 2025, the U.S. escalated its trade war with China, which had been the main importer of U.S. soybeans. In May, China responded to higher U.S. tariffs with its own steep tariffs, which practically halted its imports of U.S. soybeans. Assume all U.S. soybean exports had gone to China and no other country will import U.S. soybeans at any price.

2 points (b) Producers plant soybeans in the spring and harvest in the fall. If producers planted before the trade war escalation and cannot change that quantity, and if soybeans cannot be stored or destroyed, how much must the price adjust, if at all? Explain and show the price level after the trade war escalation on the graph in part (a).

Show answer

The price must fall a lot, all the way to Ptw, the price on the domestic demand curve at quantity QS. Once the crop is planted and cannot be stored or destroyed, short-run supply is a vertical line at QS. With no exports, the whole crop must be sold to American buyers. They will buy that much only at a much lower price, so you read the new price straight off the demand curve at QS. It ends up below the old no-trade price P1, because farmers planted for the export market and the home market now has to absorb all of it.

Full credit (key): if suppliers are stuck with QS and world demand falls to zero while they cannot store or destroy their soybeans, the price "would fall by a lot, all the way to Ptw, read off the demand curve at quantity QS, a really big reduction." Mark Ptw on the graph below P1.

What (a) and (b) test: exports at a world price and perfectly inelastic short-run supply. Taught in Trade in supply and demand.

Setup for (c): Consider the labor market for workers in U.S. soybean production. The labor supply curve reflects workers' marginal cost of supplying their labor. The labor demand curve is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MR is the marginal revenue from selling one more bushel and MPL (the marginal product of labor) is how many extra bushels one more worker produces.

4 points (c) Illustrate the situation in the labor market before and after the trade war escalation as we have described it up to now. Show any and all demand and supply curves and equilibrium wages and employment. Briefly explain.

Show answer

Labor demand shifts inward, so both the wage and employment fall. The trade war cut the price farmers get for soybeans, which is their marginal revenue. Since labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL, a lower MR lowers the value of what each worker produces at every level of employment, so the whole demand curve shifts down and to the left. Workers' willingness to work at each wage did not change, so labor supply stays where it is.

Full credit (key): "The trade war escalation significantly reduces the price or marginal revenue of soybeans, and thus it shifts the entire labor demand curve, the marginal revenue product, MRPL = MR · MPL, inward or downward." The key's graph shows Supply, Demand1, Demand2 to its left, arrows, and w1, w2, L1, L2 marked with dotted lines.

What it tests: labor demand as MRPL and what shifts it. Taught in Labor and wages.

Setup for (d): In late October 2025, the U.S. and China reached a truce in which China agreed to buy more U.S. soybeans. By early December, the U.S. announced a one-time government payment of $12 billion to U.S. soybean farmers, and China reported that its trade surplus reached a record high.

2 points (d) What do you think these three late-2025 events reveal about the winners and losers of this round of the trade war, and given what you illustrated earlier, why? Recall that imports from China are usually durable manufactured goods, not perishable food.

Show answer

The U.S. lost this round and China won. Even after the truce, the U.S. government felt it had to pay farmers $12 billion, which shows that U.S. soybean farmers, and through (c) their workers, took real losses. Their crop was perishable and already planted, so they had no way to wait out the dispute. China's exports are mostly durable manufactured goods that do not spoil, so Chinese firms could hold them or sell them elsewhere. A record Chinese trade surplus suggests they found other buyers. There may have been losers in China too, but the evidence points to the U.S. side bearing more of the cost.

Full credit (key): the events suggest "the U.S. lost this round and China won," because the U.S. government "still felt it had to compensate the other losers, U.S. farmers." Industrial goods "do not degrade quickly like agricultural goods do," and China's record surplus "suggests its goods found new demanders." Name a winner and a loser and give the perishable versus durable reason.

What it tests: who bears the cost of a trade war, using the graphs from (b) and (c). Taught in Trade in supply and demand.
