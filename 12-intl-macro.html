Class 23 · International macroeconomics

Exchange rates and international macro

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, and it is set by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. This unit shows what moves that price, why a higher interest rate makes a currency stronger and lowers net exports, and why a country's trade balance is the mirror image of its capital flows. On the Fall 2025 final it was multiple choice (d): the yen-dollar rate falling from ¥160 to ¥150 per dollar means the yen appreciated and the dollar depreciated. His final review deck also made it Lesson 6, with the market for dollars and the balance of payments.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Thursday, November 19, 2026.

The ideas, explained

Why exchange rates belong in the short-run model

Net exports, NX, are exports minus imports, and they are one of the four parts of planned aggregate expenditure (PAE): PAE = C + IP + G + NX, where C is consumption, IP is planned investment, and G is government purchases. So anything that changes NX shifts the PAE line in the Keynesian cross and changes GDP (Y) in the short run. Exchange rates are one of the main things that move NX, and the real interest rate r is one of the main things that move exchange rates. That is how this class connects to Class 22: his slide sums it up as PAE(r) = C(r) + I(r) + G + NX(r), where all three of C, I, and NX fall when r rises.

The nominal exchange rate is a price

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. His examples: "It currently takes 0.86 euros to buy 1 U.S. dollar," so the price of $1 is €0.86, and £0.76 per $1 for the British pound. His slides always quote the price of the dollar in foreign currency: euros per dollar, yen per dollar, yuan per dollar. That is the number on the vertical axis of every foreign exchange graph he draws.

The same exchange rate can be quoted the other way around, as dollars per unit of foreign currency. The two quotes are reciprocals: divide 1 by one to get the other.

Appreciation and depreciation

From his slide 18:

Appreciation of the dollar: the price of the dollar in some foreign currency rises. Depreciation of the dollar: the price of the dollar in some foreign currency falls. When one currency appreciates against another, the other depreciates against it.

A strong currency is one whose price in other currencies is high; a weak currency is one whose price is low. "Stronger" is another word for appreciated.

The safe way to answer any appreciation question is to ask one thing: whose price went up? That currency appreciated.

  • ¥160 per $1 falling to ¥150 per $1 (the Fall 2025 question). This number is the price of a dollar, measured in yen. It fell, so the dollar got cheaper: the dollar depreciated. A dollar now buys fewer yen, which means a yen buys more of a dollar: the yen appreciated. In the other convention, the price of a yen rose from $0.00625 to $0.006667. Same event, read from the other side.
  • ¥150 per $1 rising to ¥160 per $1. The price of the dollar rose, so the dollar appreciated and the yen depreciated.
  • $1.10 per €1 rising to $1.20 per €1. This number is the price of a euro. It rose, so the euro appreciated and the dollar depreciated, even though the number went up.

Before you circle anything, write down which currency is in the denominator ("per $1" or "per €1"). The number is the price of that currency. Up means it appreciated; down means it depreciated. The other currency does the opposite.

The percent changes are not mirror images. From ¥160 to ¥150, the dollar lost (160 − 150) ÷ 160 = 6.25% of its yen value, while the yen gained 160 ÷ 150 − 1 = 6.67% in dollar value. Each is measured from its own starting price.

What appreciation does to trade

His slide puts it plainly: "Currency appreciation raises export prices and tends to reduce exports." When the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods cost more in foreign currency and foreign goods cost fewer dollars, so U.S. exports fall, imports rise, and NX falls. When the dollar depreciates, the opposite happens and NX rises. Numbers for the move from ¥160 to ¥150 per dollar (a dollar depreciation):

  • A $1,000 U.S. computer costs a Japanese buyer ¥160,000 at the old rate and ¥150,000 at the new one. U.S. exports get cheaper in Japan, so they rise.
  • A ¥30,000 Japanese good costs an American 30,000 ÷ 160 = $187.50 at the old rate and 30,000 ÷ 150 = $200.00 at the new one. Imports get more expensive, so they fall.

The foreign exchange market for dollars

There is a market for each currency against every other currency, and the markets for one currency (like the dollar) tend to move together. His standard picture is the market where dollars trade for euros:

  • Demanders of dollars are foreigners who want to buy American goods, services, or assets. To buy American, they must convert their currency into dollars. They buy more when the dollar is cheap, so demand slopes down.
  • Suppliers of dollars are Americans who want to buy foreign goods, services, or assets. To buy foreign, they must convert dollars into foreign currency. They buy more when the euro is cheap in dollars, which is when the dollar's price in euros is high, so supply slopes up.
  • The equilibrium exchange rate is the price of the dollar that makes the quantity of dollars demanded equal the quantity supplied.

A shift in either curve changes the exchange rate. If demand for dollars rises or supply of dollars falls, the dollar appreciates. If demand falls or supply rises, the dollar depreciates.

Interest rates and the exchange rate

International investors prefer higher real interest rates. When the U.S. real interest rate rises relative to Japan's, savers in Japan want U.S. bonds instead of Japanese bonds (they demand more dollars), and savers in the U.S. also want U.S. bonds instead of Japanese bonds (they supply fewer dollars). Both effects raise the price of the dollar. His chart of U.S. and Japanese interest rates shows U.S. rates climbing from near zero in 2022 to above 4% by early 2023 while Japan's stayed near zero, and his chart of yen per dollar shows the dollar rising to about ¥150 in late 2022.

Run the same logic in reverse for his current-events slides from December 2025, when the Bank of Japan (BOJ) hinted at raising its target interest rate. A higher Japanese real rate pulls capital into Japan: demand for dollars falls and supply of dollars rises, so the dollar depreciates against the yen. His slide adds that this reduces prices of U.S. assets (and raises U.S. interest rates), which is why it was reported as a threat to U.S. markets. Why would the BOJ do it? For the same reason any central bank raises rates: to reduce inflation by lowering PAE, through lower investment (Class 22) and through an appreciated currency and lower net exports (Class 23).

This is a key step in why NX falls when r rises, which he summarizes: if r in the U.S. increases, the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods become more expensive than foreign goods, imports rise, exports fall, and NX falls. It also means monetary policy works through the exchange rate. His final review makes the same point for expansionary policy: when the Fed cuts real interest rates, it raises investment and parts of consumption like new car purchases, and it also raises NX through a currency depreciation.

The balance of payments: NX = −NKI

The balance of payments is an accounting of the supply and demand for dollars used in international transactions. At the equilibrium exchange rate, dollars demanded equal dollars supplied:

EX + KI = IM + KO, where EX is exports (foreigners buy U.S. products), KI is capital inflows (foreigners buy U.S. assets), IM is imports (Americans buy foreign products), and KO is capital outflows (Americans buy foreign assets). Rearranging, (EX − IM) + (KI − KO) = 0, so NX = −NKI, where NKI = KI − KO is net capital inflows.

In his words: if you export on net, you buy assets abroad; if you import on net, you have to sell some of your assets abroad. A trade deficit and a net capital inflow are the same thing seen from two sides. His Bureau of Economic Analysis chart shows U.S. NX and net capital inflows as near-perfect mirror images since 1960, with NX falling to around −$750 billion in the mid-2000s and capital inflows rising by about the same amount.

What determines net capital inflows:

  • The U.S. real interest rate. If r rises, American assets look better than foreign ones, so NKI rises.
  • Foreign real interest rates. If they rise, American assets look worse by comparison, so NKI falls.
  • Tastes for assets. If Americans or foreigners find American assets more attractive at a given r, NKI rises.

Since NX = −NKI, a rise in r raises NKI and so lowers NX; a fall in r raises NX.

The long run: S + NKI = I

In earlier classes the long-run real interest rate r* was set where national saving equals investment, with NX* = 0. Now allow trade. Start from Y* = C* + I* + G + NX*. Rearrange to (Y* − C* − G) − NX* = I*. National saving is S = Y − C − G, and NX = −NKI, so

S* + NKI* = I*. In the long run, r* adjusts so that domestic saving plus net capital inflows equals investment. If a country invests more than it saves, foreigners must fund the difference (NKI > 0), and the country runs a trade deficit (NX < 0).

Both saving and net capital inflows rise with r, so the S + NKI curve slopes up, and investment slopes down. His slide 9 applies this: the U.S. has looked like a country whose investment exceeds its saving since the 1960s, so it imports capital and runs trade deficits, and "China's trade surpluses reflect its very high domestic saving."

So the best way to figure out what happens to NX, short run or long run, is to figure out what happens to NKI. NX* = −NKI*.

Application: higher tariffs on many goods

His big application (with the U.S. tariffs "pending a Supreme Court decision" at the time) assumes the economy starts in long-run equilibrium, the U.S. raises tariffs on many goods for a long time, and other countries do not retaliate.

  • The foreign exchange market. Tariffs reduce U.S. purchases of foreign goods, which reduces the supply of dollars. Supply shifts left, so the dollar appreciates.
  • Two forces on NX. At a given exchange rate, imports fall, so NX would rise. But the stronger dollar reduces exports and raises imports, which lowers NX.
  • The net effect. Ask what happens to NKI. Nothing does: interest rates and tastes for assets did not change. So nothing happens to NX, in the short run or the long run. In the long-run diagram neither S + NKI nor I moves, so r* and I* do not change either.

How seriously to take this: will tariffs have literally no effect on NX? "Almost surely not: All models are approximations." But there is a clear force against the direct effect, the stronger dollar. As long as NKI does not respond much to the exchange rate, which he calls realistic, the effect on NX is small. The model gets exactly zero only because it assumes NKI does not respond at all.

Why the U.S. runs a persistent trade deficit

His FRED chart shows U.S. net exports negative as a share of GDP for decades, dipping to about −6% in the mid-2000s. Three popular explanations are mistaken: that U.S. goods are low quality or Americans are not productive enough, that Americans have strong tastes for foreign goods, and that foreign countries are widely protectionist. The problem with all three: they predict a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit. Lower quality, for example, means foreigners demand fewer dollars and Americans supply more, so the dollar simply depreciates until trade adjusts.

The deficit has to come from the capital side, because NX* = −NKI*. A large negative NX* means a large positive NKI*: U.S. assets look highly attractive, perhaps because people expect risky U.S. assets to pay off especially well (his examples: tech in the 1990s, housing in the early 2000s, "AI now?"). Foreigners buy more U.S. assets and Americans buy fewer foreign ones, the dollar appreciates, and NX falls.

Real exchange rates and the law of one price

His slide 9 states the law of one price: if transport costs are minimal and there are no trade barriers, a traded good should cost the same everywhere once you convert currencies. His example: a computer priced at $1,000 in the U.S. should be priced at ¥155,960 in Japan when the exchange rate is ¥155.96 per $1, because 1,000 × 155.96 = 155,960. Purchasing power parity (PPP) extends this to say inflation rates in two countries should be related to the exchange rate between them; he leaves the details for ECON 100B.

The textbook makes this precise with the real exchange rate, the price of the average domestic good relative to the average foreign good, with both in the same currency:

Real exchange rate = (e × P) ÷ Pf, where e is the nominal exchange rate in foreign currency per dollar, P is the U.S. price, and Pf is the foreign price. A real exchange rate of 1 means the law of one price holds. Above 1 means U.S. goods are relatively expensive, which tends to lower NX.

With his computer: (155.96 × 1,000) ÷ 155,960 = 1. If the same computer sold for ¥140,000 in Japan, the real exchange rate would be 155,960 ÷ 140,000 = 1.114, so the U.S. version is about 11% more expensive and Japanese buyers would prefer their own.

Fixed versus flexible exchange rates

Today most exchange rates are determined in markets; these are flexible (floating) exchange rates. Some countries today, and many in the past, use fixed exchange rates, which are maintained by a central bank that buys and sells foreign reserves. His example is China, which had a fixed exchange rate with the dollar from 1999 to 2005 and now has a floating rate. His FRED chart shows the yuan-per-dollar rate flat near 8.3 during the fixed period and then falling toward 6 to 7 once it floated, which means the yuan appreciated against the dollar.

The textbook explains how the buying and selling works. If the fixed rate is set above the market rate for the dollar, more dollars are offered than demanded at that price. To hold the rate, the country's central bank (China's, in his example) must buy up the excess dollars with its own currency, adding to its reserves of dollars. If the fixed rate were below the market rate, more dollars would be demanded than offered, so that central bank would have to sell dollars from its reserves, and it could run out.

Worked examples

Example: the Fall 2025 yen question, both ways

The yen-dollar rate falls from ¥160 per $1 to ¥150 per $1. Which currency appreciated? State it in both conventions and give the percent changes.

  1. The quote is "per $1," so the number is the price of a dollar in yen. It fell, so the dollar depreciated.
  2. Flip to dollars per yen: 1 ÷ 160 = $0.00625 and 1 ÷ 150 = $0.006667. The price of a yen rose, so the yen appreciated.
  3. Dollar: (150 − 160) ÷ 160 = −6.25%. Yen: 0.006667 ÷ 0.00625 − 1 = 160 ÷ 150 − 1 = +6.67%.

Answer: The yen appreciated (by 6.67%) and the dollar depreciated (by 6.25%). This is answer (i) on the Fall 2025 final.

Example: converting and reading a pound quote

The pound goes from £0.76 per $1 to £0.80 per $1. Convert both to dollars per pound and say what happened.

  1. 1 ÷ 0.76 = $1.3158 per £1. 1 ÷ 0.80 = $1.25 per £1.
  2. The price of a dollar in pounds rose (0.76 to 0.80), so the dollar appreciated.
  3. The price of a pound in dollars fell ($1.3158 to $1.25), so the pound depreciated.

Answer: Dollar appreciated, pound depreciated. A British tourist now pays more pounds for a $100 hotel night: £76 before, £80 after.

Example: what a depreciation does to prices of traded goods

The dollar goes from ¥160 to ¥150. A Japanese buyer wants a $1,000 U.S. laptop; an American wants a ¥30,000 Japanese rice cooker.

  1. Laptop in yen: 1,000 × 160 = ¥160,000 before; 1,000 × 150 = ¥150,000 after. It is ¥10,000 cheaper in Japan.
  2. Rice cooker in dollars: 30,000 ÷ 160 = $187.50 before; 30,000 ÷ 150 = $200.00 after. It is $12.50 more expensive in the U.S.
  3. U.S. exports become cheaper abroad and imports more expensive at home.

Answer: The dollar depreciation tends to raise U.S. exports and lower imports, so NX rises and the PAE line shifts up.

Example: balance of payments arithmetic

In a year (hypothetical, $ trillions), U.S. exports are 3.2, imports are 4.1, and capital outflows (Americans buying foreign assets) are 1.3. Find capital inflows, NX, and NKI.

  1. EX + KI = IM + KO, so 3.2 + KI = 4.1 + 1.3 = 5.4, and KI = 2.2.
  2. NX = EX − IM = 3.2 − 4.1 = −0.9.
  3. NKI = KI − KO = 2.2 − 1.3 = +0.9.
  4. Check: NX = −NKI, and −0.9 = −(+0.9).

Answer: KI = $2.2 trillion, a $0.9 trillion trade deficit, matched by $0.9 trillion of net capital inflows.

Example: saving, capital inflows, and investment

National saving is $4.6 trillion and investment is $5.5 trillion (hypothetical). What are net capital inflows and net exports?

  1. S + NKI = I, so NKI = 5.5 − 4.6 = 0.9.
  2. NX = −NKI = −0.9.

Answer: Foreigners fund $0.9 trillion of U.S. investment, and the U.S. runs a $0.9 trillion trade deficit. If saving rose to $5.5 trillion with investment unchanged, NKI and NX would both go to zero.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "The yen-dollar rate fell from 160 to 150, so the yen fell."

Correct: ¥ per $1 is the price of the dollar. It fell, so the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated. Always check which currency is "per 1."

Mistake: Putting "euros" on the horizontal axis or labeling the vertical axis just "price."

Correct: His graph has the quantity of dollars traded on the horizontal axis and the price of $1 in foreign currency (€ per $1, ¥ per $1) on the vertical axis.

Mistake: Saying Americans buying more foreign goods raises the demand for dollars.

Correct: Americans buying foreign things supply dollars. Foreigners buying U.S. things demand dollars.

Mistake: "A higher U.S. interest rate makes the dollar weaker because borrowing costs more."

Correct: A higher U.S. real rate attracts savers to U.S. assets: demand for dollars rises and supply falls, so the dollar appreciates, and NX falls.

Mistake: "Tariffs cut imports, so the trade deficit shrinks."

Correct: In his model, tariffs reduce the supply of dollars, the dollar appreciates, and that cancels the direct effect. NX = −NKI and NKI did not change, so NX does not change.

Mistake: Explaining the U.S. trade deficit by low-quality U.S. goods or American love of imports.

Correct: Those stories predict a weak dollar. The deficit reflects large net capital inflows: U.S. assets are attractive and U.S. investment exceeds U.S. saving.

Mistake: Getting the sign wrong in NX = −NKI.

Correct: A trade deficit (NX < 0) comes with positive net capital inflows (NKI > 0): foreigners are buying more U.S. assets than Americans buy abroad.

Practice questions

The long questions use made-up curves and numbers; the stories come from his slides. The last group is 2-point multiple choice like Q1(d) on the Fall 2025 final.

12 points Ueda's surprise. On December 1, 2025, the Bank of Japan's governor hinted at an interest-rate increase later that month, and bond yields climbed around the world. Suppose the dollar starts at ¥160 per $1.

(a) (2 points) Why would any central bank, including the Bank of Japan, raise its interest rate? Name the two channels from his slides.

(b) (4 points) Draw the foreign exchange market for dollars, priced in yen, before and after a rise in Japan's real interest rate. Explain which curves shift and why, and label e1 and e2.

(c) (2 points) Suppose the rate moves to ¥150 per $1. Which currency appreciated? Express both rates in dollars per yen.

(d) (2 points) A Japanese camera costs ¥75,000. What does it cost an American before and after? What happens to U.S. net exports and PAE?

(e) (2 points) Why was this reported as a threat to U.S. markets?

Show answer

(a) To reduce inflation by reducing planned aggregate expenditure and thus GDP, through reduced investment spending (Class 22) and through an appreciated currency and reduced net exports (Class 23).

Full credit: lower inflation by lowering PAE; both channels (investment, and appreciation lowering NX).

(b) Japanese bonds now pay more, and international investors prefer higher real interest rates. Savers in Japan buy fewer U.S. bonds, so demand for dollars shifts left. Savers in the U.S. buy more Japanese bonds, so they supply more dollars: supply shifts right. Both shifts lower the price of the dollar, from e1 = ¥160 to e2 = ¥150.

Full credit: axes labeled (Q of $, price of $ in ¥); D left and S right with reasons; e2 below e1 on the vertical axis.

(c) The price of a dollar fell from ¥160 to ¥150, so the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated. In dollars per yen: 1 ÷ 160 = $0.00625 before and 1 ÷ 150 = $0.006667 after; the price of a yen rose.

Full credit: yen appreciated, dollar depreciated; $0.00625 and $0.006667 per yen.

(d) Before: 75,000 ÷ 160 = $468.75. After: 75,000 ÷ 150 = $500.00. Imports from Japan get more expensive for Americans and U.S. goods get cheaper for Japanese buyers, so U.S. imports fall, exports rise, NX rises, and the U.S. PAE line shifts up.

Full credit: $468.75 and $500.00; NX and PAE rise.

(e) A higher Japanese real rate pulls capital into Japan and out of U.S. assets. Selling U.S. assets lowers their prices, and lower bond prices mean higher U.S. interest rates.

Full credit: capital flows to Japan, so U.S. asset prices fall and U.S. interest rates rise.

12 points Tariff wall. The U.S. economy starts in long-run equilibrium. The U.S. then raises tariffs on many goods for a long time, and (unrealistically) no other country retaliates. Before the tariffs, the dollar trades at €0.86.

(a) (2 points) Which curve in the foreign exchange market for dollars shifts, and in which direction? Why?

(b) (4 points) Draw the market for dollars (priced in euros) before and after. Show e1 and e2. Does the dollar appreciate or depreciate?

(c) (2 points) Describe the two opposing effects on net exports.

(d) (2 points) What is the overall effect on net exports in the long run? Use S + NKI = I.

(e) (2 points) Will higher tariffs have literally no effect on NX? Explain how seriously to take the model.

Show answer

(a) Supply of dollars shifts left. Tariffs make imports more expensive, so Americans buy fewer foreign goods and need fewer euros, which means they offer fewer dollars on the foreign exchange market.

Full credit: supply of $ shifts left because Americans buy fewer imports.

(b) With supply shifted left and demand unchanged, the price of the dollar rises: the dollar appreciates, here from €0.86 to €0.92 (made-up numbers).

Full credit: labeled axes; S shifts left; e2 above e1; "appreciates."

(c) At a given exchange rate, imports fall, which raises NX. But the dollar appreciates, which makes U.S. exports more expensive abroad and imports cheaper at home, which lowers NX.

Full credit: both effects: direct rise from fewer imports, and the fall from the stronger dollar.

(d) No change. To find NX, find NKI. Tariffs do not change the U.S. real interest rate, foreign rates, or tastes for assets, so NKI does not change, and since NX = −NKI, NX does not change. In the long-run diagram neither S + NKI nor I shifts, so r* and I* stay the same too.

Full credit: NX unchanged because NKI is unchanged (NX = −NKI); S + NKI and I curves do not move.

(e) Almost surely not literally zero, since all models are approximations. But the effect is likely small, because the stronger dollar clearly works against the direct effect, and NKI does not respond much to the exchange rate. The model gets exactly zero because it assumes NKI does not respond at all.

Full credit: not literally zero, but approximately zero because of the offsetting appreciation.

(a) (2 points) Explain why the first claim does not explain a large, persistent trade deficit.

(b) (4 points) Draw the foreign exchange market for dollars when tastes shift toward U.S. assets. Explain each shift and the effect on the dollar.

(c) (2 points) Suppose net capital inflows into the U.S. are $0.9 trillion this year. What are U.S. net exports? Explain.

(d) (2 points) U.S. national saving is $4.6 trillion. What is U.S. investment, using the long-run condition?

Show answer

(a) Lower-quality U.S. goods would lower foreign demand for dollars and raise Americans' supply of dollars, so the dollar would depreciate. A weaker dollar makes U.S. goods cheaper and foreign goods dearer, which pushes NX back up. So the theory predicts a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit.

Full credit: the claim predicts a weak dollar; it does not create a lasting deficit.

(b) Foreigners want more U.S. assets, so demand for dollars shifts right. Americans want fewer foreign assets, so supply of dollars shifts left. Both raise the price of the dollar: it appreciates (0.86 to 0.98 in the figure above). The strong dollar lowers exports and raises imports.

Full credit: D right and S left with reasons; e rises; the dollar appreciates, NX falls.

(c) NX = −NKI = −$0.9 trillion, a $0.9 trillion trade deficit. The dollars foreigners use to buy U.S. assets come from Americans buying more imports than they export.

Full credit: NX = −0.9 with NX = −NKI.

(d) S + NKI = I, so I = 4.6 + 0.9 = $5.5 trillion. The U.S. invests more than it saves and foreigners fund the gap.

Full credit: I = $5.5 trillion from S + NKI = I.

Show answer

Full credit: KI = 2.2 with the equation.

(b) NX = 3.2 − 4.1 = −0.9. NKI = 2.2 − 1.3 = +0.9. NX = −NKI holds.

Full credit: −0.9 and +0.9.

(c) The U.S. imported $0.9 trillion more than it exported, and paid for it by selling $0.9 trillion more assets to foreigners than it bought abroad. If you import on net, you have to sell some of your assets abroad.

Full credit: trade deficit financed by net sales of U.S. assets to foreigners.

(b) (2 points) If the dollar falls from €0.86 to €0.80, did it appreciate or depreciate? What is the price of a euro in dollars before and after?

Show answer

One-minute review

  • The nominal exchange rate is the price of a currency. His slides quote the price of $1 in foreign currency: €0.86 per $1, ¥150 per $1. The other convention is the reciprocal.
  • Appreciation: the currency's price rises. Depreciation: it falls. ¥160 to ¥150 per $1 means the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated.
  • Always ask "whose price is this number?" before deciding which currency rose.
  • Demand for dollars: foreigners buying U.S. goods, services, and assets. Supply of dollars: Americans buying foreign goods, services, and assets.
  • A higher U.S. real rate raises demand for dollars and lowers supply, so the dollar appreciates. A foreign rate hike does the reverse.
  • Appreciation raises export prices, lowers exports, raises imports, and lowers NX and PAE. So NX falls when r rises: PAE(r) = C(r) + I(r) + G + NX(r).
  • Balance of payments: EX + KI = IM + KO, so NX = −NKI. A trade deficit equals net capital inflows.
  • Long run: S* + NKI* = I*. Investment above saving means capital inflows and a trade deficit; China's surpluses reflect high saving.
  • Tariffs: supply of dollars falls, the dollar appreciates, and NX is (approximately) unchanged because NKI is unchanged.
  • The persistent U.S. deficit comes from attractive U.S. assets (large NKI), not from low quality or tastes for imports, which would just weaken the dollar.
  • Most rates float; fixed rates are held by a central bank buying and selling foreign reserves (China, 1999 to 2005).
