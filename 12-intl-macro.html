Exchange rates and international macro

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, and it is set by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. This unit shows what moves that price, why a higher interest rate makes a currency stronger and lowers net exports, and why a country's trade balance is the mirror image of its capital flows. On the Fall 2025 final it was multiple choice (d): the yen-dollar rate falling from ¥160 to ¥150 per dollar means the yen appreciated and the dollar depreciated. His final review deck also made it Lesson 6, with the market for dollars and the balance of payments.

The ideas, explained

The big idea

An exchange rate is just the price of a currency, and supply and demand for that currency set it. When U.S. interest rates rise, savers around the world want U.S. assets, the dollar gets more expensive, and U.S. net exports fall. For the final, always check which currency the number is the price of, and remember that net exports are the mirror image of net capital inflows: NX = −NKI.

Why exchange rates belong in the short-run model

Net exports, NX, are exports minus imports, and they are one of the four parts of planned aggregate expenditure (PAE): PAE = C + IP + G + NX, where C is consumption, IP is planned investment, and G is government purchases. So anything that changes NX shifts the PAE line in the Keynesian cross and changes GDP (Y) in the short run. Exchange rates are one of the main things that move NX, and the real interest rate r is one of the main things that move exchange rates. That is how this class connects to Class 22: his slide sums it up as PAE(r) = C(r) + I(r) + G + NX(r), where all three of C, I, and NX fall when r rises.

In plain words: what the U.S. sells to other countries minus what it buys from them is part of total spending. Exchange rates change how much the U.S. sells and buys, so they change total spending and GDP.

The nominal exchange rate is a price

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. His examples: "It currently takes 0.86 euros to buy 1 U.S. dollar," so the price of $1 is €0.86, and £0.76 per $1 for the British pound. His slides always quote the price of the dollar in foreign currency: euros per dollar, yen per dollar, yuan per dollar. That is the number on the vertical axis of every foreign exchange graph he draws.

In plain words: an exchange rate is a price tag on a currency. "€0.86 per $1" means one dollar costs 86 euro cents.

The same exchange rate can be quoted the other way around, as dollars per unit of foreign currency. The two quotes are reciprocals: divide 1 by one quote to get the other. For example, if $1 costs €0.86, then €1 costs 1 ÷ 0.86 = $1.1628.

Appreciation and depreciation

From his slide 18:

Appreciation of the dollar: the price of the dollar in some foreign currency rises. Depreciation of the dollar: the price of the dollar in some foreign currency falls. When one currency appreciates against another, the other depreciates against it.

In plain words: a currency appreciates when it gets more expensive, so each unit of it buys more of the other currency. If the dollar appreciates against the yen, the yen has depreciated against the dollar, and the reverse.

A strong currency is one whose price in other currencies is high. A weak currency is one whose price is low (his slide 27). "Stronger" is another word for appreciated, and "weaker" is another word for depreciated.

The safe way to answer any appreciation question is to ask one thing: whose price went up? That currency appreciated.

  • ¥160 per $1 falling to ¥150 per $1 (the Fall 2025 question). This number is the price of a dollar, measured in yen. It fell, so the dollar got cheaper: the dollar depreciated. A dollar now buys fewer yen, which means a yen buys more of a dollar: the yen appreciated. In the other convention, the price of a yen rose from $0.00625 to $0.006667. Same event, read from the other side.
  • ¥150 per $1 rising to ¥160 per $1. The price of the dollar rose, so the dollar appreciated and the yen depreciated.
  • $1.10 per €1 rising to $1.20 per €1. This number is the price of a euro. It rose, so the euro appreciated and the dollar depreciated, even though the number went up.

Before you circle anything, write down which currency is in the denominator ("per $1" or "per €1"). The number is the price of that currency. Up means it appreciated; down means it depreciated. The other currency does the opposite.

The percent changes are not mirror images. From ¥160 to ¥150, the dollar lost (160 − 150) ÷ 160 = 6.25% of its yen value, while the yen gained 160 ÷ 150 − 1 = 6.67% in dollar value. Each is measured from its own starting price.

What appreciation does to trade

His slide puts it plainly: "Currency appreciation raises export prices and tends to reduce exports." When the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods cost more in foreign currency and foreign goods cost fewer dollars, so U.S. exports fall, imports rise, and NX falls. When the dollar depreciates, the opposite happens and NX rises.

In plain words: a strong dollar makes American goods pricey for foreigners and foreign goods cheap for Americans. So the U.S. sells less abroad and buys more from abroad. A weak dollar does the opposite.

To convert a price, multiply a dollar price by the rate to get the foreign price, and divide a foreign price by the rate to get the dollar price. Numbers for the move from ¥160 to ¥150 per dollar (a dollar depreciation):

  • A $1,000 U.S. computer costs a Japanese buyer ¥160,000 at the old rate and ¥150,000 at the new one. U.S. exports get cheaper in Japan, so they rise.
  • A ¥30,000 Japanese good costs an American 30,000 ÷ 160 = $187.50 at the old rate and 30,000 ÷ 150 = $200.00 at the new one. Imports get more expensive, so they fall.

The foreign exchange market for dollars

There is a market for each currency against every other currency, and the markets for one currency (like the dollar) tend to move together. His standard picture is the market where dollars trade for euros:

  • Demanders of dollars are foreigners who want to buy American goods, services, or assets. To buy American, they must convert their currency into dollars. When the dollar is cheap, U.S. goods and assets are cheap for them, so they buy more and need more dollars. That is why demand slopes down.
  • Suppliers of dollars are Americans who want to buy foreign goods, services, or assets. To buy foreign, they must convert dollars into foreign currency. When the dollar's price in euros is high, each dollar buys many euros, so European goods are cheap for Americans. They buy more of them and convert more dollars. That is why supply slopes up.
  • The equilibrium exchange rate is the price of the dollar that makes the quantity of dollars demanded equal the quantity supplied.

In plain words: anyone abroad who wants something American needs dollars, so they demand dollars. Any American who wants something foreign has to give up dollars to get the other currency, so they supply dollars.

A shift in either curve changes the exchange rate. If demand for dollars rises or supply of dollars falls, the dollar appreciates. If demand falls or supply rises, the dollar depreciates.

In plain words: more people wanting dollars, or fewer people offering them, pushes the dollar's price up. Fewer people wanting dollars, or more people offering them, pushes it down.

What shifts the curves

His learning-objectives slide (slide 8) lists what raises demand for a country's currency in the short run: a rise in its domestic real interest rate, global uncertainty that sends investors to "safe haven" currencies, and higher demand for its exports. A safe haven currency is one that investors buy when they are scared, because they trust it to hold its value; the dollar is one. His examples fill in the rest. Interest rates and the exchange rate

International investors prefer higher real interest rates. When the U.S. real interest rate rises relative to Japan's, savers in Japan want U.S. bonds instead of Japanese bonds (they demand more dollars), and savers in the U.S. also want U.S. bonds instead of Japanese bonds (they supply fewer dollars). Both effects raise the price of the dollar. His chart of U.S. and Japanese interest rates shows U.S. rates climbing from near zero in 2022 to above 4% by early 2023 while Japan's stayed near zero, and his chart of yen per dollar shows the dollar rising to about ¥150 in late 2022.

In plain words: savers send their money where it earns the most. When U.S. interest rates go up, money flows into the U.S., and people need dollars to move it there. More people want dollars and fewer Americans sell them, so the dollar's price goes up.

Run the same logic in reverse for his current-events slides from December 2025, when the Bank of Japan (BOJ) hinted at raising its target interest rate. A higher Japanese real rate pulls capital into Japan: demand for dollars falls and supply of dollars rises, so the dollar depreciates against the yen. His slide adds that this reduces prices of U.S. assets (and raises U.S. interest rates), which is why it was reported as a threat to U.S. markets. Why would the BOJ do it? For the same reason any central bank raises rates: to reduce inflation by lowering PAE, through lower investment (Class 22) and through an appreciated currency and lower net exports (Class 23).

This is a key step in why NX falls when r rises, which he summarizes: if r in the U.S. increases, the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods become more expensive than foreign goods, imports rise, exports fall, and NX falls. It also means monetary policy works through the exchange rate. His final review makes the same point for expansionary policy: when the Fed cuts real interest rates, it raises investment and parts of consumption like new car purchases, and it also raises NX through a currency depreciation.

The balance of payments: NX = −NKI

The balance of payments is an accounting of the supply and demand for dollars used in international transactions. At the equilibrium exchange rate, dollars demanded equal dollars supplied:

EX + KI = IM + KO, where EX is exports (foreigners buy U.S. products), KI is capital inflows (foreigners buy U.S. assets), IM is imports (Americans buy foreign products), and KO is capital outflows (Americans buy foreign assets). Rearranging, (EX − IM) + (KI − KO) = 0, so NX = −NKI, where NKI = KI − KO is net capital inflows.

The rearranging is one step: subtract IM and KO from both sides of EX + KI = IM + KO to get EX − IM + KI − KO = 0. The first pair is NX and the second pair is NKI, so NX + NKI = 0. Here "capital" means financial assets such as bonds, stocks, and real estate, not machines.

In plain words: every dollar that leaves the U.S. to buy something foreign comes back when a foreigner buys something American, either a product or an asset. So if the U.S. buys more products than it sells, foreigners must be buying more U.S. assets than Americans buy abroad, by exactly the same amount.

In his words: if you export on net, you buy assets abroad; if you import on net, you have to sell some of your assets abroad. A trade deficit and a net capital inflow are the same thing seen from two sides. His Bureau of Economic Analysis chart shows U.S. NX and net capital inflows as near-perfect mirror images since 1960, with NX falling to around −$750 billion in the mid-2000s and capital inflows rising by about the same amount.

What determines net capital inflows:

  • The U.S. real interest rate. If r rises, American assets look better than foreign ones, so NKI rises.
  • Foreign real interest rates. If they rise, American assets look worse by comparison, so NKI falls.
  • Tastes for assets. If Americans or foreigners find American assets more attractive at a given r, NKI rises.

Since NX = −NKI, a rise in r raises NKI and so lowers NX; a fall in r raises NX.

The long run: S + NKI = I

In earlier classes the long-run real interest rate r* was set where national saving equals investment, with NX* = 0. Now allow trade. Start from Y* = C* + I* + G + NX*. Subtract C*, G, and NX* from both sides to get (Y* − C* − G) − NX* = I*. National saving is S = Y − C − G, so the part in parentheses is S*. Since NX = −NKI, the term −NX* equals +NKI*. Put those two swaps in and you get

S* + NKI* = I*. In the long run, r* adjusts so that domestic saving plus net capital inflows equals investment. If a country invests more than it saves, foreigners must fund the difference (NKI > 0), and the country runs a trade deficit (NX < 0).

In plain words: a country pays for its investment with its own saving plus money from abroad. The money from abroad is exactly the size of its trade deficit. A country that saves more than it invests sends the extra abroad and runs a trade surplus.

Both saving and net capital inflows rise with r, so the S + NKI curve slopes up, and investment slopes down. His slide 9 applies this: the U.S. has looked like a country whose investment exceeds its saving since the 1960s, so it imports capital and runs trade deficits, and "China's trade surpluses reflect its very high domestic saving."

So the best way to figure out what happens to NX, short run or long run, is to figure out what happens to NKI. NX* = −NKI*.

Application: higher tariffs on many goods

His big application (with the U.S. tariffs "pending a Supreme Court decision" at the time) assumes the economy starts in long-run equilibrium, the U.S. raises tariffs on many goods for a long time, and other countries do not retaliate.

  • The foreign exchange market. Tariffs reduce U.S. purchases of foreign goods, which reduces the supply of dollars. Supply shifts left, so the dollar appreciates.
  • Two forces on NX. At a given exchange rate, imports fall, so NX would rise. But the stronger dollar reduces exports and raises imports, which lowers NX.
  • The net effect. Ask what happens to NKI. Nothing does: interest rates and tastes for assets did not change. So nothing happens to NX, in the short run or the long run. In the long-run diagram neither S + NKI nor I moves, so r* and I* do not change either.

In plain words: tariffs cut imports, but they also make the dollar stronger, and a stronger dollar cuts exports and brings some imports back. The trade balance ends up about where it started.

How seriously to take this: will tariffs have literally no effect on NX? "Almost surely not: All models are approximations." But there is a clear force against the direct effect, the stronger dollar. As long as NKI does not respond much to the exchange rate, which he calls realistic, the effect on NX is small. The model gets exactly zero only because it assumes NKI does not respond at all.

Why the U.S. runs a persistent trade deficit

His FRED chart shows U.S. net exports negative as a share of GDP for decades, dipping to about −6% in the mid-2000s. Three popular explanations are mistaken: that U.S. goods are low quality or Americans are not productive enough, that Americans have strong tastes for foreign goods, and that foreign countries are widely protectionist. The problem with all three: they predict a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit. Lower quality, for example, means foreigners demand fewer dollars and Americans supply more, so the dollar simply depreciates until trade adjusts.

The deficit has to come from the capital side, because NX* = −NKI*. A large negative NX* means a large positive NKI*: U.S. assets look highly attractive, perhaps because people expect risky U.S. assets to pay off especially well (his examples: tech in the 1990s, housing in the early 2000s, "AI now?"). Foreigners buy more U.S. assets and Americans buy fewer foreign ones, the dollar appreciates, and NX falls.

In plain words: the U.S. buys more than it sells abroad because the world wants to own U.S. assets. Foreigners pay for those assets with dollars they earn by selling things to Americans.

Real exchange rates and the law of one price

His slide 9 states the law of one price: if transport costs are minimal and there are no trade barriers, a traded good should cost the same everywhere once you convert currencies. His example: a computer priced at $1,000 in the U.S. should be priced at ¥155,960 in Japan when the exchange rate is ¥155.96 per $1, because 1,000 × 155.96 = 155,960. Purchasing power parity (PPP) extends this to say inflation rates in two countries should be related to the exchange rate between them; he leaves the details for ECON 100B.

The textbook makes this precise with the real exchange rate, the price of the average domestic good relative to the average foreign good, with both in the same currency:

Real exchange rate = (e × P) ÷ Pf, where e is the nominal exchange rate in foreign currency per dollar, P is the U.S. price, and Pf is the foreign price. A real exchange rate of 1 means the law of one price holds. Above 1 means U.S. goods are relatively expensive, which tends to lower NX.

In plain words: turn the U.S. price into the foreign currency, then divide by the foreign price. A result above 1 means the U.S. version costs more, and a result below 1 means it costs less.

With his computer: (155.96 × 1,000) ÷ 155,960 = 1. If the same computer sold for ¥140,000 in Japan, the real exchange rate would be 155,960 ÷ 140,000 = 1.114, so the U.S. version is about 11% more expensive and Japanese buyers would prefer their own.

Fixed versus flexible exchange rates

Today most exchange rates are determined in markets; these are flexible (floating) exchange rates. Some countries today, and many in the past, use fixed exchange rates, which are maintained by a central bank that buys and sells foreign reserves. Foreign reserves are the stock of other countries' currencies (and assets in them) that a central bank holds. His example is China, which had a fixed exchange rate with the dollar from 1999 to 2005 and now has a floating rate. His FRED chart shows the yuan-per-dollar rate flat near 8.3 during the fixed period and then falling toward 6 to 7 once it floated, which means the yuan appreciated against the dollar.

The textbook explains how the buying and selling works. If the fixed rate is set above the market rate for the dollar, more dollars are offered than demanded at that price. To hold the rate, the country's central bank (China's, in his example) must buy up the excess dollars with its own currency, adding to its reserves of dollars. If the fixed rate were below the market rate, more dollars would be demanded than offered, so that central bank would have to sell dollars from its reserves, and it could run out.

In plain words: a flexible rate moves with supply and demand every day. A fixed rate stays put only because the central bank keeps buying or selling currency to hold it there.

Worked examples

Example: the Fall 2025 yen question, both ways

The yen-dollar rate falls from ¥160 per $1 to ¥150 per $1. Which currency appreciated? State it in both conventions and give the percent changes.

  1. The quote is "per $1," so the number is the price of a dollar in yen. It fell, so the dollar depreciated.
  2. Flip to dollars per yen: 1 ÷ 160 = $0.00625 and 1 ÷ 150 = $0.006667. The price of a yen rose, so the yen appreciated.
  3. Dollar: (150 − 160) ÷ 160 = −6.25%. Yen: 0.006667 ÷ 0.00625 − 1 = 160 ÷ 150 − 1 = +6.67%.

Answer: The yen appreciated (by 6.67%) and the dollar depreciated (by 6.25%). This is answer (i) on the Fall 2025 final.

Example: converting and reading a pound quote

The pound goes from £0.76 per $1 to £0.80 per $1. Convert both to dollars per pound and say what happened.

  1. 1 ÷ 0.76 = $1.3158 per £1. 1 ÷ 0.80 = $1.25 per £1.
  2. The price of a dollar in pounds rose (0.76 to 0.80), so the dollar appreciated.
  3. The price of a pound in dollars fell ($1.3158 to $1.25), so the pound depreciated.

Answer: Dollar appreciated, pound depreciated. A British tourist now pays more pounds for a $100 hotel night: £76 before, £80 after.

Example: what a depreciation does to prices of traded goods

The dollar goes from ¥160 to ¥150. A Japanese buyer wants a $1,000 U.S. laptop; an American wants a ¥30,000 Japanese rice cooker.

  1. Laptop in yen: 1,000 × 160 = ¥160,000 before; 1,000 × 150 = ¥150,000 after. It is ¥10,000 cheaper in Japan.
  2. Rice cooker in dollars: 30,000 ÷ 160 = $187.50 before; 30,000 ÷ 150 = $200.00 after. It is $12.50 more expensive in the U.S.
  3. U.S. exports become cheaper abroad and imports more expensive at home.

Answer: The dollar depreciation tends to raise U.S. exports and lower imports, so NX rises and the PAE line shifts up.

Example: balance of payments arithmetic

In a year (hypothetical, $ trillions), U.S. exports are 3.2, imports are 4.1, and capital outflows (Americans buying foreign assets) are 1.3. Find capital inflows, NX, and NKI.

  1. EX + KI = IM + KO, so 3.2 + KI = 4.1 + 1.3 = 5.4, and KI = 2.2.
  2. NX = EX − IM = 3.2 − 4.1 = −0.9.
  3. NKI = KI − KO = 2.2 − 1.3 = +0.9.
  4. Check: NX = −NKI, and −0.9 = −(+0.9).

Answer: KI = $2.2 trillion, a $0.9 trillion trade deficit, matched by $0.9 trillion of net capital inflows.

Example: saving, capital inflows, and investment

National saving is $4.6 trillion and investment is $5.5 trillion (hypothetical). What are net capital inflows and net exports?

  1. S + NKI = I, so NKI = 5.5 − 4.6 = 0.9.
  2. NX = −NKI = −0.9.

Answer: Foreigners fund $0.9 trillion of U.S. investment, and the U.S. runs a $0.9 trillion trade deficit. If saving rose to $5.5 trillion with investment unchanged, NKI and NX would both go to zero.

Common mistakes

Mistake: "The yen-dollar rate fell from 160 to 150, so the yen fell."

Correct: ¥ per $1 is the price of the dollar. It fell, so the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated. Always check which currency is "per 1."

Mistake: Putting "euros" on the horizontal axis or labeling the vertical axis just "price."

Correct: His graph has the quantity of dollars traded on the horizontal axis and the price of $1 in foreign currency (€ per $1, ¥ per $1) on the vertical axis.

Mistake: Saying Americans buying more foreign goods raises the demand for dollars.

Correct: Americans buying foreign things supply dollars. Foreigners buying U.S. things demand dollars.

Mistake: "A higher U.S. interest rate makes the dollar weaker because borrowing costs more."

Correct: A higher U.S. real rate attracts savers to U.S. assets: demand for dollars rises and supply falls, so the dollar appreciates, and NX falls.

Mistake: "Tariffs cut imports, so the trade deficit shrinks."

Correct: In his model, tariffs reduce the supply of dollars, the dollar appreciates, and that cancels the direct effect. NX = −NKI and NKI did not change, so NX does not change.

Mistake: Explaining the U.S. trade deficit by low-quality U.S. goods or American love of imports.

Correct: Those stories predict a weak dollar. The deficit reflects large net capital inflows: U.S. assets are attractive and U.S. investment exceeds U.S. saving.

Mistake: Getting the sign wrong in NX = −NKI.

Correct: A trade deficit (NX < 0) comes with positive net capital inflows (NKI > 0): foreigners are buying more U.S. assets than Americans buy abroad.

Mistake: Computing the real exchange rate as e ÷ P, or comparing a dollar price with a yen price directly.

Correct: Use (e × P) ÷ Pf, which converts the U.S. price into foreign currency first so both prices are in the same units. A $400 bike at ¥150 per $1 is ¥60,000, and that is what you compare with the Japanese price.

Mistake: "A strong dollar is good for U.S. exporters."

Correct: A strong (appreciated) dollar makes U.S. goods more expensive abroad, so it hurts exporters. It helps U.S. importers, U.S. consumers of foreign goods, and Americans traveling abroad.

Mistake: Treating the textbook's "balance-of-payments deficit" under a fixed rate as the trade deficit.

Correct: In the textbook, a balance-of-payments deficit is the excess supply of a currency at its official rate, which the central bank must buy with reserves each period. It is a loss of reserves, not NX. (His slides use "balance of payments" for the identity EX + KI = IM + KO.)

If you get stuck on the exam

  • Write the units next to the number in the margin and finish the sentence "this number is the price of one ___." That currency appreciated if the number went up and depreciated if it went down.
  • For any event, ask who is buying what. Foreigners buying anything American (goods, trips, bonds, stocks) demand dollars. Americans buying anything foreign supply dollars. Then shift only the curve for the group that changed.
  • Draw the plain market first: label the axes "Q of $" and "Price of $ in ¥ (¥ per $1)," draw S and D, and mark e1 and Q1. Then move one curve at a time and mark e2.
  • For a price conversion, write the rule before the numbers: foreign price = dollar price × rate, and dollar price = foreign price ÷ rate, with the rate in foreign currency per $1.
  • For any question about NX that goes past one step, find NKI first. NX = −NKI, and NKI changes only with U.S. interest rates, foreign interest rates, or tastes for assets.
  • If both curves shift and push the price the same way, state the price change with confidence and say the quantity traded could go either way.

The stronger dollar offsets part of the tariff's effect on import prices, exactly as in his Class 23 tariff example.

Full credit: circle (i).

6 points Defending a peg. A country fixes its currency at a value above what the market would set. Adapted from the textbook, Chapter 28, and Edwards' Class 23 slides 13 and 14

(a) (2 points) At the official rate, is there excess supply or excess demand for its currency? What must its central bank do to hold the rate?

(b) (2 points) Why can it not do this forever?

(c) (2 points) It could instead raise interest rates to defend the peg. What does that cost at home?

Show answer

(a) At a price above the market rate, more of its currency is offered than demanded, so there is excess supply. His slides say fixed rates are held by a central bank that buys and sells foreign reserves: here it must buy up the extra units of its own currency, paying with its foreign reserves.

Full credit: excess supply; the central bank buys its own currency with foreign reserves.

(b) Reserves are finite. If traders expect a devaluation, they rush to sell the currency before it happens (the textbook calls this a speculative attack), which drains reserves even faster and can force the devaluation.

Full credit: reserves run out; expectations of devaluation speed the drain.

(c) A higher real interest rate attracts foreign savers and raises demand for the currency, but it also lowers investment and consumption, so the PAE line shifts down and output falls. Monetary policy is tied to the exchange rate instead of to domestic goals, and the defense can cause a recession.

Full credit: higher r raises demand for the currency but lowers PAE and output at home.

(iii). Work back from the outcome: an appreciation needs demand for dollars to shift right or supply to shift left. 2 points Roman holiday. A hotel in Rome charges €172 a night both years. Last year a night cost an American $200; this year it costs $215. What was the exchange rate in euros per $1 each year, and which currency appreciated?

Show answer

The euro price is the dollar price times the rate, so the rate is the euro price divided by the dollar price. Last year: 172 ÷ 200 = €0.86 per $1. This year: 172 ÷ 215 = €0.80 per $1. The price of a dollar in euros fell, so the dollar depreciated and the euro appreciated. The dollar lost (0.80 − 0.86) ÷ 0.86 = −6.98% of its euro value. That is why the same €172 room costs an American $15 more.

Full credit: €0.86 and €0.80 per $1; dollar depreciated, euro appreciated.

6 points Europe slows down. Europe falls into a recession, and European households and firms cut back on buying U.S. goods. The dollar starts at €0.86. Nothing happens to interest rates. Adapted from Edwards' Class 23 slide 8 and his Fall 2025 final review slide 51

(a) (2 points) Which curve in the market for dollars (priced in euros) shifts, and which way? Why?

(b) (2 points) Describe the graph: what happens to the price of the dollar, and what do you label?

(c) (2 points) U.S. exports to Europe fall. Explain why the change in the exchange rate makes the drop in exports smaller than it would be at the old rate of €0.86.

Show answer

(a) Demand for dollars shifts left. Europeans who buy U.S. goods must first buy dollars, so when they buy fewer U.S. goods they demand fewer dollars at every exchange rate. Supply does not move, because nothing changed for Americans buying European goods or assets.

Full credit: demand for $ shifts left because Europeans buy fewer U.S. goods.

(b) Axes: quantity of dollars traded across, price of $1 in euros up the side. Draw S, D1, and D2 to the left of D1. The new crossing is lower, so e2 is below e1 = €0.86 and the dollar depreciates. The quantity traded also falls, because only demand moved.

Full credit: labeled axes; D shifts left; e2 below e1; dollar depreciates.

(c) The weaker dollar makes U.S. goods cheaper in euros. For example, a $100 U.S. product costs 100 × 0.86 = €86 at the old rate and only €80 if the rate falls to €0.80. Cheaper U.S. goods win back some European buyers and make imports dearer for Americans, which offsets part of the fall in exports and in NX. His final review slide 51 says that in the short run net exports can shift when consumers' incomes change, and the depreciation is the force that softens that shift.

Full credit: the dollar depreciates, U.S. goods get cheaper abroad, so some of the lost exports come back.

4 points Protection abroad. A senator says, "The U.S. runs a trade deficit because other countries put tariffs on our goods." Suppose many countries do raise tariffs on U.S. goods, while interest rates and tastes for assets do not change. Adapted from Edwards' Class 23 slides 45 and 49

(a) (2 points) What happens in the market for dollars, and to the value of the dollar?

(b) (2 points) What happens to U.S. net exports overall? Use NX = −NKI, and say whether the senator's explanation fits his model.

Show answer

(a) Foreign tariffs make U.S. goods more expensive abroad, so foreigners buy fewer of them and demand fewer dollars. Demand for dollars shifts left and the dollar depreciates. This is the mirror image of his U.S. tariff example, where the supply of dollars shifted left and the dollar appreciated.

Full credit: demand for $ shifts left; the dollar depreciates.

(b) No change. At the old exchange rate, U.S. exports would fall. But the weaker dollar makes U.S. goods cheaper abroad and foreign goods dearer at home, which pushes NX back up. To get the net effect, find NKI: interest rates and tastes for assets did not change, so NKI did not change, and NX = −NKI did not change either. His slide 49 lists foreign protectionism as a mistaken explanation because "these theories predict a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit."

Full credit: NX unchanged because NKI is unchanged; the senator's story predicts a weak dollar, not a deficit.

4 points Two ways to a strong dollar. Compare two separate events: (1) the U.S. raises tariffs on many goods, and (2) the Fed raises the U.S. real interest rate. Adapted from Edwards' Class 23 slides 32 to 35 and 42 to 46

(a) (2 points) Both events make the dollar appreciate. Which curve or curves shift in each case, and why?

(b) (2 points) In his model, only one of the two lowers U.S. net exports. Which one, and why?

Show answer

(a) Tariffs: Americans buy fewer imports, so they supply fewer dollars. Only supply shifts left. Rate hike: foreign savers want U.S. bonds, so demand for dollars shifts right, and U.S. savers keep their money in U.S. bonds, so supply shifts left. Both cases raise the price of the dollar.

Full credit: tariffs move S left only; the rate hike moves D right and S left.

(b) The rate hike. NX = −NKI, so NX changes only if net capital inflows change. A higher U.S. real rate makes U.S. assets more attractive, which raises NKI (his slide 32), so NX falls. Tariffs do not change interest rates or tastes for assets, so NKI stays the same and NX stays the same; the appreciation cancels the direct effect of fewer imports.

Full credit: the rate hike, because it raises NKI; tariffs leave NKI and NX unchanged.

8 points The exchange-rate channel in the Keynesian cross. In trillions of dollars, C = 10 + (1/3)Y, I = 6, G = 5, and NX = −1 (the Fall 2025 final's numbers). The Fed then raises the real interest rate. As a result, autonomous consumption falls from 10 to 9.5, investment falls from 6 to 5, and net exports fall from −1 to −1.5. Adapted from the Fall 2025 final Q3 and Edwards' Class 23 slide 33

(a) (2 points) Find equilibrium GDP before the rate hike. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Explain why each of C, I, and NX falls when r rises.

(c) (2 points) Find the new equilibrium GDP and the change in GDP.

(d) (2 points) How much of the drop in GDP comes through the exchange-rate channel?

Show answer

(a) In equilibrium Y = PAE = C + I + G + NX. So Y = 10 + (1/3)Y + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20 + (1/3)Y. Subtract (1/3)Y from both sides: (2/3)Y = 20, so Y = 20 × 3/2 = 30.

Full credit: Y = 30 with the algebra.

(b) A higher r makes borrowing more expensive, so consumers buy fewer interest-sensitive goods such as new cars (C falls) and firms buy fewer machines and buildings (I falls). It also attracts foreign savers to U.S. assets, so the dollar appreciates, U.S. exports get more expensive abroad and imports cheaper at home, and NX falls.

Full credit: C and I fall because borrowing costs more; NX falls because the dollar appreciates.

(c) New autonomous spending = 9.5 + 5 + 5 − 1.5 = 18, a drop of 2. So (2/3)Y = 18 and Y = 18 × 3/2 = 27. GDP falls by 3. Check with the multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 1.5, and 1.5 × (−2) = −3.

Full credit: Y = 27, a fall of 3.

(d) NX fell by 0.5, and every dollar of lower autonomous spending lowers GDP by the multiplier, 1.5. So the exchange-rate channel accounts for 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75 of the 3 drop. The rest is investment (1 × 1.5 = 1.5) and consumption (0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75), and 0.75 + 1.5 + 0.75 = 3.

Full credit: 0.75 trillion, from 0.5 × 1.5.

One-minute review

Quick check

The rate goes from ¥150 per $1 to ¥140 per $1. Did the dollar appreciate or depreciate?

Depreciated: the number is the price of a dollar in yen, and it fell, so the yen appreciated.

Americans start buying more French wine. Which curve in the market for dollars shifts?

Supply of dollars shifts right, because Americans buying foreign goods give up dollars for euros.

The European Central Bank raises its real interest rate. What happens to the dollar against the euro?

It depreciates: savers move toward European bonds, so demand for dollars falls and supply rises.

A country's net exports are −$0.5 trillion. What are its net capital inflows?

+$0.5 trillion, because NX = −NKI.

Saving is $4 trillion and investment is $5 trillion. What are net exports?

−$1 trillion: NKI = I − S = 1, and NX = −NKI.

In his model, what do higher U.S. tariffs do to net exports in the long run?

Nothing: the dollar appreciates and cancels the direct effect, because NKI does not change.

  • The nominal exchange rate is the price of a currency. His slides quote the price of $1 in foreign currency: €0.86 per $1, ¥150 per $1. The other convention is the reciprocal.
  • Appreciation: the currency's price rises. Depreciation: it falls. ¥160 to ¥150 per $1 means the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated.
  • Always ask "whose price is this number?" before deciding which currency rose.
  • Demand for dollars: foreigners buying U.S. goods, services, and assets. Supply of dollars: Americans buying foreign goods, services, and assets.
  • A higher U.S. real rate raises demand for dollars and lowers supply, so the dollar appreciates. A foreign rate hike does the reverse.
  • Appreciation raises export prices, lowers exports, raises imports, and lowers NX and PAE. So NX falls when r rises: PAE(r) = C(r) + I(r) + G + NX(r).
  • Balance of payments: EX + KI = IM + KO, so NX = −NKI. A trade deficit equals net capital inflows.
  • Long run: S* + NKI* = I*. Investment above saving means capital inflows and a trade deficit; China's surpluses reflect high saving.
  • Tariffs: supply of dollars falls, the dollar appreciates, and NX is (approximately) unchanged because NKI is unchanged.
  • The persistent U.S. deficit comes from attractive U.S. assets (large NKI), not from low quality or tastes for imports, which would just weaken the dollar.
  • Most rates float; fixed rates are held by a central bank buying and selling foreign reserves (China, 1999 to 2005).
