Letter-grade cutoffs

The syllabus "Grading Policies" section gives straight-percentage cutoffs and a curve that can only help. Quoted exactly:

"Our aim in ECON 2 is to award an average and median overall course grade of a B (= 3.0). We expect that students' scores on the combination of assignments in ECON 2 will probably produce an average and median overall course grade of a B or higher using the standard percentage thresholds for grades (0-59% = F; 60-69% = D; 70-79% = C; 80-89% = B; 90-100% = A), sometimes called the "straight percentage" grade."

Fall 2026 syllabus, Grading Policies

"At the end of the term, we will compare each student's straight-percentage letter grade to the letter grade implied by a curve around a median of a B. Each student will earn the higher of those two grades. In recent terms, the class median score has been closer to a B+, or around 88%."

Fall 2026 syllabus, Grading Policies

Why it matters: 90% is the A line on the straight scale, and the curve can raise a grade but never lower it ("We will not award a curved grade that is lower than that student's "straight percentage" grade").

The syllabus does not list plus and minus cutoffs (for example, where an A− ends and an A begins), and it does not say how a score like 89.6% is rounded. It points to grading-scheme files from his ECON 140 course for more detail. If the line between A− and A matters to you, ask Edwards or a GSI directly.

What full-credit answers look like

His answer key answers each 2-point part in one to three sentences. Every full-credit answer has the same pieces:

  1. A direction or verdict word, stated first: increased, decreased, falls, different, "raise G."
  2. The number with units when there is one: "$2 trillion," "Y = 30."
  3. One sentence of reasoning in the model's vocabulary: marginal revenue, marginal propensity to consume, the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), the world price.
  4. For "show your work," the equation line by line: "20 + 1/3 Y = Y, so 20 = 2/3 Y, so Y = 30."

Example: a full-credit answer from the key

Q2(a) asked whether a Big Mac got more expensive in real terms when its price rose 3.5 times and the CPI rose 2.5 times.

Key answer: "The CPI rose by a factor of 2.5, while the Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5. Therefore the real price of a Big Mac has increased." Two sentences: the comparison, then the verdict.

Graphs

Four graph parts were worth 4 points each, 16 of the 60 points. The key's graphs all do the same things:

  • Label every curve: "PAE = 20 + 1/3 Y," "45°," "Supply," "Demand1," "Demand2."
  • Label intercepts and equilibrium values on both axes, with dotted lines from each equilibrium to each axis.
  • Show shifts with arrows, and keep a shifted line parallel when the slope did not change.
  • Draw to scale on the printed grid when it has numbers. In a Keynesian cross, the 45-degree line must go through (10, 10), (20, 20), and so on.

Spending your 90 minutes

Sixty points in 90 minutes is 1.5 minutes per point. The multiple choice goes faster than that, which buys time for the graphs and a final check.

  • If one part stalls you for more than 3 minutes, write what you know, leave a mark, and move on. Later parts usually do not depend on getting the number exactly right.
  • Check each equilibrium by plugging it back in. In Fall 2025, 20 + 30/3 = 30 confirmed Y = 30 in five seconds.
  • In a multi-part story, use the earlier part's numbers even if you doubt them. Graders can follow a consistent mistake.
  • Stop the moment time is called. The penalty is 10 points and it repeats.

What the cheat sheet is for

One page, front and back, is enough room for lookup material and not enough for paragraphs. Use it for the things you would otherwise have to remember exactly under time pressure:

  • Formulas with every symbol defined: PV, the multiplier, MRPL = MR × MPL, real price = nominal price ÷ CPI × 100, the unemployment rate.
  • Step-by-step recipes for each graph: the Keynesian cross, exports and imports at a world price, a tariff, the labor market, the foreign exchange market.
  • "If the question says X, do Y" rules, such as "a fall in the price of the good shifts labor demand in."
  • The handful of midterm facts that multiple choice reaches back for: MB = MC, comparative advantage means lower opportunity cost, and a monopolist raises price and cuts quantity.

Do not plan to read explanations off the sheet during the exam. If you need to read a paragraph to understand an idea, you will not have time. Learn the ideas from the units, then use the sheet only to check a formula or a graph label. The site's Cheat sheet is built from the same units and sized to print on one sheet.
