How the final works

This unit covers the scope, format, grading habits, cheat sheet rules, logistics, and pacing of the December 15 final. It is built from Edwards' Fall 2025 final and its official answer key, his Fall 2025 final review slides, and his Fall 2026 syllabus, course calendar, bCourses announcements, and lectures before the midterm. Nothing here is new economics. It is about turning what you learn in the units into points on paper in 90 minutes.

How he grades

Graduate student instructors (GSIs) score the exams with his answer key. He said so in Class 1. These are the habits that matter, each with where it comes from.

Less is more. Rule 6 on the Fall 2025 final cover said to write legibly, keep your answer inside the box, and "Don't fill the space with words. Less is more." If the grader cannot read it, you get no credit. His official answers on the final were one to four sentences per part.

Graders look for the key's ideas, not its exact words. In Class 1 he read a model answer aloud and said: "You don't have to say exactly that, but you have to kind of get in that general sandbox of answer." Asked whether answers must match the key word for word, he said no, and that shorthand is fine "if it's clear and readable."

"Explain fully" and "Show your work" mean what they say. Eight of the ten Keynesian cross parts on the Fall 2025 final said one or the other. The key's "show your work" answers write the algebra line by line. Its "explain fully" answers give the number, say whether it is an increase or decrease, name the model's reason, and say where it shows up on the graph. In Class 4 he said that on exams "Explain. That's usually what I write," so "you need to have some kind of explanation." A bare number is not a full answer.

Partial credit is real. The syllabus says exams "will be graded on a points system with partial credit possible," and the lowest Fall 2025 midterm score was 11.75, which means graders give quarter points. So never leave a box blank. A correct method with an arithmetic slip still earns something.

Opinion questions reward reasoning, not a side. Fall 2025 Q4(d) asked what you think three news events reveal about the winners and losers of the trade war. In Class 1 he promised: "Political views are never going to affect your score, I promise, never ever." Answer with the model and the evidence in the story.

If a question is unclear, write down how you read it. In Class 9 he said that if a question confuses you, write "I think this is what you mean" and answer that, and "that's at least partial if not full credit."

Some words are required. His keys reward specific vocabulary, and when a question names a concept, use those exact words. This table lists what the Fall 2025 final key used for second-half topics.

How to write a 2-point short answer

Five of last year's 2-point items were short answers in Q2, and ten more 2-point parts sat inside the long questions. His key answers them the same way every time.

  1. Lead with the verdict: "increased," "falls," "different," "No," "raise G by $2 trillion." A grader should find your answer in the first line.
  2. Give one or two sentences of why, using the model and its terms: the CPI grew less than the price, the right side of the present-value formula shrinks, the mpc creates a multiplier.
  3. If numbers are involved, show them: the two growth factors, the algebra, the size of the change with units ("$3 trillion"). Then stop.

The key's Q2 answers were one or two sentences each. A box filled to the edge is a warning sign that you are repeating yourself.

How to answer the multiple choice

Last year's Q1 was six items at 2 points each, with the instruction "circle the one correct answer." This was the only multiple choice he wrote for the course that year; the midterm had none. Three of the six reached back to midterm material, which is why the midterm refresher exists.

  • Read every option before circling. The wrong options are near misses. For comparative advantage, "the task that I do best" was right and "all tasks that I do better than others" (absolute advantage) was the trap.
  • Do not assume a pattern. "All of the above" was correct twice last year (capital, social infrastructure) and a trap once (comparative advantage).
  • Write the units in the margin for exchange rates: "¥ per $ fell" means one dollar buys fewer yen, so the dollar depreciated.
  • Budget about a minute per item. If one stalls you, circle your best guess, mark it, and come back. Nothing on the Fall 2025 cover mentions a penalty for wrong answers, so never leave one blank.

How to do the Keynesian cross computation

Last year's Q3 was 24 points, and the computation parts all follow one recipe. Here it is with the Fall 2025 numbers: C = 10 + (1/3)Y, I = 6, G = 5, NX = −1, in trillions of dollars. C is consumption, I is planned investment, G is government purchases, NX is net exports, and Y is real GDP (output, which also equals income).

  1. Write PAE = C + I + G + NX and substitute every piece: PAE = 10 + (1/3)Y + 6 + 5 − 1.
  2. Collect the numbers that do not depend on Y. That sum is autonomous spending and the vertical intercept: 10 + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20, so PAE = 20 + (1/3)Y. Watch the sign on NX; adding the 1 instead of subtracting it gives 22.
  3. Set PAE = Y and solve, writing each line: 20 + (1/3)Y = Y, so 20 = (2/3)Y, so Y = 30. The shortcut is Y = autonomous spending ÷ (1 − mpc) = 20 ÷ (2/3) = 30, but show the lines anyway.
  4. Check in five seconds: 20 + 30 ÷ 3 = 30. It works.
  5. For a shock, redo steps 1 to 3 with the new numbers. Last year the new intercept was 8 + 5 + 5 + 0 = 18, and Y = 18 ÷ (2/3) = 27.
  6. The change in PAE is the change in the intercept, broken down by component: autonomous consumption −2, investment −1, net exports +1, so −2.
  7. The change in GDP is the multiplier times the change in PAE: 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 1.5, and 1.5 × (−2) = −3. That matches 27 − 30.
  8. To cancel the shock with government purchases, change G by the change in PAE (raise G by 2), not by the change in GDP. With a tax cut instead, PAE rises by only mpc times the cut, so the cut must be larger (his Fall 2025 Class 20 slide 63).

How to draw a 4-point graph

The Fall 2025 final had four graph parts at 4 points each, 16 of the 60 points. His keys are clean and simple, and they share one checklist.

  1. Use the printed axes and their scale. Last year the Keynesian cross axes were numbered 0 to 35 on both sides, the trade graph came with domestic supply and demand already drawn, and the labor graph came as blank axes labeled Wage and Employment.
  2. For the Keynesian cross, draw the 45° line through (0, 0), (10, 10), (20, 20), and so on, because it is the set of points where Y = PAE. Draw PAE from its intercept with slope equal to the mpc: with mpc = 1/3, it rises 1 for every 3 across. Use a ruler.
  3. Mark each equilibrium where PAE crosses the 45° line, with dotted lines to both axes and the value written on each axis (30 and 30).
  4. For a shock that changes only autonomous spending, draw the new PAE parallel to the old one, shifted vertically by the change in PAE. Label both lines, ideally with their equations, and add a small arrow showing the direction.
  5. For exports, draw the world price as a horizontal line above the domestic equilibrium price. Mark quantity demanded and quantity supplied at that price, and bracket the horizontal gap between them as exports.
  6. For a labor market, label the demand curve as MRPL (marginal revenue product of labor) and the supply curve as labor supply. Shift the whole demand curve, keep the original, and mark w1, w2, L1, L2 on the axes.
  7. Write one or two sentences next to the graph if the part says "briefly explain." The key did on every graph part.

The cheat sheet rules

This is certain for the final. In Class 7 he said "You have one page front and back for the midterm and for the ... final exam," and his Fall 2025 review slide said the final had the "Same rules with 1 page of cheatsheet, front and back; open book." In Class 8 he added that it can be typed or handwritten, and you can make it on an iPad, but it must be printed on paper because no screens are allowed. You can build it with friends and each bring a copy, though his advice was to make your own, because "the act of writing it typically means that you'll remember it more."

Two more points from before the midterm still apply. The textbook is allowed but useless for answers, because each exam is written new. And copying old answer keys onto the sheet does not help, because the new questions need new answers.

For the final, these belong on the sheet:

  • The Keynesian cross recipe: PAE = C + I + G + NX, intercept = autonomous spending, Y = autonomous ÷ (1 − mpc), multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), and the rule that G moves PAE one for one while a tax change moves it by mpc times the change.
  • Present value, PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3, with "rate up, price down" and "at i = 0, the price is the sum of the payments."
  • MRPL = MR × MPL and MRPK = MR × MPK (MPK is the marginal product of capital), with what shifts each: the output price shifts MR, and technology or the amount of the other input shifts the marginal product.
  • The real price: real price = nominal price ÷ CPI × 100, or compare the two growth factors.
  • Graph recipes: the Keynesian cross, exports and imports at a world price, a tariff, the labor market, the market for dollars, and the Fed's reaction function.
  • Exchange-rate direction rules with units, written as full sentences.
  • The midterm facts the multiple choice reaches back for: marginal benefit equals marginal cost, comparative advantage means the lower opportunity cost, a monopolist raises price and cuts quantity, inelastic means steep.

Do not plan to read explanations off the sheet. If you need a paragraph to understand an idea, you will not have time in the room. Learn the ideas from the units, and use the sheet to check a formula or a graph label. The site's cheat sheet is built from the same units and sized to print on one sheet, front and back.

Good answer versus weak answer

Each example below is a real Fall 2025 final question (paraphrased), a weak answer of the kind students write, what is wrong with it, and an answer that matches his official key.

Example: Comparative advantage (Fall 2025, Q1(b), multiple choice, 2 points)

The principle of comparative advantage implies that (i) I should specialize in doing the task that I do best, (ii) I should specialize in all tasks that I do better than others, (iii) I should specialize in doing the task that is respected most by others, or (iv) all of the above.

  1. Weak answer: (ii) or (iv). Option (ii) describes absolute advantage, being better than others at a task. If you are better at everything, you still cannot do everything, so (ii) cannot be the rule for specializing.
  2. What the key wants: (i). Your best task is the one where your opportunity cost is lowest relative to others, which is what comparative advantage means.

Answer: (i). When two options sound alike, ask which one is about opportunity cost and which one is about simply being better.

Example: The yen and the dollar (Fall 2025, Q1(d), multiple choice, 2 points)

The yen-dollar exchange rate falls from ¥160 per U.S. dollar to ¥150 per U.S. dollar.

  1. Weak answer: "The yen depreciated, because the number went down." This reads the number without its units.
  2. Read the units: ¥ per $1 is the price of one dollar in yen. One dollar now buys 150 yen instead of 160, so the dollar is worth less: it depreciated.
  3. If the dollar buys fewer yen, the yen buys more dollars, so the yen appreciated.

Answer: (i), the yen appreciated and the dollar depreciated. Write "price of $1 in yen fell" in the margin before choosing.

Example: The real price of a Big Mac (Fall 2025, Q2(a), 2 points)

A Big Mac cost $1.60 in 1986 and $5.65 in 2021, about 3.5 times higher. The CPI (1982 to 1984 = 100) was about 110 in 1986 and about 271 in 2021, about 2.5 times higher. Has the price of a Big Mac increased, decreased, or stayed the same in real, inflation-adjusted terms? Explain.

  1. Weak answer: "Increased, because $5.65 is much more than $1.60." That is the nominal price. The question is about the real price, so the answer must compare the price with the CPI.
  2. Compare growth factors: the price grew about 3.5 times, and prices in general grew about 2.5 times. The Big Mac grew faster than overall prices.
  3. The same thing in real dollars: $1.60 ÷ 110 × 100 = $1.45 in 1982 to 1984 dollars, and $5.65 ÷ 271 × 100 = $2.08. The real price rose.

Answer: His key in full: "The CPI rose by a factor of 2.5, while the Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5. Therefore the real price of a Big Mac has increased." Two sentences: the comparison, then the verdict.

Example: Bond prices and interest rates (Fall 2025, Q2(c) to (e), 2 points each)

The question gave the present value formula for a 3-year bond or claim to capital, PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + F3 ÷ (1 + i)3, and asked what happens to the bond's price when the interest rate i rises, what the price is when i = 0, and what happens to the price of capital when its future marginal product falls.

  1. Weak answer to (c): "The price falls because interest rates went up." The verdict is right, but there is no reason, only the question restated.
  2. Full-credit (c): "It falls. The payments F are fixed, and a higher i makes every denominator larger, so the right side of the formula, the present value, gets smaller. The bond's price is its present value." His key says the price "must fall because the right-hand side decreases when the interest rate i increases."
  3. Full-credit (d): "With i = 0, every 1 + i equals 1, so the price is F1 + F2 + F3." That is the whole answer.
  4. Full-credit (e): "For capital, each F is the marginal revenue product of capital, MR × MPK. If capital's future marginal product (MPK) falls, for example because less labor is expected, the future F values fall, which pulls down the present value and so the price of a unit of capital."

Answer: Every present-value answer has the same shape: say which piece of the formula changes, which way the present value moves, and that the price equals the present value.

Example: "Show your work" (Fall 2025, Q3(b), 2 points)

Using PAE = 20 + (1/3)Y from part (a), set PAE = Y and solve for the short-run equilibrium level of Y. Show your work.

  1. Weak answer: "Y = 30." The number is right, but the question said "show your work," and a bare number gives the grader nothing to award if it is wrong and no proof if it is right.
  2. Full-credit answer: "20 + (1/3)Y = Y, so 20 = (2/3)Y, so Y = 30." That is the key's answer, almost word for word.

Answer: Three short lines of algebra. It takes ten seconds longer than the bare number and protects both points.

Example: Why GDP moved more than PAE (Fall 2025, Q3(i), 2 points)

PAE fell by $2 trillion and GDP fell by $3 trillion. Are those the same or different? If different, which is larger in magnitude, and why? Explain fully, referencing aspects of the model.

  1. Weak answer: "Different, because of the multiplier." This names the right idea, but it skips which one is larger, the numbers, and where the multiplier comes from. Expect partial credit.
  2. What the key wants: the verdict (different), which is larger with both numbers, and the reason in model terms: a nonzero mpc of 1/3 creates a multiplier of 1/(1 − mpc).
  3. Full-credit answer: "Different. The change in GDP, $3 trillion, is larger than the change in PAE, $2 trillion. Because the marginal propensity to consume is 1/3, lower income cuts consumption again in later rounds, which creates a multiplier of 1/(1 − 1/3) = 1.5, and 1.5 × $2 trillion = $3 trillion."

Answer: "Explain fully" means verdict, numbers, and the model's mechanism. Three sentences cover it.

Example: The offsetting change in G (Fall 2025, Q3(j), 2 points)

How big a change in government purchases G would fully counteract the shock, and would it be an increase or a decrease?

  1. Weak answer: "Raise G by $3 trillion, because GDP fell by $3 trillion." This is the most common trap on the question. Raising G by $3 trillion raises PAE by $3 trillion, and through the multiplier it would raise GDP by $4.5 trillion, overshooting.
  2. The shock moved PAE down by $2 trillion. G enters PAE one for one, so raising G by $2 trillion moves PAE back up by exactly $2 trillion, and the multiplier then restores the full $3 trillion of GDP.
  3. Full-credit answer: "Increase G by $2 trillion. The shock cut PAE by $2 trillion, so an equal and opposite $2 trillion increase in G puts the PAE line back where it was. With the same mpc, the multiplier then returns GDP to $30 trillion."

Answer: Match the drop in PAE, not the drop in GDP. His key adds the condition "As long as the marginal propensity to consume remains the same."

Example: The shock graph (Fall 2025, Q3(f), 4 points)

On printed axes, graph the original PAE line, the new PAE' line, and the 45-degree line. Show equilibria and label intercepts.

  1. Weak answer: a 45° line drawn freehand that misses (30, 30), two PAE lines that are not parallel, no intercepts, and no values on the axes. Even with the right idea, the grader cannot see the equilibria or the size of the shift.
  2. Draw the 45° line through (0, 0) and (30, 30) with a ruler.
  3. Draw PAE from 20 with slope 1/3 (it passes through (30, 30)) and PAE' from 18 with the same slope (it passes through (27, 27)). Label each with its equation.
  4. Mark both equilibria with dotted lines to both axes, writing 27 and 30. Add a downward arrow between the lines.

Answer: Labeled lines, both intercepts, both equilibria with dotted lines and values, and a parallel shift. That is what the 4 points pay for.

Example: Exports and the price collapse (Fall 2025, Q4(a) and (b), 4 + 2 points)

On a printed domestic supply and demand graph for U.S. soybeans, show roughly half of quantity supplied exported at a world price. Then China stops buying, the harvest is already planted, and soybeans cannot be stored or destroyed. How far must the price fall?

  1. Weak answer for (a): a world price drawn below the domestic equilibrium (that is the picture for imports), or exports measured as a vertical distance.
  2. Full-credit (a): the world price Pw is a horizontal line above the domestic price, because exporters will only sell abroad at a higher price. Quantity demanded at home is read off demand at Pw, quantity supplied off supply, and the horizontal gap between them is exports.
  3. Weak answer for (b): "The price falls back to the domestic equilibrium." That would be true only if farmers could cut their quantity back to the domestic equilibrium, and the question says they cannot.
  4. Full-credit (b): with quantity stuck at QS and no foreign buyers, the whole harvest must be sold at home, so the price falls all the way to the height of domestic demand at QS, which his key calls Ptw, "a really big reduction."

Answer: Horizontal gaps are quantities traded. A fixed quantity means a vertical supply, and the price is read off demand.

Example: The labor market and winners and losers (Fall 2025, Q4(c) and (d), 4 + 2 points)

Part (c) asked you to show the soybean labor market before and after the trade war, with labor demand equal to MRPL = MR × MPL. Part (d) gave three late-2025 events (a truce, a $12 billion payment to U.S. soybean farmers, and China's record trade surplus) and asked what they reveal about winners and losers.

  1. Weak answer for (c): shifting the labor supply curve, or saying workers became less productive. Nothing happened to workers' productivity (MPL). The price of soybeans, which is the marginal revenue (MR), collapsed.
  2. Full-credit (c): "The trade war cuts the price, or marginal revenue, of soybeans, so the whole labor demand curve MRPL = MR × MPL shifts inward or downward. The wage falls from w1 to w2 and employment from L1 to L2." Draw it with both demand curves and both equilibria labeled.
  3. Weak answer for (d): an opinion about trade policy with no link to the story.
  4. Full-credit (d), in the key's words: the U.S. lost this round and China won, because the U.S. government "still felt it had to compensate the other losers, U.S. farmers," and because manufactured goods, unlike crops, do not spoil, so China's record surplus "suggests its goods found new demanders."

Answer: In the labor market, ask which factor in MR × MPL changed. In a winners-and-losers question, point to the evidence in the story.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Writing only a number when the question says "show your work" or "explain fully."

Correct: Write the algebra lines, or the comparison, and one sentence of what the number means. Eight of last year's ten Keynesian cross parts carried one of those two phrases.

Mistake: Dropping the negative sign on net exports when you add up autonomous spending (10 + 6 + 5 + 1 = 22).

Correct: Net exports of −1 subtract: 10 + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20. Write each term with its sign before adding.

Mistake: Setting the offsetting change in G equal to the change in GDP.

Correct: G must match the change in PAE. The multiplier does the rest. Raising G by the GDP gap overshoots.

Mistake: Assuming a tax cut works dollar for dollar like government purchases.

Correct: A tax cut raises PAE by only mpc times the cut, because households save part of it. His Fall 2025 Class 20 slide 63: "$100 billion increase in G will increase PAE by $100 billion; $100 billion of tax cuts will increase PAE by MPC·$100 billion."

Mistake: Drawing the 45° line freehand, or drawing a PAE line steeper than the 45° line.

Correct: Use a ruler and the printed scale: the 45° line goes through (10, 10), (20, 20), (30, 30). PAE has slope mpc, which is less than 1, so it is always flatter than the 45° line and crosses it from above.

Mistake: Shifting PAE by the change in GDP, or rotating it, when only autonomous spending changed.

Correct: A change in C̄, I, G, or NX shifts PAE vertically by that amount, parallel to the old line. Only a change in the mpc would change the slope.

Mistake: Drawing the world price below the domestic price for an exported good, or measuring exports vertically.

Correct: Exports happen when the world price is above the domestic price. Exports are the horizontal gap between quantity supplied and quantity demanded at the world price. Below the domestic price, the gap is imports.

Mistake: Shifting labor supply, or saying workers became less productive, when the price of the output fell.

Correct: Labor demand is MRPL = MR × MPL. A lower output price lowers MR, so the demand curve shifts in. Labor supply does not move.

Mistake: Calling a nominal price increase a real one.

Correct: Compare the price's growth with the CPI's growth. The real price rises only if the price grew faster than the CPI.

Mistake: Reading "¥ per $ fell" as the yen getting weaker.

Correct: ¥ per $ is the price of a dollar. If it falls, the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated.

Mistake: Leaving a multiple-choice item or a short-answer box blank.

Correct: Circle your best guess and write your best reasoning. Partial credit is in the syllabus, and nothing on the Fall 2025 cover penalized a wrong choice.

Mistake: Forgetting your name and SID on every page, or still writing when time is called.

Correct: Do names first; that was a free point last year. When time is called, put the pen down. The Fall 2025 penalty for continuing was 10 points, then another 10.

Practice questions

Each question below is written the way Edwards writes final exam questions. The skill being practiced is turning what you know into the answer his key rewards. Write what you would put in the box, or sketch the graph, before you open the model answer. All numbers and stories are made up for practice.

2 points If the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, the multiplier in the Keynesian cross is

(i) 0.75
(ii) 1.33
(iii) 4
(iv) 7.5

Show answer

(iii). The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. Option (ii) is the trap: 1 ÷ 0.75 = 1.33 divides by the mpc instead of by 1 − mpc.

Full credit: (iii) circled.

2 points If the exchange rate rises from €0.85 per U.S. dollar to €0.95 per U.S. dollar, then

(i) The dollar has appreciated and the euro has depreciated
(ii) The euro has appreciated and the dollar has depreciated
(iii) Both currencies have appreciated
(iv) We cannot tell based on this information

Show answer

(i). Euros per dollar is the price of one dollar in euros. It rose, so a dollar now buys more euros: the dollar appreciated. If a dollar buys more euros, a euro buys fewer dollars, so the euro depreciated. This is last year's yen question run in the other direction.

Full credit: (i) circled.

2 points Which of these counts in this year's U.S. GDP?

(i) A used car resold this year
(ii) Shares of stock bought this year
(iii) A new house built and sold this year
(iv) A Social Security payment to a retiree

Show answer

(iii). GDP counts the market value of final goods and services produced in the country this year. The used car was produced in an earlier year, a stock purchase is a financial trade and not production, and a Social Security payment is a transfer, not a purchase of anything produced. A new house built this year is new production (it counts as investment). See unit 06.

Full credit: (iii) circled.

2 points If the Federal Reserve lowers real interest rates, then in the Keynesian cross

(i) The 45-degree line rotates upward
(ii) The PAE line shifts up, because investment and some consumption rise
(iii) The PAE line shifts down, because households save more
(iv) Nothing changes, because only fiscal policy shifts PAE

Show answer

(ii). Lower real rates make borrowing cheaper, so planned investment rises, and so do interest-sensitive parts of consumption like new car purchases. His Fall 2025 review slide 47 lists those effects and adds that a currency depreciation raises net exports too. All of these raise PAE at every level of output. The 45-degree line never moves; it is just the set of points where Y = PAE. See unit 10.

Full credit: (ii) circled.

2 points A firm in a perfectly competitive market maximizes profit by producing the quantity where

(i) Price equals average cost
(ii) Marginal revenue, which equals the price, equals marginal cost
(iii) Total revenue is as large as possible
(iv) Marginal cost is at its lowest

Show answer

(ii). A price-taking firm gets the market price for each extra unit, so its marginal revenue is the price. It keeps producing while an extra unit adds more revenue than cost, and stops where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. His Fall 2025 review slide 27 says "Profits are largest at q1, where mr=mc." This is the kind of midterm item last year's multiple choice reached back for. See unit 13.

Full credit: (ii) circled.

2 points Suppose a movie ticket cost $4.00 in 1990 and $12.00 in 2025, and the CPI (1982 to 1984 = 100) rose from 130 to 325 over the same years. Has the real price of a movie ticket increased, decreased, or stayed the same? Explain. A student wrote: "Increased, because $12 is three times $4." Grade it and write the full-credit answer.

Show answer

The student compares nominal prices only and never uses the CPI, so the reasoning does not answer a question about real prices. Expect partial credit at best, even though the verdict happens to be right.

Full-credit answer: "The ticket price rose by a factor of 3 ($12 ÷ $4), while the CPI rose by a factor of 2.5 (325 ÷ 130). The ticket rose faster than prices in general, so its real price increased. In 1982 to 1984 dollars it went from $4.00 ÷ 130 × 100 = $3.08 to $12.00 ÷ 325 × 100 = $3.69."

Full credit: both growth factors (or both real prices) and the verdict "increased," as in his Big Mac key.

2 points A one-year bond pays $1,100 in one year. What is its price when the interest rate is 10%? What happens to its price if the interest rate rises to 21%? Show your work.

Show answer

"The price is the present value of the payment. At 10%: PV = $1,100 ÷ 1.10 = $1,000.00. At 21%: PV = $1,100 ÷ 1.21 = $909.09. The price falls by $90.91, because the payment is fixed and a higher interest rate makes the denominator larger."

Full credit: $1,000.00 and $909.09 with the division shown, and "the price falls" with the fixed-payment reason.

2 points A three-year bond pays $50 at the end of year 1, $50 at the end of year 2, and $1,050 at the end of year 3. What is its price if the interest rate is zero? Would its price be higher or lower at an interest rate of 5%?

Show answer

"At i = 0, every 1 + i equals 1, so the price is the sum of the payments: $50 + $50 + $1,050 = $1,150. At 5%, every payment is divided by more than 1, so the price would be lower than $1,150."

This is Fall 2025 Q2(d) with numbers. His key's answer was just PV = F1 + F2 + F3.

Full credit: $1,150 as the sum of the payments, and lower at 5%.

2 points Suppose new AI software is expected to make every data-center server more productive in future years, starting next year. What happens to the price of a server today? Explain using the present value of the server's future returns.

Show answer

"The price of a server rises. Each year's return from a server is its marginal revenue product, MRPK = MR × MPK. Better software raises the server's future marginal product of capital (MPK), so the future returns Ft rise. The price of a unit of capital is the present value of those returns, so it rises."

This is Fall 2025 Q2(e) in the other direction. There, an expected future fall in labor lowered capital's future marginal product and pulled its price down.

Full credit: price rises, with the chain: future MPK up, future MRPK up, present value up.

2 points In a Keynesian cross with mpc = 0.5, a shock lowers PAE by $4 trillion at every level of output. A question asks whether the change in PAE and the change in GDP are the same or different, which is larger, and why. A student wrote: "Different, because of the multiplier." Grade it and write the full-credit answer.

Show answer

The student has the verdict and names the right idea, but does not say which change is larger, gives no numbers, and does not connect the multiplier to the mpc. That is the "weak answer" pattern from Fall 2025 Q3(i), so expect partial credit.

Full-credit answer: "Different. GDP falls by $8 trillion, which is larger than the $4 trillion drop in PAE. Because the mpc is 0.5, the first drop in income cuts consumption, which cuts income again, round after round. That creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2, and 2 × $4 trillion = $8 trillion."

Full credit: different; GDP larger, with both numbers; the mpc and the multiplier 1/(1 − mpc).

4 points Suppose an economy's spending, in trillions of dollars, is C = 3 + 0.75Y, I = 4, G = 5, and NX = −2.

(a) (2 points) Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find the vertical intercept. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Set PAE = Y and solve for the short-run equilibrium level of Y. Show your work.

Show answer

(a) "PAE = C + I + G + NX = 3 + 0.75Y + 4 + 5 − 2 = 10 + 0.75Y. When Y = 0, PAE = 10, which is the vertical intercept."

(b) "10 + 0.75Y = Y, so 10 = 0.25Y, so Y = 40." Check: 10 + 0.75 × 40 = 10 + 30 = 40.

The trap is the sign on net exports: adding 2 instead of subtracting it gives an intercept of 14 and Y = 56.

Full credit: (a) the substitution line and an intercept of 10; (b) the algebra lines and Y = 40.

8 points How do you like them apples? Suppose U.S. apple growers sell at home and export to one large foreign buyer. Domestic demand is P = 10 − 0.5Q and domestic supply is P = 0.5Q, where Q is millions of boxes per year and P is dollars per box. The world price is $7 per box.

(a) (4 points) Draw the domestic market. Show the price and quantity with no trade, the world price, and the quantity of exports, with values.

(b) (2 points) After the apples are picked, the foreign buyer imposes steep tariffs and stops buying, and no one else abroad will buy U.S. apples at any price. Apples cannot be stored. What happens to the price of apples? Show your work.

(c) (2 points) Labor demand for apple pickers is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL. If growers expect the price to stay this low next season, what happens to the labor market for apple pickers? Briefly explain.

Show answer

(a) With no trade, set demand equal to supply: 10 − 0.5Q = 0.5Q, so Q = 10 million boxes and P = $5. At the world price of $7, which is above $5, growers export. Quantity supplied: 0.5Q = 7, so Q = 14. Quantity demanded at home: 10 − 0.5Q = 7, so Q = 6. Exports are the horizontal gap, 14 − 6 = 8 million boxes.

(b) "The harvest is fixed at 14 million boxes and cannot be stored, so all of it must be sold at home. The price falls to where domestic demand meets 14: P = 10 − 0.5 × 14 = $3. That is below even the $5 no-trade price, because there are more apples at home than the no-trade quantity of 10."

(c) "The price of apples is the marginal revenue of each box, and it fell from $7 to $3. Since MRPL = MR × MPL, the value of each picker's marginal product falls at every level of employment, so labor demand shifts inward. Along an unchanged labor supply curve, the wage and the number of pickers both fall."

Full credit: (a) world price above the no-trade price, QD = 6, QS = 14, exports of 8 shown as a horizontal gap, no-trade point at (10, $5), curves labeled; (b) $3 read off demand at the fixed 14, with the substitution shown; (c) MR falls, so MRPL shifts in, and the wage and employment fall.

Story question: "The center cannot hold" (12 points)

This one is built like Fall 2025 Q3, at half the length. Suppose that in a hypothetical economy, GDP is $32 trillion, the marginal propensity to consume is 1/2, and planned aggregate expenditure, in trillions of dollars, is modeled by C = 6 + (1/2)Y, I = 6, G = 5, and NX = −1. Then a data-center building boom ends suddenly. Planned investment falls to I' = 4, and worried households cut their autonomous consumption, so C' = 5 + (1/2)Y. Government purchases and net exports do not change.

2 points (a) Combine the expenditure terms in PAE, find the vertical intercept, and solve for the short-run equilibrium level of Y before the bust. Show your work.

Show answer

"PAE = C + I + G + NX = 6 + (1/2)Y + 6 + 5 − 1 = 16 + (1/2)Y, so the intercept is 16. Setting PAE = Y: 16 + (1/2)Y = Y, so 16 = (1/2)Y, so Y = 32." Check: 16 + 32 ÷ 2 = 32, which matches the $32 trillion GDP in the story.

Full credit: intercept 16 and Y = 32 with the algebra lines shown.

2 points (b) Find the new intercept and the new short-run equilibrium after the bust. Show your work.

Show answer

"PAE' = C' + I' + G + NX = 5 + (1/2)Y + 4 + 5 − 1 = 13 + (1/2)Y, so the intercept is 13. Setting PAE' = Y: 13 + (1/2)Y = Y, so 13 = (1/2)Y, so Y = 26." Check: 13 + 26 ÷ 2 = 26.

Full credit: intercept 13 and Y = 26 with the algebra lines shown.

4 points (c) On axes running from 0 to 40 on both sides, graph the original PAE line, the new PAE' line, and the 45-degree line. Show both equilibria and label intercepts.

Show answer

Draw the 45° line through (0, 0) and (40, 40). Draw PAE from 16 with slope 1/2, so it rises 1 for every 2 across and reaches (32, 32). Draw PAE' from 13 with the same slope, reaching (26, 26). Mark both equilibria with dotted lines to both axes and label each line.

Full credit: 45° line through the origin at a true 45 degrees; both PAE lines labeled with intercepts 16 and 13 and the same slope of 1/2; equilibria at (32, 32) and (26, 26) with dotted lines and values on both axes.

2 points (d) By how much did PAE change, and by how much did GDP change? Are the two the same or different? If different, which is larger, and why? Explain fully, referencing aspects of the model.

Show answer

"PAE decreased by $3 trillion: autonomous consumption fell by $1 trillion and investment fell by $2 trillion, a vertical drop of 3 in the PAE line. GDP decreased by $6 trillion, from 32 to 26. The two are different, and the change in GDP is larger. Because the mpc is 1/2, lower income cuts consumption again in later rounds, which creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 1/2) = 2, and 2 × $3 trillion = $6 trillion."

Full credit: both changes as decreases with numbers ($3 trillion and $6 trillion), the components of the PAE drop, "different," GDP larger, and the multiplier 1/(1 − mpc) = 2 from the mpc.

2 points (e) Congress considers two bills that would take effect quickly. Bill A raises government purchases G by $3 trillion. Bill B cuts taxes by $3 trillion, which raises households' disposable income by $3 trillion. Which bill would fully restore GDP to $32 trillion? For the bill that falls short, how large would it need to be? Explain fully.

Show answer

"Bill A fully restores GDP. G enters PAE one for one, so raising G by $3 trillion moves the intercept from 13 back to 16, and Y returns to 32. Bill B falls short. Households spend only the mpc of each extra dollar, so a $3 trillion tax cut raises consumption by (1/2) × $3 trillion = $1.5 trillion. The intercept rises only to 14.5, and Y = 14.5 ÷ (1/2) = 29. To restore GDP to 32, the tax cut would need to be $6 trillion, because (1/2) × $6 trillion = $3 trillion."

This is Fall 2025 Q3(j) with the tax option added from his Fall 2025 Class 20 slide 63: a dollar of G moves PAE by a dollar, and a dollar of tax cuts moves it by the mpc.

Full credit: Bill A works because it matches the $3 trillion drop in PAE; Bill B raises PAE by only mpc × $3 trillion = $1.5 trillion and would need to be $6 trillion.
