Class 19 · Short-run macro fluctuations

Recessions and the Keynesian cross

In the short run, output (real GDP, written Y) is set by planned aggregate expenditure (PAE), the total of planned consumption C, planned investment I, government purchases G, and net exports NX. A change in planned spending moves GDP by more than the change itself because of the multiplier, 1/(1 − mpc), where the mpc (marginal propensity to consume) is the share of each extra dollar of income that households spend. This is the most important unit for the final: Fall 2025 Q3 was a 24-point Keynesian cross problem (40% of the exam) that asked you to combine C + I + G + NX, find the intercept, solve PAE = Y, graph it, shift it, compare the change in PAE with the change in GDP, explain the multiplier, and find the change in G that undoes the shock.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Thursday, November 5, 2026.

The ideas, explained

Recessions, expansions, and potential output

The last unit was about potential output Y*, the output the economy produces when it uses its resources at normal rates. Actual GDP does not sit exactly on Y*. His slide 18 plots U.S. real GDP against potential GDP: most of the time they are close, and in recessions actual GDP drops below potential for a while.

Short-run fluctuations are the times when output moves above or below potential: booms and recessions. Compared with decades of growth, they look small on a chart. Edwards' slide contrasts two views. In a "rational model" (Robert Lucas), people should care about long-term growth more than short-term ups and downs. In the real world, short-run fluctuations matter a lot to people and affect elections, partly because losses are more noticeable to people than gains.

Recessions are costly and painful, mostly for the people who lose their jobs. His slide 21 lists the damage beyond lost income: self-reported happiness and self-esteem drop sharply on becoming unemployed, unemployment is linked to more depression and anxiety, and "Half of American households don't have any savings at hand to cushion blow from lost earnings." His slide 20 shows the deviation of GDP from potential moving opposite to the unemployment rate: when output falls below potential, unemployment rises.

Output gaps

The textbook gives names to these deviations. The output gap is the difference between actual output and potential output, Y − Y* (often stated as a percent of Y*).

A recessionary gap is when Y < Y*: output is below potential, resources (especially workers) sit idle, and unemployment is above normal. An expansionary gap is when Y > Y*: output is above potential, firms and workers are running harder than normal, and (as his review slides say) inflation tends to rise over time.

The textbook also connects the gap to unemployment. The natural rate of unemployment, u*, is the unemployment rate when output is at potential (frictional plus structural unemployment). Unemployment above u* is cyclical unemployment, the part caused by a recession. Okun's law in the textbook says each extra percentage point of cyclical unemployment goes with about a 2 percentage point larger output gap (as a percent of potential). His Class 19 slides show the relationship as a picture (slide 20) but do not state Okun's law, so treat the "about 2" number as textbook background, not something he has emphasized.

Why demand sets output in the short run

In the long run, output equals potential output, which depends on capital, labor, and technology. Edwards calls these "supply." In the short run, output can be above, below, or equal to potential, and it is determined mainly by demand: what consumers, firms, and the government are buying.

The reason is that prices do not change quickly or completely enough to keep output at potential. His list of reasons: limited information, long-term contracts, consumers disliking "unfair" price changes, workers disliking wage cuts, and other factors. Keynes built his framework after the Great Depression to explain exactly this.

His analogy is a mom-and-pop restaurant in Berkeley. In the short run its menu prices are fixed, so how many meals it serves depends only on how many customers walk in: busy on some nights, partly idle on others. In the long run the owners adjust prices: if demand is always high they raise prices, and if it is always low they cut them, until they have a normal workload. If prices adjusted perfectly and immediately, there would be no short-run business fluctuations, apart from real shocks like weather for farmers or COVID. Keynes's contribution was to say the short run needs a different analysis from the long run.

Real and nominal interest rates

The deck starts with the Fisher relation from Chapter 18, because spending responds to the real interest rate. The nominal interest rate i is the stated rate, in dollars, with no adjustment for prices. The real interest rate r is the rate measured in purchasing power.

i = r + π, or r = i − π, where π is the inflation rate. (Precisely, π should be expected inflation.) His example: i = 10% and π = 9% gives r = 1%.

Edwards describes the real interest rate as the price of spending today: the opportunity cost of what you give up tomorrow by spending now. A higher r makes people and firms spend less now. The Great Depression shows why this matters. After the 1929 crash nominal interest rates fell, but consumer prices collapsed (deflation, so π was negative). With r = i − π, a negative π pushes the real rate up, and his slide 12 shows the real interest rate soaring at first, which made things much worse. A made-up example: if i = 2% and prices fall 8% a year (π = −8%), then r = 2 − (−8) = 10%.

In the long run, saving and investment respond to the normal real interest rate r* (his slide 17): investment demand slopes down like a demand curve, with the real interest rate as the price of investment, and saving is the supply of funds for investment, probably sloping up.

Planned aggregate expenditure

Recall that GDP is Y = C + I + G + NX. His slide 25 gives the rough U.S. shares: consumption 65 to 70% of GDP, investment 15 to 20%, government purchases 15 to 20%, and net exports (exports minus imports) about −3%.

Planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) is the total amount that households, businesses, and the government plan to buy. His slides note that "planned aggregate expenditure," "planned spending," and "planned aggregate demand" all mean the same thing.

PAE = C + IP + G + NX. C is consumption, IP is planned investment, G is government purchases, and NX is net exports (exports minus imports). On the exam he writes I for planned investment.

"Planned" does not mean good for the economy. His slide says it means "optimal for me, forget you," and that is the challenge: each household and firm plans its own spending without regard to the effect on others' incomes.

What moves each piece:

  • Planned investment: the real interest rate (a higher r reduces it) and expectations about future demand, which Keynes called "animal spirits." His slide 34 shows investment swinging much more than GDP over the business cycle.
  • Government purchases: politics, wars, natural disasters. In the model, G is taken as given.
  • Net exports: for now, also taken as given. The economics of NX comes in the international unit.
  • Consumption: the real interest rate (a higher r reduces current consumption; think car sales), expectations about future income ("consumer confidence," measured by the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index on slide 38), wealth (a stock market boom raises consumption), and disposable income.

The consumption function

Disposable income is income after taxes: Y − T, where T is net taxes (taxes minus transfers back to households, such as Social Security benefits). Consumption rises with disposable income. The slides write it in a straight-line form:

C = C̄ + mpc × (Y − T). C̄ ("C bar") is autonomous consumption, the part of consumption that does not depend on income. The marginal propensity to consume (mpc, written c on his slides) is the change in consumption from one more dollar of disposable income, between 0 and 1.

The economy's mpc is an average over everyone. Edwards notes that people with low incomes tend to have an mpc near 1 (they spend each extra dollar), while people with high incomes have a much lower mpc, closer to 0, partly because their needs are small relative to their income and partly because of their wealth. The Fall 2025 final used mpc = 1/3 as "a reasonable estimate of the aggregate marginal propensity to consume."

When there are no taxes in the problem (as in Fall 2025 Q3), the consumption function is just C = C̄ + mpc × Y, for example C = 10 + (1/3)Y.

The expenditure line: autonomous spending plus mpc × Y

PAE depends on Y because one of its pieces, consumption, depends on income. Substitute the consumption function into PAE (his slide 45):

PAE = (C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX) + mpc × Y. The part in parentheses does not depend on Y. It is autonomous spending, and it is the vertical intercept of the PAE line. The slope is the mpc.

So to "combine the expenditure terms," add up every piece that is not multiplied by Y, keeping signs (a net export number of −1 subtracts 1), and keep the mpc × Y term separate. With C = 10 + (1/3)Y, I = 6, G = 5, NX = −1: PAE = 10 + 6 + 5 − 1 + (1/3)Y = 20 + (1/3)Y. If there are taxes, note that T enters as −mpc × T, not −T: with C = 3 + 0.5(Y − 2), consumption is 3 − 1 + 0.5Y = 2 + 0.5Y.

The words induced and autonomous help. Autonomous spending is the spending that happens whatever income is. Induced spending, mpc × Y, is the extra consumption that happens because income is higher.

The 45° line and short-run equilibrium

The Keynesian cross diagram has output (income) Y on the horizontal axis and planned spending PAE on the vertical axis. It has two lines:

  • The 45° line, Y = PAE. Every point on it has output equal to planned spending. It starts at the origin and has slope 1, so it makes a 45° angle only if both axes use the same scale. Edwards says it captures the equilibrium condition, and also the fact that in the short run output responds to planned spending, like the restaurant serving whoever walks in.
  • The expenditure line, PAE = autonomous spending + mpc × Y. It starts at autonomous spending on the vertical axis and has slope mpc. Since the mpc is less than 1, it is flatter than the 45° line, so the two lines cross exactly once.

Short-run equilibrium output is where the lines cross: Y = PAE. Setting Y = A + mpc × Y (A is autonomous spending) and solving gives Y = A ÷ (1 − mpc).

The algebra is the same every time. Fall 2025 Q3(b): Y = 20 + (1/3)Y, so Y − (1/3)Y = 20, so (2/3)Y = 20, so Y = 20 ÷ (2/3) = 30.

Planned and actual investment: how the economy gets to equilibrium

GDP counts what is produced. Goods produced but not yet sold go into inventories, and inventories count as investment in GDP (his slide 33). So actual investment can differ from planned investment by the unplanned change in inventories:

Y − PAE = unplanned inventory investment. Actual investment = planned investment + unplanned inventory investment. At equilibrium, Y = PAE, so unplanned inventory investment is zero and actual investment equals planned investment.

  • If output is above equilibrium, Y > PAE: firms produce more than people buy, unsold goods pile up, and firms cut production. Output falls toward equilibrium.
  • If output is below equilibrium, Y < PAE: people buy more than firms produce, shelves run down, and firms increase production. Output rises toward equilibrium.

Shocks shift the PAE line

Anything that changes planned spending at a given level of income shifts the expenditure line. A change in autonomous consumption C̄, planned investment I, government purchases G, net exports NX, or taxes T changes autonomous spending, which moves the intercept. The slope stays the mpc, so the new line is parallel to the old one. A change in income itself is a movement along the line, not a shift.

A fall in any part of autonomous spending shifts PAE down in parallel and lowers equilibrium Y. A rise shifts it up and raises Y. Only a change in the mpc changes the slope.

His main example is a fall in autonomous consumption after the stock market crash of October 1929. A fall in stock prices can cut spending at a given income because the loss of wealth makes consumers feel poorer, because it makes them pessimistic (lower consumer confidence), and because volatility creates uncertainty and "wait and see" behavior. Christina Romer's data on his slide 54 show retail sales collapsing in the months after the crash. Compared with September 1929, just before the crash, automobile registrations were down 23.7% by January 1930, while grocery store sales were still up 5.9% in October 1929. The slide's note: the crash hit durable goods like autos and furniture but not everyday necessities like groceries, and the collapse in demand then led to a collapse in production.

His final review deck makes the general point: "A negative shock to any of these expenditure components can shift PAE downward." The Fall 2025 final's shock hit three components at once: consumption (tariffs raising prices), investment (an AI data-center boom that could go bust), and net exports.

The multiplier

When autonomous spending changes, equilibrium output changes by more than the change in spending. That is the multiplier effect. The reason is that my spending is your income. When autonomous spending falls by $1, someone's income falls by $1. They cut their consumption by mpc × $1. That cut is someone else's lost income, so they cut consumption by mpc × mpc × $1, and so on (his slide 61):

Total change in Y = 1 + mpc + mpc2 + mpc3 + ... = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc).

multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) and ΔY = ΔPAE ÷ (1 − mpc), where ΔPAE is the change in autonomous spending (the vertical shift of the PAE line). mpc = 1/3 gives 1.5; 1/2 gives 2; 0.6 gives 2.5; 2/3 gives 3; 0.75 gives 4; 0.8 gives 5.

On the graph, the multiplier is the reason the horizontal move is bigger than the vertical shift. The PAE line drops by ΔPAE. Because the line slopes up and is flatter than the 45° line, the crossing point slides down the 45° line by more than that. A flatter PAE line (smaller mpc) means a smaller multiplier; a steeper one (mpc close to 1) means a big one.

The multiplier explains why moderate changes in planned spending can cause much larger changes in output. His slide 58 shows U.S. real GDP falling steeply after 1929.

Change in PAE versus change in GDP

Fall 2025 Q3 parts (g), (h), and (i) test whether you can keep these two numbers apart.

  • The change in PAE is the change in autonomous spending: add up the changes in C̄, I, G, and NX. It is the vertical shift of the PAE line at every level of Y.
  • The change in GDP is the change in equilibrium Y: the horizontal distance between the two crossings with the 45° line.
  • They are different whenever the mpc is above zero. The change in GDP is larger, by the factor 1 ÷ (1 − mpc).

In Fall 2025 Q3, autonomous consumption fell by 2, investment fell by 1, and net exports rose by 1, so PAE fell by 2 trillion. GDP fell from 30 to 27, by 3 trillion. The ratio 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 is the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3).

Offsetting a shock with government purchases

G enters PAE one for one, so raising G by $1 shifts the PAE line up by $1, just like any other autonomous spending. To undo a shock completely, change G by the same amount as the change in PAE, in the opposite direction. The same multiplier applies to both, so the effects cancel.

To offset a shock, set ΔG = −ΔPAE (the vertical shift), not −ΔY. In Fall 2025 Q3(j), PAE fell by $2 trillion, so Congress should raise G by $2 trillion, not $3 trillion.

The same logic closes an output gap. If equilibrium output is below potential by a recessionary gap, the needed change in autonomous spending is the gap times (1 − mpc), because the multiplier will do the rest. Class 20 (fiscal policy) builds this out with taxes.

Short run versus long run

In the short run, PAE can be more than, less than, or equal to Y*, and output adjusts to match PAE (the Keynesian analysis). In the long run, output is at Y*, set by normal capital, labor, and technology, and PAE adjusts to equal Y*; movement in the long-run real interest rate r* brings this about (the classical analysis). Edwards called the Keynesian analysis a true paradigm shift in economics: from the classical view, where prices do all the adjusting, it looks almost absurd, yet it explains the Depression. The later classes add government policy (Class 20), the Fed (Class 21), and the slow return to potential through inflation (Class 22).

Worked examples

Example 1: Fall 2025 Q3, "We Keynesian Cross Ourselves" (all 24 points)

The story: in 2025, U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion, a reasonable estimate of the aggregate mpc is 1/3, and planned spending in trillions of dollars is C = 10 + (1/3)Y, I = 6, G = 5, NX = −1 (net exports are negative $1 trillion). Then tariffs, a possible AI bubble, and immigration enforcement combine into a new situation in early 2026: C' = 8 + (1/3)Y, I' = 5, G = 5, NX' = 0.

  1. (a) Intercept, 2 points. PAE = C + I + G + NX = 10 + (1/3)Y + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20 + (1/3)Y. When Y = 0, PAE = 20. That is the vertical intercept.
  2. (b) Equilibrium, 2 points. Set PAE = Y: 20 + (1/3)Y = Y. Then 20 = (2/3)Y, so Y = 30. (It matches the story's $30 trillion GDP.)
  3. (c) Graph, 4 points. Equal scales on both axes. The 45° line from the origin. The PAE line from 20 on the vertical axis with slope 1/3. The crossing at (30, 30), with 30 marked on both axes and 20 marked as the intercept. See the figure in "The 45° line and short-run equilibrium" above.
  4. (d) New intercept, 2 points. PAE' = C' + I' + G + NX' = 8 + (1/3)Y + 5 + 5 + 0 = 18 + (1/3)Y. When Y = 0, PAE' = 18.
  5. (e) New equilibrium, 2 points. 18 + (1/3)Y = Y, so 18 = (2/3)Y, so Y = 27.
  6. (f) Graph both, 4 points. The original PAE line (intercept 20), the parallel PAE' line below it (intercept 18, same slope 1/3), and the 45° line, with equilibria at 30 and 27 labeled on both axes.
  7. (g) Change in PAE, 2 points. PAE decreased by $2 trillion. Autonomous consumption fell by $2 trillion and investment fell by $1 trillion, while net exports rose by $1 trillion: −2 − 1 + 1 = −2. On the graph, the PAE line drops vertically by 2 at every Y.
  8. (h) Change in GDP, 2 points. GDP decreased by $3 trillion, from 30 to 27. The PAE line dropped by 2, and because it slopes upward, its crossing with the 45° line moves down and left by 3.
  9. (i) Same or different, 2 points. Different. The change in GDP ($3 trillion) is larger than the change in PAE ($2 trillion) because the mpc is 1/3, not zero, which creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 1.5. Lower spending lowers income, which lowers consumption, which lowers income again. 2 × 1.5 = 3.
  10. (j) Offsetting G, 2 points. Raise G by $2 trillion. The shock cut PAE by $2 trillion; as long as the mpc stays the same, an equal and opposite $2 trillion increase in G restores PAE to 20 + (1/3)Y and Y to 30. Check: 8 + 5 + 7 + 0 = 20.

Answer: (a) 20; (b) 30; (d) 18; (e) 27; (g) PAE down $2 trillion; (h) GDP down $3 trillion; (i) different, GDP change larger because of the multiplier 1/(1 − mpc) = 1.5; (j) raise G by $2 trillion. The key's explanations were two or three sentences each: say the number, say increase or decrease, and say what it looks like on the graph.

Example 2: Unplanned inventories

Using the Fall 2025 economy, PAE = 20 + (1/3)Y with planned investment I = 6. Suppose firms produce Y = 36. What happens to inventories and actual investment, and what do firms do next? Repeat for Y = 24.

  1. At Y = 36: PAE = 20 + 12 = 32. Firms produce 36 but buyers plan to spend 32, so 36 − 32 = 4 of goods go unsold into inventories.
  2. Actual investment = planned 6 + unplanned 4 = 10. Firms did not want those extra inventories, so they cut production toward 30.
  3. At Y = 24: PAE = 20 + 8 = 28. Buyers want 28 but only 24 is produced, so inventories run down by 4. Actual investment = 6 − 4 = 2, and firms raise production toward 30.

Answer: Above equilibrium, unplanned inventory buildup (+4) leads firms to cut output; below it, unplanned inventory depletion (−4) leads firms to raise output. Only at Y = 30 is actual investment equal to planned investment.

Example 3: The multiplier round by round

In Fall 2025 Q3, PAE fell by $2 trillion with mpc = 1/3. Show how the total fall in GDP builds up to $3 trillion.

  1. Round 1: spending falls by 2, so income falls by 2.
  2. Round 2: households cut consumption by 1/3 of their lost income: 2 × 1/3 = 0.667.
  3. Round 3: 0.667 × 1/3 = 0.222. Round 4: 0.074. Each round is 1/3 of the one before.
  4. After four rounds the total is 2 + 0.667 + 0.222 + 0.074 = 2.963, closing in on 2 × 1/(1 − 1/3) = 3.

Answer: GDP falls by 2 × 1.5 = $3 trillion. The extra $1 trillion beyond the initial $2 trillion is induced consumption lost as income falls.

Example 4: Taxes in the consumption function

A made-up economy (in trillions of dollars) has C = 4 + 0.75(Y − T), net taxes T = 4, I = 3, G = 4, and NX = −1. Find PAE, its intercept, and equilibrium Y.

  1. Consumption: 4 + 0.75(Y − 4) = 4 − 3 + 0.75Y = 1 + 0.75Y. Taxes lower autonomous consumption by mpc × T = 0.75 × 4 = 3, not by 4, because households would have spent only 75% of that money.
  2. PAE = 1 + 0.75Y + 3 + 4 − 1 = 7 + 0.75Y. Intercept 7.
  3. Y = 7 + 0.75Y, so 0.25Y = 7, so Y = 28. Check: 7 + 0.75 × 28 = 7 + 21 = 28.

Answer: PAE = 7 + 0.75Y, intercept 7, Y = 28. The tax term enters autonomous spending as −mpc × T.

Example 5: How much G closes a recessionary gap

PAE = 10 + 0.5Y and potential output is Y* = 24 (made-up numbers). Find the gap and the change in G that closes it.

  1. Equilibrium: 0.5Y = 10, so Y = 20.
  2. Gap: Y − Y* = 20 − 24 = −4, a recessionary gap of 4.
  3. Multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2. Needed change in autonomous spending: 4 ÷ 2 = 2.
  4. Raise G by 2: PAE = 12 + 0.5Y, so 0.5Y = 12 and Y = 24. Gap closed.

Answer: Recessionary gap of 4; raise G by 2, which is the gap × (1 − mpc).

Example 6: Real interest rates

(a) A bank pays a nominal rate of 5% and inflation is 3%. What is the real rate? (b) In a deflation, the nominal rate is 1% and prices fall 6% a year. What is the real rate, and what does it do to spending?

  1. (a) r = i − π = 5 − 3 = 2%.
  2. (b) r = 1 − (−6) = 7%. Even with a nominal rate near zero, falling prices make the real cost of borrowing high.
  3. A higher real rate lowers planned investment and consumption (think car purchases), so autonomous spending falls and the PAE line shifts down.

Answer: (a) 2%. (b) 7%, which depresses spending; this is the early-1930s story on his slides 10 to 12.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Answering Q3(j) with the change in GDP: "GDP fell by 3, so raise G by 3."

Correct: Offset the change in PAE: raise G by 2. G gets the same multiplier as the shock, so a $2 trillion rise in G undoes a $2 trillion fall in autonomous spending.

Mistake: Saying the changes in PAE and GDP are the same, or that GDP changes by less.

Correct: With any mpc above zero, GDP changes by more: ΔY = ΔPAE ÷ (1 − mpc).

Mistake: Using the wrong multiplier: 1 ÷ mpc, 1 ÷ (1 + mpc), or just mpc.

Correct: 1 ÷ (1 − mpc). Sanity check: it must be larger than 1. With mpc = 1/3 it is 1.5, not 3.

Mistake: Dropping the sign on net exports. With NX = −1, writing 10 + 6 + 5 + 1 = 22.

Correct: 10 + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20. Also, when NX goes from −1 to 0, net exports rose by 1, which partly offsets the other shocks.

Mistake: Drawing the shocked PAE line with a different slope, or drawing the 45° line on unequal axis scales so it does not actually bisect the corner.

Correct: Shocks to C̄, I, G, NX, or T shift PAE in parallel; only the mpc sets the slope. Use the same scale on both axes and start the 45° line at the origin.

Mistake: Treating the intercept as the equilibrium, or labeling only one axis.

Correct: The intercept is PAE at Y = 0 (autonomous spending). The equilibrium is where PAE crosses the 45° line; label its Y on the horizontal axis and the same value on the vertical axis.

Mistake: Subtracting all of T from autonomous spending: C = 3 + 0.5(Y − 2) becomes 1 + 0.5Y.

Correct: Taxes enter as −mpc × T: 3 − 0.5 × 2 + 0.5Y = 2 + 0.5Y.

Mistake: Saying firms raise production when unsold goods pile up.

Correct: If Y > PAE, inventories build up unexpectedly and firms cut production. If Y < PAE, inventories run down and firms raise production.

Mistake: Calling Y > Y* a recessionary gap.

Correct: Below potential is recessionary; above potential is expansionary.

Mistake: Treating a change in income as a shift of the PAE line.

Correct: Changes in Y move you along the line (that is the mpc × Y term). The line shifts only when autonomous spending changes.

(ii) The marginal propensity to consume
(iii) Autonomous spending

2 points Firms produce output of 50 while planned aggregate expenditure at that output is 46. Then

(i) Inventories fall unexpectedly by 4 and firms raise production
(ii) Inventories rise unexpectedly by 4 and firms cut production
(iii) The economy is in short-run equilibrium
(iv) Planned investment rises by 4

Show answer

(ii). Output exceeds planned spending by 4, so 4 of goods go unsold into inventories. That inventory investment was not planned, so firms cut production toward equilibrium. (Planned investment does not change; actual investment rises by the unplanned 4.)

Short answer

4 points The corner restaurant. (a) (2 points) Using Edwards' restaurant analogy, explain why output is determined by demand in the short run. (b) (2 points) Why does that stop being true in the long run?

Show answer

(a) In the short run the restaurant's menu prices are fixed. It serves however many customers come in, so on slow nights it is partly idle and on busy nights it is overly busy. Its output is set by demand. The whole economy behaves this way because prices and wages are slow to adjust (contracts, limited information, dislike of "unfair" price changes and of wage cuts).

(b) Over time the owners adjust prices: if demand stays high they raise prices, and if it stays low they cut them, until they have a normal workload. For the whole economy, prices adjust so that output returns to potential, Y*, which depends on capital, labor, and technology.

Full credit: (a) fixed prices, output follows demand; (b) prices adjust, output returns to potential.

6 points October 1929. (a) (2 points) Give two reasons from Edwards' slides why a stock market crash can reduce consumer spending at a given level of income. (b) (2 points) Show the effect in the Keynesian cross: which line shifts, which way, and what happens to equilibrium output? (c) (2 points) Why did his slides say the drop in GDP was larger than the drop in consumer spending that started it?

Show answer

(a) Any two: lost wealth makes consumers feel poorer; falling stock prices make consumers pessimistic (lower confidence); stock price volatility creates uncertainty and "wait and see" behavior.

(b) Autonomous consumption C̄ falls, so the PAE line shifts down in parallel (same slope, the mpc) by the amount of the drop. The 45° line does not move. The crossing moves down and to the left along the 45° line, so short-run equilibrium output falls below its old level (and below Y*, if it started at potential).

(c) The multiplier. Lower spending lowers income; with lower income, people cut consumption further (by the mpc), which lowers income again. "As Y declines, C declines further." So output falls by the initial drop times 1/(1 − mpc).

Full credit: (a) two of wealth, confidence, uncertainty; (b) PAE shifts down in parallel, Y falls; (c) multiplier with the mpc mentioned.

4 points Gaps and jobs. Potential output in a made-up economy is $25 trillion. Short-run equilibrium output is $24 trillion. (a) (2 points) What kind of output gap is this, and how big is it in dollars and as a percent of potential? (b) (2 points) What would you expect to see in the labor market, and why?

4 points Your friend says: "If investment falls by $1 trillion and the mpc is 0.5, GDP falls by $1 trillion, because GDP is just C + I + G + NX." (a) (2 points) What is wrong? (b) (2 points) Walk through the first three rounds and give the total.

Show answer

(a) The identity ignores that C depends on income. When investment spending falls, the people who would have earned that money have less income, so they consume less, and that lowers others' incomes too.

(b) Round 1: income falls by 1. Round 2: consumption falls by 0.5 × 1 = 0.5. Round 3: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. And so on: 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + ... = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2. GDP falls by $2 trillion.

Full credit: (a) consumption falls as income falls; (b) 1, 0.5, 0.25 and a total of $2 trillion from the multiplier 2.

One-minute review

  • Short run: prices are sticky, so output follows planned spending. Long run: output returns to potential Y*.
  • Recessionary gap: Y < Y*, unemployment above normal. Expansionary gap: Y > Y*, inflation tends to rise.
  • r = i − π. A higher real rate lowers investment and consumption.
  • C = C̄ + mpc × (Y − T) and PAE = C + I + G + NX = (C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX) + mpc × Y.
  • Intercept = autonomous spending. Slope = mpc. The 45° line is Y = PAE (equal scales, through the origin).
  • Equilibrium: Y = A ÷ (1 − mpc). Fall 2025: 20 ÷ (2/3) = 30.
  • Y > PAE means unplanned inventory buildup and firms cut output; Y < PAE means inventories run down and firms raise output.
  • Shocks to C̄, I, G, NX shift PAE in parallel. ΔY = ΔPAE ÷ (1 − mpc), larger than ΔPAE.
  • To offset a shock, change G by the change in PAE (opposite sign), not by the change in GDP.
