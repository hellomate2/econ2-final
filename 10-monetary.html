Class 21 · Monetary Policy

Monetary policy and financial markets

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) changes nominal interest rates, and because inflation moves slowly, that changes the real interest rate too. A higher real rate lowers investment and consumption, so the PAE line shifts down; a lower rate shifts it up. Financial crises shift PAE as well, through credit spreads, tight lending, and lost confidence. On the Fall 2025 final, this class showed up in the review deck's monetary version of the Keynesian cross ("The central bank ... could reduce real interest rates") and in Q2(c), which asked what happens to a bond's price when interest rates rise: it falls, the same present-value logic that sank Silicon Valley Bank.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Thursday, November 12, 2026.

The ideas, explained

The Fed and what it is trying to do

His learning objectives (slide 27) set the frame. The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the U.S., and it conducts monetary policy. It "is charged with maintaining low and stable inflation and full employment." Other currency areas have their own central banks that do similar things. Most central banks operate independently of government and politics, "but sometimes the lines are blurry."

"The Fed's key policy tool is the real interest rate." Raising it reduces the interest-sensitive parts of investment (I) and consumption (C), like residential housing and new car purchases. Cutting it can temporarily raise spending.

Two more things can shift PAE (slide 29), on top of the shocks from earlier units. Monetary policy is "Actions taken by the central bank to affect nominal and real interest rates." Disruptions in financial markets means, specifically, a financial crisis. This unit covers both. (PAE is planned aggregate expenditure, PAE = C + I + G + NX: consumption, planned investment, government purchases, and net exports.)

Many interest rates, but "the" interest rate

In practice there are many nominal interest rates. His slide 30 says they depend especially on two things:

  • Risk. A riskier borrower pays more. His FRED chart (slide 31) shows 10-year Treasury bonds below Moody's Aaa corporate bonds, which are below Baa corporate bonds.
  • Maturity, the time until the loan is repaid. His slides 32 and 33 compare the 3-month Treasury bill with 5-year and 20-year Treasury yields.

"However, different nominal interest rates generally move together." So "most of the time we talk about 'the' nominal interest rate (and similarly, 'the' real interest rate)." The gap between the rate on risky loans and the rate on safe assets is the credit spread. It matters later, because a financial crisis widens it.

Money and open-market operations

His slide 35: "The Fed is the only institution that is allowed to put money (dollars) into circulation." It does this by printing money, "literally or electronically," and trading it for financial assets, most often short-term U.S. government debt (Treasury bills). These trades "are called 'open-market operations.'"

One implication he flags: when the Fed buys bonds, "it isn't adding to government spending or making transfer payments to banks or individuals (that is, it isn't engaging in fiscal policy)." The Fed swaps one asset (new money) for another (a bond). G and T do not change. Monetary policy works through interest rates.

Banks, reserves, and the federal funds rate

Reserves are "money held by banks" (slide 36), in their vaults or in their accounts at the Fed. His slide 36 lists two "conventional" tools that move the general level of nominal interest rates:

  • Changes in the interest rate the Fed pays on banks' reserves.
  • Changes in the supply of reserves, done through open-market operations.

The rate the Fed targets is the federal funds rate, the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves (definition from the textbook; his slides use the term on slide 73 and in the forward guidance quotes). How the two tools move it, in plain terms: a bank will not lend its reserves to another bank for less than the Fed pays it just to hold them, so raising the interest rate on reserves pulls the federal funds rate up. And when the Fed buys bonds with new reserves, banks have more reserves to lend, which pushes the rate down; selling bonds drains reserves and pushes it up. His chart on slide 73 shows the effective federal funds rate a little above 5 percent through mid-2007, then cut in steps to about 2 percent by mid-2008 and to near zero by the end of 2008.

Money demand and money supply (textbook). His Fall 2025 Class 21 slides do not draw a money market. The textbook does: people hold less money when the nominal interest rate is higher, because money pays little or no interest, so the money demand curve slopes down against i; the Fed controls the supply. When the Fed buys bonds, the money supply rises and the nominal interest rate falls. If you see this graph, it tells the same story as the slides: open-market purchases lower i, and sales raise it.

Bond prices move opposite to interest rates

A bond pays fixed amounts in the future. Its price today is the present value of those payments (from the capital unit): PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2 + ..., where Ft is the payment in year t and i is the interest rate. When i rises, every denominator gets bigger, so the price falls. His slide 56 asks what happens to the value of long-term Treasury bonds when interest rates rise: "They fall (this is the concept of 'present value' at work)."

When interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall. Long-maturity bonds fall the most, because their payments are discounted over more years.

From the nominal rate to the real rate

The real interest rate is the nominal rate minus inflation: r = i − π, or with expected inflation, r = i − πe. Here i is the nominal rate, π (pi) is the inflation rate, and r is the real rate. The Fed sets a nominal rate, but spending decisions respond to the real rate, because the real rate is the true cost of borrowing in terms of goods.

His slides 37 and 74 explain why the Fed controls r in the short run: there is "inflation inertia (inflation only changes slowly)." If π stays put while the Fed raises i by 1 point, r rises by 1 point too. "So: When the Fed changes i, it changes r." From Class 20, slide 5: "The real interest rate can dip negative," which happens when inflation is above the nominal rate.

The long run is different (slide 39). In the long run, r "must be at the level that equilibrates S* and I*," saving and investment at full employment. That level is r*. "The Fed cannot keep r away from r* indefinitely." He saves the reason (inflation) for the next class.

Short run: the Fed moves i, and r moves the same way because inflation is slow to change. Long run: r returns to r*, the rate that balances saving and investment.

How the real interest rate moves PAE

His slide 41 goes through each piece of PAE = C + I + G + NX:

  • Planned investment is lower when r is higher, because borrowing to build a house, a factory, or a data center costs more.
  • "Saving is higher when r is higher, so C is lower when r is higher." Buying a car on credit costs more, and saving pays more.
  • Net exports are lower when r is higher. He covers this two classes later (a higher r attracts capital from abroad and appreciates the dollar).
  • G is taken as given.

"Conclusion: An increase in r reduces PAE at a given Y." It lowers the intercept of the PAE line, so PAE shifts down in parallel and output falls by the multiplier, 1 ÷ (1 − mpc), where mpc is the marginal propensity to consume. A cut in r does the reverse (slide 75): "a derease in r raises PAE at a given Y" (his typo).

Definitions from slide 44: contractionary monetary policy is Fed action to increase nominal and real interest rates; expansionary monetary policy is Fed action to decrease them. His slide 45 gives three reasons the Fed might do either:

  • To offset some other force shifting PAE. This is countercyclical monetary policy. His example: the Great Recession.
  • To pursue some other objective, mainly inflation (next class).
  • A mistake. His example: the Great Depression.

Closing a gap with the interest rate

The slides draw this as shifts without numbers. The textbook writes autonomous spending as depending on r, which lets you compute how big a rate change is needed. A made-up example: PAE = (18 − 100r) + 0.5Y, with r written as a decimal. Each 1 percentage point of r (0.01) moves autonomous spending by 100 × 0.01 = 1.

  • At r = 3 percent: autonomous spending = 18 − 3 = 15, so Y = 15 ÷ 0.5 = 30.
  • Suppose Y* = 34. The gap is 4. The multiplier is 2, so PAE must rise by 2: autonomous spending must be 17.
  • 18 − 100r = 17 gives r = 0.01. The Fed cuts the real rate from 3 percent to 1 percent. With inflation steady, it cuts the nominal rate by the same 2 points.

A mistake: monetary policy in the Great Depression

His slides 46 to 51 tell the story with FRED data and Friedman and Schwartz, A Monetary History of the United States. Industrial production fell steeply after 1929. The money stock and bank reserves both fell sharply in the early 1930s, and the real interest rate rose into double digits. In the cross:

  • PAE2 "shows the effects of the fall in autonomous consumption."
  • PAE3 "shows the effect of monetary contraction and the rise in r." Instead of offsetting the drop, policy made it worse.

Financial intermediation and how a bank fails

Financial intermediation is "The process of getting saving into productive investment." Financial intermediaries are the markets and institutions that do it: banks, investment banks, money market mutual funds, pension funds, and so on (slide 52).

His stylized bank balance sheet (slide 54):

Uses of funds (assets)Sources of funds (liabilities and equity)
LoansShort-term borrowing (deposits are the main example)
ReservesEquity ("capital")
Bonds

Equity is the cushion: assets minus what the bank owes. How an institution fails (slide 55):

  • Defaults and changes in asset values reduce the value of its loans and bonds.
  • If assets fall below what it owes depositors and lenders, equity is gone and the institution is insolvent.
  • Even the belief that it might become insolvent can make depositors withdraw and lenders stop lending, "which can cause the insolvency to occur." That is a run.

Silicon Valley Bank (2023), slide 56. Many of its assets were long-term U.S. Treasury bonds. When interest rates rose, those bonds lost value (present value at work), which ate into its equity and set off withdrawals. His slide 58 shows First Republic Bank's shares diving right after SVB collapsed, with the Reuters headline from 3/13/2023, "First Republic shares dive on contagion fear."

Contagion and financial crises

A financial crisis (slide 53) is a time when a number of financial institutions are in danger of failing and people lose confidence in many of them, so there is "widespread disruption of financial intermediation." Trouble spreads from one institution to others through four channels (slide 60):

  • Confidence: trouble at one creates doubts about others "even if there are no connections between them."
  • Linkage: direct harm through loans, insurance contracts, and other links among institutions.
  • Fire sale: one institution dumps assets, which drives down the prices of assets other institutions hold.
  • Macroeconomic: trouble lowers PAE and so Y, which hurts other institutions' borrowers.

How a crisis lowers PAE (slide 61): it raises credit spreads (risky borrowers pay much more than safe ones), it may tighten lending standards or reduce the availability of loans, and it may hurt consumer and firm confidence. "All of these developments are likely to reduce PAE at a given level of Y." His charts show each one in 2008: the spread between risky and safe rates jumped (slide 62), banks tightened loan standards (slide 63), and consumer sentiment fell sharply (slide 65). In the Depression, the number of U.S. banks fell from about 25 thousand in 1929 to about 15 thousand in 1933 (slide 64), and deposits in failed banks spiked (slide 57).

Policies to prevent crises (slide 68): higher "capital" requirements for financial institutions (more equity cushion), deposit insurance (so depositors have no reason to run), regulation of risk-taking and of linkages among institutions, and using monetary and fiscal policy to keep the economy stable.

Countercyclical policy: 2007 to 2008

His slide 70: when house prices collapsed, it led directly to a huge fall in housing investment, lowered wealth, lowered consumer and firm confidence, and led to more defaults, troubles at financial institutions, and eventually a full-fledged financial crisis. "The result was a big downward shift of the PAE line." The Fed responded by cutting the federal funds rate toward zero (slide 73). Because inflation moves slowly, that lowered r, which raised PAE at a given Y (slide 75). His slide 77 draws PAE3 between PAE1 and PAE2: "An example of 'countercyclical' monetary policy."

His slides 78 to 81 compare the first months of the Great Depression (from July 1929) with the Great Recession (from July 2008). The BAA-AAA interest rate spread rose much more in 2008 to 2009 than in 1929 to 1930. Real national wealth fell further in 2007 to 2009 than in 1928 to 1930. Industrial production fell by similar amounts in the first eleven months. Yet real GDP fell far more in the Depression. Set next to the policy slides, the comparison says the 2008 shocks were at least as large at the start, and the responses differed: monetary contraction in the 1930s, aggressive rate cuts in 2008.

The zero lower bound and unconventional tools

By the end of 2008 the federal funds rate was near zero, and payroll employment was falling fast (slide 83). The problem (slide 82): "nominal interest rates cannot go (much) below zero." The reason, in his words, is that currency is an asset that "offers a zero nominal rate of return for sure." Nobody lends at −2 percent when they can hold cash at 0 percent. So the Fed turned to two tools that work on a subset of interest rates:

  • Forward guidance: "Statements or actions that influence expectations about future nominal interest rates." Long-term rates depend on expected future short rates, so promising to keep short rates low lowers long rates today.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): "Buying bonds other than short-term government debt with reserves," such as long-term Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities. Buying them raises their prices, which lowers their interest rates.

His slide 84 shows the Fed's holdings jumping from under $1 trillion to over $2 trillion in late 2008. His slide 85 quotes the Fed's forward guidance: on December 16, 2008, that weak conditions were "likely to warrant exceptionally low levels of the federal funds rate for some time," and on March 18, 2009, "for an extended period."

Worked examples

Example: nominal versus real

Inflation is 3 percent and expected to stay there. The Fed raises the nominal federal funds rate from 2 percent to 4 percent.

  1. Before: r = i − π = 2 − 3 = −1 percent. The real rate is negative.
  2. After: r = 4 − 3 = 1 percent.
  3. Because inflation did not move, the real rate rose by the same 2 points as the nominal rate.
  4. Effect: higher r lowers I and C (and later NX), so PAE shifts down and output falls by the multiplier times the drop in PAE.

Answer: The real rate rises from −1 percent to 1 percent. This is contractionary monetary policy.

Example: how big a rate cut closes the gap

C = 3 + 0.6(Y − T) − 50r, I = 6 − 150r, G = 5, T = 5, NX = 1, with r as a decimal. Potential output Y* = 25. Inflation is 2 percent. The real rate is now 2 percent.

  1. Combine: PAE = 3 + 0.6Y − 0.6 × 5 − 50r + 6 − 150r + 5 + 1 = (12 − 200r) + 0.6Y.
  2. At r = 0.02: autonomous spending = 12 − 4 = 8. Output Y = 8 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 20.
  3. To reach Y* = 25, autonomous spending must be 25 × 0.4 = 10.
  4. Solve 12 − 200r = 10: r = 0.01, or 1 percent.
  5. With inflation at 2 percent, the nominal rate goes from i = 2 + 2 = 4 percent to i = 1 + 2 = 3 percent.

Answer: Cut the real rate from 2 percent to 1 percent by cutting the nominal rate from 4 percent to 3 percent. Output rises from 20 to 25.

Example: why SVB's bonds lost value

A bank holds a bond that pays $1,000 in 10 years and nothing before. Interest rates rise from 2 percent to 5 percent.

  1. At 2 percent: PV = 1,000 ÷ 1.0210 = 1,000 ÷ 1.21899 = $820.35.
  2. At 5 percent: PV = 1,000 ÷ 1.0510 = 1,000 ÷ 1.62889 = $613.91.
  3. Loss: 820.35 − 613.91 = $206.44, about 25 percent of the bond's value.
  4. Compare a 1-year bond: 1,000 ÷ 1.02 = $980.39 falls to 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = $952.38, a loss of $28.01, under 3 percent.

Answer: The 10-year bond loses about a quarter of its value; the 1-year bond loses under 3 percent. A bank full of long-term bonds takes a big hit to its assets when rates rise.

Example: from a hit to bond values to insolvency

A bank has loans of 80, reserves of 10, and bonds of 30 (total assets 120). It owes 110 in short-term borrowing (deposits). Equity is 120 − 110 = 10.

  1. Interest rates rise and its bonds lose 25 percent: bonds fall to 22.5. Assets are 80 + 10 + 22.5 = 112.5. Equity falls to 112.5 − 110 = 2.5. Still solvent, barely.
  2. Depositors hear about the losses and start to withdraw. To pay them the bank must sell assets, possibly at fire-sale prices.
  3. Then 10 percent of its loans default: loans fall to 72. Assets are 72 + 10 + 22.5 = 104.5, less than the 110 it owes. Equity is −5.5.

Answer: The bank is insolvent: its assets are worth less than its obligations. A higher capital requirement (more equity to begin with) or deposit insurance (so depositors do not run) would have made this less likely.

Common mistakes

Mistake: Saying higher interest rates raise bond prices because bonds now "pay more."

Correct: An existing bond's payments are fixed. A higher i discounts them more, so its price falls. This was Fall 2025 Q2(c).

Mistake: Calling the Fed's bond purchases government spending.

Correct: Open-market operations swap money for bonds. They do not change G or T, so they are not fiscal policy (his slide 35). They work by changing interest rates.

Mistake: Saying the Fed sets the real interest rate directly and can hold it anywhere forever.

Correct: The Fed sets a nominal rate. Because inflation moves slowly, r moves with i in the short run. In the long run r returns to r*, the rate that balances saving and investment.

Mistake: Drawing a rate hike as a change in the slope of PAE.

Correct: r changes autonomous investment and consumption, which are in the intercept. PAE shifts down in parallel; the slope is still the mpc.

Mistake: Thinking a rate hike is expansionary because savers earn more.

Correct: Higher r means more saving and less spending: C, I, and NX fall, so PAE and output fall. A hike is contractionary.

Mistake: Defining quantitative easing as "printing money to cut the federal funds rate."

Correct: QE is buying bonds other than short-term government debt (long-term Treasuries, mortgage-backed securities) with reserves. It is used when the short-term rate is already near zero.

Mistake: Saying a bank fails only when it is already insolvent.

Correct: The belief that a bank might become insolvent can trigger withdrawals and a halt to lending, which can cause the insolvency (slide 55). Confidence alone can spread a crisis to unconnected banks.

How it shows up on the final

The Fall 2025 final had no long question built only on this class, but its pieces showed up in several places, and his review deck draws the monetary version of the cross.

  • Q2(c), 2 points: "Suppose the interest rate i increases. What happens to the price of this bond?" Key: the price "must fall" because the right-hand side of the present value formula decreases when i increases. Same logic as SVB on slide 56.
  • Final review deck, slide 47: the Fed "could reduce real interest rates," which "would increase" I and parts of C "like new car purchases," and also raise NX through a currency depreciation. "All of these effects of expansionary monetary policy would raise PAE." Expect a Keynesian cross part that asks how the Fed could offset a shock, or what a rate change does to PAE and Y.
  • Review deck, Lessons 4 and 5: monetary and fiscal policy affect planned spending; the Fed's reaction function raises r when output is above potential and inflation rises (the next unit).
  • Review deck, Lesson 6: central banks raising rates can attract capital inflows and appreciate the currency, which lowers net exports (the international macro unit).

Full-credit pattern for "what does the Fed do and why": name the direction of the interest rate change, say that r moves with i because inflation is slow to adjust, name which parts of PAE respond (I, C, and NX), say which way PAE shifts at a given Y, and say output changes by a multiplied amount. On a graph: parallel shift of PAE, new equilibrium on the 45-degree line, both equilibria labeled.

A short-answer or multiple-choice question could ask for any of these definitions: open-market operations, reserves, contractionary versus expansionary monetary policy, financial intermediation, insolvency, the four contagion channels, forward guidance, or quantitative easing. 4 points Explain how a financial crisis lowers PAE at a given level of Y. Then draw it.

His slide 61: a crisis raises credit spreads, so risky borrowers (most firms and households) pay higher rates even if the Fed's rate does not change; it tightens lending standards or reduces the availability of loans; and it hurts consumer and firm confidence. All three lower I and C at every income level. On the cross, PAE1 shifts down in parallel to PAE2, and equilibrium output falls from Y* to Y2, by more than the vertical shift because of the multiplier.

6 points Overheating. With r as a decimal, PAE = (20 − 100r) + 0.5Y. The real rate is 4 percent. Potential output is Y* = 30.

(a) (2 points) Find equilibrium output. Is there a gap?

(b) (2 points) What real rate brings output to Y*?

(c) (2 points) Is this expansionary or contractionary monetary policy? What happens to I and C?

Show answer

(a) Autonomous spending = 20 − 100 × 0.04 = 16. Y = 16 ÷ 0.5 = 32. Output is 2 above Y*: an expansionary gap.

(b) Need autonomous spending = 30 × 0.5 = 15. 20 − 100r = 15 gives r = 0.05, or 5 percent. The Fed raises r by 1 point.

(c) Contractionary: the Fed raises nominal and real rates. Higher r lowers planned investment and raises saving, so consumption falls; PAE shifts down by 1 and output falls by 2.

Full credit: Y = 32, expansionary gap of 2; r = 5 percent; contractionary, with I and C falling.

4 points A bank has loans of 80, reserves of 10, and bonds of 30. It owes 110 in deposits and other short-term borrowing.

(a) (2 points) What is its equity? Rates rise and the bonds lose 25 percent of their value. What is its equity now?

(b) (2 points) Then 10 percent of its loans default. Is it solvent?

Show answer

(a) Equity = 120 − 110 = 10. Bonds fall to 22.5, assets to 112.5, equity to 2.5.

(b) Loans fall to 72, assets to 72 + 10 + 22.5 = 104.5, below the 110 it owes. Equity is −5.5, so it is insolvent.

4 points Compare fiscal and monetary policy.

(a) (2 points) Who carries out each, and what tool does each use?

(b) (2 points) Which parts of PAE does each move first?

Show answer

(a) Fiscal policy: Congress and the President, using government purchases, taxes, and transfers. Monetary policy: the Fed, using interest rates (interest on reserves and open-market operations, and at zero, forward guidance and QE).

(b) Fiscal policy moves G directly, or C through disposable income (by mpc per dollar of tax change). Monetary policy moves the interest-sensitive parts of I and C (housing, cars), and NX through the exchange rate.

Full credit: correct actors and tools; G and C for fiscal, I, C, and NX through r for monetary.

One-minute review

  • The Fed aims for low, stable inflation and full employment. Its key tool is the real interest rate.
  • Interest rates differ by risk and maturity but move together, so we talk about "the" interest rate. Credit spread = risky rate minus safe rate.
  • Open-market operations: the Fed creates money and buys financial assets (mostly short-term government debt). This is not fiscal policy.
  • Conventional tools: the interest rate paid on reserves and the supply of reserves. The federal funds rate is the overnight rate between banks.
  • Bond prices move opposite to interest rates; long-term bonds move the most (SVB, Fall 2025 Q2(c)).
  • r = i − π. Inflation is slow to change, so moving i moves r in the short run. In the long run, r returns to r*.
  • Higher r lowers I, C, and NX, so PAE shifts down in parallel and output falls by the multiplier. Lower r does the reverse.
  • Great Depression: monetary contraction made the fall worse (a mistake). 2007 to 2008: rate cuts offset part of the fall (countercyclical).
  • Financial crisis: institutions near failure and lost confidence disrupt intermediation; contagion spreads by confidence, linkage, fire sales, and macro feedback; spreads, lending standards, and confidence lower PAE.
  • At the zero lower bound: forward guidance (expectations of future rates) and quantitative easing (buying longer-term bonds with reserves).
