Class 20 · Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is what the government buys (G) and what it collects in net taxes (T, which is taxes minus transfers). In the Keynesian cross, a change in G moves planned spending one for one, while a tax change moves it only through consumption, scaled down by the marginal propensity to consume. On the Fall 2025 final, the last part of the 24-point Keynesian cross question, Q3(j), asked how big a change in G would cancel a $2 trillion drop in planned spending. The answer was to raise G by $2 trillion: match the drop in planned spending, not the drop in GDP.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Tuesday, November 10, 2026.

The ideas, explained

What fiscal policy is

His slide defines it this way: "Fiscal policies are tax rates, cash and other transfers, and direct government purchases of goods and services." So there are two levers. The first is government purchases (G): the government buys goods and services directly, such as roads, military equipment, or teachers' time. The second is net taxes (T): taxes collected minus transfers paid back to households. A transfer is a payment the government makes without buying anything in return, such as Social Security retirement benefits or unemployment insurance.

This unit builds on the Keynesian cross from the previous unit. Planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) is total planned spending on the economy's output: PAE = C + I + G + NX, where C is consumption, I is planned investment (his slides write Ip), G is government purchases, and NX is net exports. Short-run equilibrium output is where planned spending equals output, PAE = Y, which is where the PAE line crosses the 45-degree line. The marginal propensity to consume (mpc) is the share of each extra dollar of income that households spend; his slides call it c.

His slides list what fiscal policy is good for: "Stimulating an economy in recession; cooling off an overheating economy," and creating long-run investments (or the conditions for them). They also list the limits: governments "can't spend more than they tax forever," publicly held debt is high, and "Exactly how and when to tax and spend is a subject of continuing debate and learning."

Taxes reach spending through disposable income

Households can only spend what is left after taxes, and they also spend transfers they receive. Edwards' slide 20 sets this up:

  • Aggregate income is the same as aggregate output, Y.
  • Net taxes T equal taxes minus transfers back to households.
  • Disposable income is Y − T. Households split it between consumption and saving.

The consumption function in his notation is C = C̄ + mpc × (Y − T). Here C̄ (read "C bar") is autonomous consumption, the part of consumption that does not depend on income. The mpc is "between 0 and 1." His slide adds why the economy-wide mpc is an average: "The poor have high MPC equal to 1, while the rich have much lower MPC (closer to 0)." Part of the reason is the size of consumption needs relative to income, and part is wealth.

Because taxes enter as mpc × (Y − T), a $1 rise in T lowers consumption by mpc dollars at every level of income. Households absorb the rest of the hit by saving less.

On the Fall 2025 final, the consumption function was C = 10 + (1/3)Y with no T anywhere. Taxes were already folded into the constant. When a question gives you C = constant + mpc × Y, just use it. You need the T version only when a question changes taxes or transfers.

PAE with the government in it

Substitute the consumption function into PAE (his slide 23):

PAE = C̄ + mpc × (Y − T) + I + G + NX = (C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX) + mpc × Y

The part in parentheses does not depend on Y. His slide labels it autonomous spending, and it is the vertical intercept of the PAE line. The slope of the PAE line is the mpc. Setting PAE = Y and solving (his slide 33):

Y = (C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX) ÷ (1 − mpc). Equilibrium output equals autonomous spending times the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc). G enters autonomous spending with a coefficient of 1. T enters with a coefficient of −mpc.

Why there is a multiplier, in his two pictures

His slide 34 gives the round-by-round story. Suppose autonomous spending rises by $1. In the first round, output rises by $1, because someone sold $1 more. That $1 is someone's income, so in the second round they spend mpc of it, and output rises by another mpc dollars. In the third round, those sellers spend mpc of that, adding mpc² dollars. Adding every round, 1 + mpc + mpc² + ... = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc). His slide 6 says the same thing in words: "Shocks to income multiply across consumers because we're interconnected."

His slides 9 to 11 make the point with numbers. If PAE were a flat line, so that spending did not depend on income, GDP would be $30 trillion in normal times, "And maybe $29 if a trillion dollar shock pushes planned spending down." Once PAE slopes upward because C depends on Y, the same $1 trillion shock lowers output to $27.5 trillion: "an even bigger multiplied effect on output." A $2.5 trillion fall from a $1 trillion shock is a multiplier of 2.5, which goes with an mpc of 0.6, since 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 2.5.

The spending multiplier and the tax multiplier

Slide 63 is the heart of this class: "$100 billion increase in G will increase PAE by $100 billion; $100 billion of tax cuts will increase PAE by MPC·$100 billion." The reason is in the intercept, C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX. G sits there with a coefficient of 1. T sits there multiplied by −mpc, because a tax cut first lands in households' pockets, and they save part of it.

Change in PAE from fiscal policy: ΔPAE = ΔG − mpc × ΔT. Change in equilibrium output: ΔY = ΔPAE ÷ (1 − mpc).

Spending multiplier: ΔY ÷ ΔG = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc). Tax multiplier: ΔY ÷ ΔT = −mpc ÷ (1 − mpc). The tax multiplier is negative (a tax cut raises output) and smaller in size than the spending multiplier.

Use Δ (delta) to mean "change in." The table shows both multipliers for the mpc values most likely to appear.

A pattern worth knowing: the tax multiplier is always exactly 1 smaller in size than the spending multiplier, because 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) − mpc ÷ (1 − mpc) = (1 − mpc) ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1.

Transfers work like negative taxes. Because T is taxes minus transfers, a $10 billion increase in transfers is a $10 billion cut in T. It raises PAE by mpc × $10 billion, the same as a tax cut, because households save part of it.

Balanced-budget changes (this is from the textbook, not his slides). If the government raises G and T by the same amount, the budget balance does not change, but PAE still rises: ΔPAE = ΔG − mpc × ΔG = (1 − mpc) × ΔG. Multiply by 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) and output rises by exactly ΔG. The balanced-budget multiplier is 1.

Closing an output gap: how big a change in G or T

Potential output Y* is the output the economy produces with normal use of its labor, capital, and technology. His slide 13: in the short run, "Output responds to match PAE," so output can sit below or above Y*. In the long run, output returns to Y*.

  • A recessionary gap means Y is below Y*. Expansionary fiscal policy (raise G or cut T) closes it.
  • An expansionary gap means Y is above Y* (an overheating economy). Contractionary fiscal policy (cut G or raise T) closes it.

Three steps. (1) Needed change in output: ΔY = Y* − Y. (2) Needed change in PAE: ΔPAE = ΔY × (1 − mpc), because ΔY = ΔPAE ÷ (1 − mpc). (3) Either change G by ΔPAE, or change T by −ΔPAE ÷ mpc.

The offsetting-shock shortcut (Fall 2025 Q3(j)). If the question tells you a shock moved PAE by some amount and asks for the G that fully counteracts it, you do not need the multiplier at all. Change G by the same amount in the opposite direction. The shock lowered the intercept of PAE; the new G raises it back to where it was, so the PAE line, and with it equilibrium output, returns to the original. The key's wording: "As long as the marginal propensity to consume remains the same across these offsetting shocks, then government could match the decrease in PAE with an equal and opposite increase in PAE deriving from G." To offset the same shock with taxes, you would need a tax cut of the PAE drop divided by mpc, which is bigger.

Words he uses: surplus, deficit, debt, and the direction of policy

From his slides 39 and 40:

  • Government budget surplus = tax revenues minus government purchases, T − G. When it is negative, the government runs a deficit.
  • The deficit "is the annual excess in spending above taxes." The debt "is the accumulation of past deficits." The deficit is a flow per year; the debt is the stock that builds up.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy: actions that increase the budget surplus (cut G or raise T). It decreases PAE at a given level of Y.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy: actions that decrease the budget surplus (raise G or cut T). It increases PAE at a given level of Y.
  • Countercyclical fiscal policy: "Working against the business cycle, expansionary when in recession."

His slide 41 shows the federal budget surplus as a percent of GDP from 1997 to 2006, with the Bush tax cuts in the title. The bars are positive (a surplus) from 1998 through 2001 and negative (a deficit) every year from 2002 through 2006. Tax cuts lower T, so T − G falls.

Why debt matters, and what limits it

His slide 36 shows federal debt held by the public as a share of GDP from about 1900, with a CBO projection to 2035 (source: CBO's January 2025 Economic and Budget Outlook). He notes economists prefer this measure, and it "shows historically high levels." Slide 37 adds that CBO estimates the July 2025 reconciliation law (the "Big Beautiful Bill") "will further raise debt-GDP by 9.5 ppts at the end of 2034." The chart marks 118% in 2035 without the law and 127.5% with it.

Why care (slide 38):

  • More government debt "probably raises interest rates." The government competes with private borrowers for saving.
  • Debt that rises too high as a share of GDP "could trigger a debt crisis, where investors lose confidence quickly."
  • Debt "can be serviced rather than paid off," meaning the government keeps paying interest and rolls the debt over. But "our children and grandchildren will have to pay more."

What limits deficits (slide 39):

  • For every government, savers in financial markets have to be willing to buy its debt.
  • State and local governments face balanced budget amendments. In bad times, he notes, this often means cuts to many services and to K-12 schools. That is procyclical: a recession lowers tax revenue, the rule forces spending cuts, and lower G pushes PAE down further.
  • The federal government faces periodic debt ceiling votes and annual budgets. In 2025 the U.S. had the longest government shutdown on record because no FY2026 budget had passed; on 11/12/25 a continuing resolution reopened the government, set to expire after Jan 30, 2026.

His summary title for slide 39: debt does not justify paralysis, "but in good times we should be 'keeping our powder dry,' running surpluses not deficits." The idea is to have borrowing room saved for the next recession.

Fiscal policy in practice: the 2008 recession and the 2009 stimulus

What pushed PAE down. House prices rose sharply into 2006 and then fell (his slides 49 and 50, FRED's Case-Shiller index). The housing bubble had raised both I and C, and household debt rose a lot (slide 52, Mian and Sufi). When the bubble burst, his slide 57 lists the damage:

  • Investment fell, especially in housing. The bust lowered the expected future marginal revenue product of housing (housing is a kind of capital), and the financial crisis hurt "animal spirits" and made it hard for firms to get credit. Single-family housing starts collapsed (slide 53).
  • Consumption fell. The housing bust and the stock market decline destroyed wealth, and the crisis hurt consumer confidence and made it hard for households to borrow. Real consumption "teetered then collapsed" around November 2007 (slide 54).

In the Keynesian cross this is a big downward shift of PAE, from PAE1 to PAE2, with output falling from Y* to Y2 (slides 58 and 59).

Designing the response. His slides 62, 63, and 65 list three design questions for the 2009 stimulus, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA, also called the Recovery Act):

  • How big? You need an estimate of how far PAE shifted down, and "We don't observe this directly or rapidly."
  • What mix? A dollar of G raises PAE by a dollar; a dollar of tax cuts raises PAE by mpc dollars. Different types of stimulus also work at different speeds.
  • How long? You need a forecast of how long PAE will stay depressed: "If PAE will be low for more than a year, fiscal stimulus needs to last for more than a year as well."

His slide 64 (from the Council of Economic Advisers) shows what the Recovery Act had delivered through August 2009, in billions of dollars:

His verdict on slide 66: "The Recovery Act counteracted some, but by no means all of the fall in output."

Timing and magnitude. His slides 45 to 47 combine a fall in autonomous consumption (PAE1 down to PAE2) with a tax cut that shifts PAE back up (PAE3, drawn on top of PAE1). The note on the slide: "The keys to getting this right involve timing and magnitude," and "Neither is super obvious." Too small, and the gap stays open. Too large, or too late (arriving after private spending has already recovered), and it pushes output above Y*.

Automatic stabilizers and other practical limits (textbook)

His Fall 2025 Class 20 slides do not cover these, so treat this as background from the textbook chapter. Automatic stabilizers are parts of the budget that move countercyclically on their own, with no new law. In a recession, incomes fall, so income tax collections fall (T drops), and more people qualify for unemployment insurance and other transfers (T drops again, since transfers count against taxes). Lower T props up disposable income and consumption, so PAE falls by less than it otherwise would. In a boom the reverse happens. The textbook also notes practical limits on deliberate fiscal policy: it takes time for Congress to agree and for money to be spent, and fiscal choices affect long-run supply (for example, public investment in roads or education, or tax rates that change incentives to work and invest), so they are about more than PAE. His learning objectives make the same long-run point: fiscal policy can create "investments in types of capital that produce long-run benefits."

Worked examples

Example: Fall 2025 final Q3, start to finish

U.S. GDP is about $30 trillion. In trillions of dollars, C = 10 + (1/3)Y, I = 6, G = 5, NX = −1. Then 2026 headwinds (tariffs, an AI investment bust, immigration enforcement) give C' = 8 + (1/3)Y, I' = 5, G = 5, NX' = 0.

  1. Combine: PAE = 10 + (1/3)Y + 6 + 5 − 1 = 20 + (1/3)Y. Intercept 20.
  2. Solve PAE = Y: 20 + (1/3)Y = Y, so 20 = (2/3)Y and Y = 30.
  3. After the shock: PAE' = 8 + (1/3)Y + 5 + 5 + 0 = 18 + (1/3)Y. Intercept 18.
  4. Solve: 18 = (2/3)Y, so Y = 27.
  5. Change in PAE at any given Y: 18 − 20 = −2. Autonomous consumption fell 2, investment fell 1, and net exports rose 1, so the net is −2. On the graph, a parallel downward shift of 2.
  6. Change in GDP: 27 − 30 = −3. The new line crosses the 45-degree line 3 to the left (and 3 lower).
  7. Why different: the mpc of 1/3 creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 1.5, and 2 × 1.5 = 3.
  8. Offset with G: the shock lowered the intercept by 2, so raise G by 2. Check: 8 + 5 + 7 + 0 = 20, so PAE is back to 20 + (1/3)Y and Y = 30.
  9. Offset with taxes instead (not asked, but a natural follow-up): a tax cut changes PAE by mpc × the cut, so the cut must be 2 ÷ (1/3) = 6. Check: (1/3) × 6 = 2.

Answer: Y falls from 30 to 27. PAE fell by $2 trillion, GDP by $3 trillion, because of the multiplier 1.5. Raise G by $2 trillion (or cut taxes by $6 trillion) to return to 30.

Example: his slide 63 with numbers

The mpc is 0.8. Compare a $100 billion increase in G with a $100 billion tax cut.

  1. Spending multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5.
  2. G: PAE rises by $100 billion, so Y rises by 100 × 5 = $500 billion.
  3. Tax cut: PAE rises by mpc × 100 = 0.8 × 100 = $80 billion. Households save the other $20 billion.
  4. Y rises by 80 × 5 = $400 billion. Tax multiplier check: −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4, and −4 × (−100) = +400.

Answer: The G increase raises output by $500 billion; the tax cut raises it by $400 billion. The gap is exactly the $100 billion itself, since the multipliers differ by 1.

Example: closing a recessionary gap two ways

PAE = 14 + 0.5Y and potential output Y* = 32 (trillions). How big a change in G closes the gap? How big a tax cut?

  1. Current output: 14 = 0.5Y, so Y = 28. The gap is 32 − 28 = 4 (recessionary).
  2. Multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2. Needed ΔPAE = 4 ÷ 2 = 2.
  3. With G: raise G by 2. New PAE = 16 + 0.5Y, so Y = 16 ÷ 0.5 = 32.
  4. With T: a tax cut raises PAE by 0.5 per dollar, so the cut is 2 ÷ 0.5 = 4. New intercept: 14 + 0.5 × 4 = 16, the same line.

Answer: Raise G by $2 trillion, or cut T by $4 trillion. Either one moves PAE up by 2 and output from 28 to 32.

Example: cooling an overheating economy with a tax increase

PAE = 18 + 0.5Y and Y* = 32.

  1. Current output: Y = 18 ÷ 0.5 = 36, which is 4 above Y* (an expansionary gap).
  2. Needed ΔPAE = −4 × (1 − 0.5) = −2.
  3. Tax increase: −0.5 × ΔT = −2, so ΔT = +4. New intercept 18 − 2 = 16, and Y = 32.
  4. Budget effect: the surplus T − G rises by 4, so this is contractionary fiscal policy by his definition.

Answer: Raise net taxes by $4 trillion (or cut G by $2 trillion). Output falls from 36 to 32.

Example: a transfer increase

The mpc is 0.75. Congress raises unemployment benefits by $40 billion.

  1. Transfers count against taxes, so T falls by $40 billion.
  2. ΔPAE = −mpc × ΔT = −0.75 × (−40) = +$30 billion.
  3. Multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4. ΔY = 30 × 4 = $120 billion.

Answer: Output rises by $120 billion, compared with $160 billion if the same $40 billion had been spent directly as G.

Common mistakes

Mistake: On Fall 2025 Q3(j), answering "raise G by $3 trillion" because GDP fell by $3 trillion.

Correct: G has to undo the $2 trillion drop in PAE. The multiplier then works on the G increase too, so +2 in G brings GDP back up by 3. Match the shift in PAE, not the change in Y.

Mistake: Treating a $100 billion tax cut like a $100 billion increase in G.

Correct: The tax cut raises PAE by only mpc × $100 billion, because households save part of it. His slide 63 says this exactly.

Mistake: Dividing the output gap by the multiplier and calling that the tax cut.

Correct: Gap ÷ multiplier gives the needed change in PAE. For G, that is the answer. For T, divide once more by mpc: tax cut = ΔPAE ÷ mpc.

Mistake: Changing the slope of PAE when G or T changes.

Correct: G and T sit in the intercept, (C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX). The slope is the mpc and stays the same, so the line shifts parallel.

Mistake: Counting an increase in transfers (Social Security, unemployment benefits) as an increase in G.

Correct: Transfers are part of net taxes: T = taxes − transfers. More transfers means lower T, which raises PAE by mpc per dollar.

Mistake: Mixing up the deficit and the debt.

Correct: The deficit is one year's spending above taxes. The debt is the accumulation of past deficits. A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.

Mistake: Calling a tax increase expansionary because it brings in money.

Correct: A tax increase raises the surplus T − G and lowers PAE, so it is contractionary.

Mistake: Saying the Fed's bond purchases are fiscal policy.

Correct: Fiscal policy is taxes, transfers, and government purchases, decided by Congress and the President. When the Fed buys bonds it is not spending or making transfers (Class 21 slide 35). That is monetary policy, the next unit.

Practice questions

2 points The marginal propensity to consume is 0.8. Compared with a $100 billion increase in government purchases, a $100 billion tax cut

(i) raises PAE by the same amount, $100 billion
(ii) raises PAE by $80 billion, so it raises output by less
(iii) raises PAE by $500 billion
(iv) lowers PAE, because the government loses revenue

Show answer

(ii). A tax cut first raises disposable income by $100 billion. Households spend mpc of it, 0.8 × 100 = $80 billion, and save the rest. G enters PAE one for one, so it raises PAE by the full $100 billion. Output then rises by 5 × 80 = $400 billion versus 5 × 100 = $500 billion.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points The government budget surplus is

(i) G − T
(ii) T − G
(iii) the accumulation of past deficits
(iv) exports minus imports

Show answer

(ii). His slide 40 defines it as tax revenues minus government purchases. The accumulation of past deficits is the debt, and exports minus imports is net exports.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points Which of these is expansionary fiscal policy?

(i) Raising income tax rates during a recession
(ii) Cutting government purchases during a boom
(iii) Increasing Social Security benefits during a recession
(iv) The Federal Reserve buying Treasury bills

Show answer

(iii). Higher transfers lower net taxes T, which lowers the surplus T − G and raises PAE at every level of Y. Options (i) and (ii) raise the surplus, so they are contractionary. Option (iv) is monetary policy, not fiscal policy.

Full credit: circle (iii).

2 points A government starts with debt of $100 billion. Over the next three years it runs deficits of $5 billion and $3 billion and then a surplus of $2 billion. Its debt at the end is

(i) $94 billion
(ii) $100 billion
(iii) $106 billion
(iv) $110 billion

Show answer

(iii). The debt is the accumulation of past deficits. Deficits add to it and a surplus pays some down: 100 + 5 + 3 − 2 = $106 billion.

Full credit: circle (iii).

(b) Set 16 + (1/2)Y = Y. Then 16 = (1/2)Y, so Y = 32.

(c) PAE' = 8 + (1/2)Y + 2 + 5 − 2 = 13 + (1/2)Y. Then 13 = (1/2)Y, so Y = 26.

(d) PAE fell by 3 (from intercept 16 to 13): investment fell 2 and net exports fell 1. GDP fell by 6 (from 32 to 26). GDP fell more because the mpc of 1/2 creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 1/2) = 2: lower output means lower income, which lowers consumption, which lowers output again. 3 × 2 = 6.

(e) Raise G by $3 trillion, matching the $3 trillion drop in PAE, so the intercept returns to 16 and Y returns to 32. A tax cut changes PAE by only mpc per dollar, so it would have to be 3 ÷ (1/2) = $6 trillion.

Full credit: 16 + (1/2)Y and Y = 32; 13 + (1/2)Y and Y = 26; PAE down $3 trillion, GDP down $6 trillion, because of the multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 2; increase G by $3 trillion (not 6); tax cut of $6 trillion.

4 points Draw the Keynesian cross for the previous question. Show the original PAE, PAE' after the shock, and PAE'' after Congress's change in G. Label intercepts and equilibria. Explain what you drew.

Show answer

Axes: Y across, PAE up, with the same scale on both, say 0 to 40. Draw the 45-degree line from the origin. PAE starts at 16 on the vertical axis with slope 1/2 and crosses the 45-degree line at (32, 32). PAE' is parallel, starting at 13, and crosses at (26, 26). Draw a downward arrow of 3 between them and mark the horizontal fall of 6 on the Y axis. After G rises by 3, PAE'' starts at 16 again and lies exactly on top of the original PAE, so label that line "PAE = PAE''" with the equilibrium back at 32.

Full credit: 45-degree line; two parallel lines with intercepts 16 and 13; equilibria marked at 32 and 26 on the horizontal axis; PAE'' drawn on the original line; one sentence saying the vertical shift is 3 and the output change is 6 because of the multiplier.

6 points In a small economy, the mpc is 0.75.

(a) (2 points) What are the spending multiplier and the tax multiplier?

(b) (2 points) The government cuts taxes by $200 billion. By how much do PAE and equilibrium output change?

(c) (2 points) If it had instead raised G by $200 billion, by how much would output change? Explain the difference.

Show answer

(a) Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4. Tax multiplier = −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3.

(b) ΔPAE = −0.75 × (−200) = +$150 billion. ΔY = 150 × 4 = +$600 billion (equivalently −3 × −200).

(c) ΔY = 200 × 4 = +$800 billion. The G increase is spending on output right away, so it moves PAE by the full $200 billion. Households save a quarter of the tax cut, so it moves PAE by only $150 billion.

Full credit: 4 and −3; PAE +$150 billion, Y +$600 billion; Y +$800 billion with the reason that part of a tax cut is saved.

6 points Economists estimate that output is $18 trillion while potential output is $20 trillion. The mpc is 0.8.

(a) (2 points) What kind of gap is this, and how big is it?

(b) (2 points) How big an increase in G would close it? Show your work.

(c) (2 points) How big a tax cut would close it instead? Show your work.

Show answer

(a) Output is below potential, so it is a recessionary gap of 20 − 18 = $2 trillion.

(b) Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5. Needed ΔPAE = 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4. So raise G by $0.4 trillion, which is $400 billion.

(c) A tax cut raises PAE by 0.8 per dollar, so the cut must be 0.4 ÷ 0.8 = $0.5 trillion, which is $500 billion. Check with the tax multiplier: −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4, and −4 × (−0.5) = +2.

Full credit: recessionary, $2 trillion; G up $400 billion; tax cut of $500 billion, with the multiplier shown.

4 points The economy is overheating: output is $33 trillion and potential is $30 trillion. The mpc is 2/3.

(a) (2 points) What change in G would bring output back to potential?

(b) (2 points) What change in taxes would do the same? Is this expansionary or contractionary fiscal policy?

Show answer

(a) Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 2/3) = 3. Output must fall by 3, so PAE must fall by 3 ÷ 3 = 1. Cut G by $1 trillion.

(b) A tax increase lowers PAE by 2/3 per dollar, so ΔT = 1 ÷ (2/3) = +$1.5 trillion. Either change raises the budget surplus T − G, so it is contractionary fiscal policy.

Full credit: decrease G by $1 trillion; raise taxes by $1.5 trillion; contractionary.

6 points The 2009 Recovery Act. Through August 2009 it had delivered $151.4 billion: $40.0 billion of individual tax cuts, $38.4 billion of state fiscal relief, $30.6 billion of aid to directly impacted individuals, $17.2 billion of business tax incentives, $16.5 billion of government investment outlays, and $8.8 billion of AMT relief (Class 20, slide 64).

(a) (2 points) Which of these raise PAE dollar for dollar, and which raise it by less? Explain using the PAE equation.

(b) (2 points) Edwards lists three design questions for a stimulus. Name them.

(c) (2 points) His slide says the Act "counteracted some, but by no means all of the fall in output." Show this on a Keynesian cross and give one reason it fell short.

Show answer

(a) Government investment outlays are G, which sits in PAE with a coefficient of 1, so they raise PAE dollar for dollar. Individual tax cuts, AMT relief, and aid to individuals raise disposable income, so they raise PAE by only mpc per dollar, since households save part. (State fiscal relief raises PAE one for one to the extent states use it to keep up their own purchases instead of cutting them.)

(b) How big should it be (you need an estimate of how far PAE fell); what should the mix be (different types of stimulus have different effects on PAE and work at different speeds); how long should it last (as long as PAE stays depressed).

(c) Draw PAE1 crossing the 45-degree line at Y*, PAE2 shifted well down crossing at Y2, and PAE3 between them crossing at Y3, with Y2 < Y3 < Y*. One reason: the size of the PAE drop is not observed "directly or rapidly," and the stimulus was smaller than the drop; much of it was tax cuts and transfers, which raise PAE by only mpc per dollar.

Full credit: investment outlays one for one, tax cuts and transfers by mpc; the three questions size, composition, duration; a graph with Y3 between Y2 and Y* plus one reason.

6 points The debt question. CBO's January 2025 outlook projected federal debt held by the public at 118% of GDP in 2035, and CBO estimates the July 2025 reconciliation law raises that to 127.5% (Class 20, slides 36 and 37).

(a) (2 points) Explain the difference between the deficit and the debt.

(b) (2 points) Give two reasons from his slides why economists care about high debt.

(c) (2 points) He says in good times we should be "keeping our powder dry." What does that mean in terms of fiscal policy over the business cycle?

Show answer

(a) The deficit is the excess of spending over taxes in one year. The debt is the accumulation of past deficits (minus any surpluses).

(b) Any two: more debt probably raises interest rates; debt that gets too high relative to GDP could trigger a debt crisis where investors lose confidence quickly; debt is serviced rather than paid off, so future generations pay more.

(c) Run surpluses (or small deficits) when output is at or above potential, so debt is lower and there is room to borrow for expansionary, countercyclical policy when the next recession comes.

Full credit: flow versus stock; two of the three slide reasons; surpluses in good times to leave room for stimulus in bad times.

4 points During a recession, a state's tax revenue falls. Its constitution requires a balanced budget, so it cuts spending on schools and services.

(a) (2 points) Is this countercyclical or procyclical? Explain using PAE.

(b) (2 points) Why can the federal government act differently?

Show answer

(a) Procyclical. The recession already lowered PAE and output. Cutting state purchases lowers G, which shifts PAE down further and, through the multiplier, lowers output more. It works with the business cycle instead of against it.

(b) The federal government has no balanced budget amendment. It can borrow (run a deficit) as long as savers in financial markets keep buying its debt, within its debt ceiling and budget process. So it can raise G or cut T in a recession.

Full credit: procyclical, because lower G lowers PAE and Y by a multiplied amount; the federal government can run deficits.

4 points A balanced-budget stimulus (textbook). The mpc is 0.5. The government raises G by $1 trillion and pays for it with $1 trillion of new taxes.

(a) (2 points) By how much does PAE change at a given Y?

(b) (2 points) By how much does equilibrium output change?

Show answer

(a) ΔPAE = ΔG − mpc × ΔT = 1 − 0.5 × 1 = +$0.5 trillion.

(b) ΔY = 0.5 × 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 0.5 × 2 = +$1 trillion. The balanced-budget multiplier is 1: output rises by exactly the change in G.

Full credit: PAE +$0.5 trillion; Y +$1 trillion with the multiplier 2 shown.

4 points Timing. Congress passes a tax cut to fight a recession caused by a fall in autonomous consumption. By the time the tax cut reaches households, consumer confidence has already recovered on its own.

(a) (2 points) Describe on a Keynesian cross what happens to output.

(b) (2 points) What does this show about his point that "timing and magnitude" are the keys?

Show answer

(a) Autonomous consumption has already moved PAE back up to PAE1, which crosses the 45-degree line at Y*. The tax cut then shifts PAE above PAE1, so the new equilibrium is to the right of Y*: output rises above potential, an expansionary gap.

(b) Stimulus has to arrive while PAE is still depressed and be about the size of the drop. Too late or too big overshoots and overheats the economy; too small leaves part of the gap. Neither the size nor the duration of the drop is easy to know in real time.

Full credit: PAE ends above PAE1 and output above Y*; the stimulus must match the timing and size of the shock.

One-minute review

  • Fiscal policy is government purchases G and net taxes T, where T = taxes − transfers.
  • Consumption depends on disposable income: C = C̄ + mpc × (Y − T). Autonomous spending is C̄ − mpc × T + I + G + NX, the PAE intercept; the slope is mpc.
  • A dollar of G moves PAE by a dollar. A dollar of tax cut or transfer moves PAE by mpc dollars.
  • Spending multiplier 1 ÷ (1 − mpc); tax multiplier −mpc ÷ (1 − mpc). They differ by exactly 1.
  • To close a gap: ΔPAE = (Y* − Y) × (1 − mpc). Change G by ΔPAE, or change T by −ΔPAE ÷ mpc.
  • To offset a shock to PAE (Fall 2025 Q3(j)): change G by the PAE shock in the opposite direction, not by the GDP change.
  • Surplus = T − G. Expansionary policy lowers the surplus; contractionary raises it; countercyclical means expansionary in recessions.
  • The deficit is one year's gap; the debt is the accumulation of deficits. High debt can raise interest rates, risk a debt crisis, and burden future generations.
  • The 2009 Recovery Act "counteracted some, but by no means all" of the fall in output; size, mix, and duration are the design problems, and timing and magnitude are hard to get right.
