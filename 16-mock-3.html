Practice final C

The hardest of the three practice finals: 60 points in exactly the shape of Edwards' Fall 2025 final, with an AI data center boom that pushes output above potential, a Fed that has to cool it down, and an almond export market hit by a stronger dollar. Take it closed book except for your one-page cheat sheet, with a calculator and a 90-minute timer, and open no answer until time is up.

The exam

Work on paper. Every graphing part shows the axes you would get printed on the exam, so copy them or print this page and draw on them. Each answer below is longer than you need so you can learn from it; the "Full credit" line says what a grader looks for. On the real exam he asks for answers inside a box and says "less is more," so aim for two to four sentences per part.

Question 1: Multiple choice, maximum marginal benefit (12 points)

For each of these questions, circle the one correct answer.

2 points 1(a). A monopolist produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. At that quantity, there is a deadweight loss because

(i) Marginal revenue is greater than marginal cost for the last unit sold
(ii) The marginal benefit to buyers of one more unit (the price) is greater than its marginal cost
(iii) The marginal benefit to buyers of one more unit is less than its marginal cost
(iv) Price equals marginal cost, just as in perfect competition

Show answer

(ii). The monopolist stops where marginal revenue (MR) equals marginal cost (MC), but its price, which is read off the demand curve, sits above MR. The demand curve is buyers' marginal benefit (MB), so at the monopoly quantity MB is greater than MC. Units that buyers value above their cost do not get made, and the lost surplus on those units is the deadweight loss. Edwards' final review slide puts it as "At Q1, MB > MC."

Option (i) describes a firm that should produce more, which is not where a profit maximizer stops. Option (iii) is the overproduction case you see with a negative externality. Option (iv) is perfect competition.

Full credit: (ii).

2 points 1(b). In one hour of work, a worker in Country A can produce 6 laptops or 3 tons of wheat. A worker in Country B can produce 2 laptops or 2 tons of wheat. Which country has the comparative advantage in wheat?

(i) Country A, because it produces more wheat per hour
(ii) Country A, because it has an absolute advantage in both goods
(iii) Country B, because its opportunity cost of a ton of wheat is 1 laptop, compared with 2 laptops in Country A
(iv) Neither country, so Country A should produce both goods

Show answer

(iii). Comparative advantage goes to whoever gives up less to make the good. For Country A, 3 tons of wheat cost 6 laptops, so 1 ton costs 6 ÷ 3 = 2 laptops. For Country B, 2 tons cost 2 laptops, so 1 ton costs 2 ÷ 2 = 1 laptop. Country B gives up less, so it has the comparative advantage in wheat, and Country A has it in laptops (½ ton of wheat per laptop, compared with 1 ton in B).

Options (i) and (ii) describe absolute advantage, which does not decide who should specialize. Both countries gain if B grows wheat and A builds laptops and they trade.

Full credit: (iii).

2 points 1(c). The Fed's target inflation rate is 2%, the normal real interest rate is 3%, and the Fed follows the reaction function r − 3% = 0.5 × (π − 2%), where r is the real interest rate and π is inflation. Inflation is currently 4%. What does the Fed do?

(i) Lowers the real interest rate to 2%, so the nominal interest rate is 6%
(ii) Keeps the real interest rate at 3%, so the nominal interest rate is 7%
(iii) Raises the real interest rate to 4%, so the nominal interest rate is 8%
(iv) Raises the real interest rate to 4%, so the nominal interest rate is 4%

Show answer

(iii). Plug in: r = 3% + 0.5 × (4% − 2%) = 3% + 1% = 4%. The Fed controls the real rate by setting the nominal rate i, and r = i − π, so i = r + π = 4% + 4% = 8%. This is the same calculation as the table on his class 22 slide 17, where 3% inflation gives a real rate of 3.5% and a nominal rate of 6.5%.

Option (iv) forgets to add inflation back when converting the real rate into the nominal rate. Option (ii) gets the arithmetic of i = r + π right but ignores that inflation is above target. Option (i) moves the rate the wrong way.

Full credit: (iii).

2 points 1(d). A large group of new workers arrives in the labor market for farm work. Nothing changes crop prices or workers' marginal product of labor. In this labor market,

(i) Labor supply shifts out, the wage falls, and employment rises
(ii) Labor demand shifts out, and both the wage and employment rise
(iii) Labor supply shifts out, and both the wage and employment fall
(iv) Nothing changes, because the marginal revenue product of labor did not change

Show answer

(i). More people willing to work at each wage is an outward shift of labor supply. Labor demand is the marginal revenue product of labor (MRPL = MR × MPL), and neither the crop price (MR) nor the marginal product of labor (MPL) changed, so demand stays put. Sliding down the same demand curve, employers hire more workers only at a lower wage, because each extra worker adds a little less than the last one.

Option (iv) is the trap: demand did not move, but supply did, and a supply shift alone changes the equilibrium.

Full credit: (i).

2 points 1(e). Which of these is counted in U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) for 2026?

(i) A 2019 car that a dealer resells to a new owner in 2026
(ii) Shares of stock in a U.S. tech company bought in 2026
(iii) Unemployment benefits paid by state governments in 2026
(iv) A new data center built in Texas in 2026 by a Japanese-owned company

Show answer

(iv). GDP is the market value of final goods and services newly produced within a country's borders during the period. A data center built in Texas in 2026 is a new structure produced inside the U.S., so it counts as investment, even though the owner is foreign. The used car was produced in 2019 and counted then. A stock purchase is a transfer of ownership of an existing asset, so nothing new is produced. Unemployment benefits are transfer payments, which are not purchases of goods or services, so they are not part of government purchases G.

Full credit: (iv).

2 points 1(f). Runoff from a fertilizer plant damages fisheries downstream, and the plant does not pay for that damage. Without government intervention, the fertilizer market produces

(i) Too little, because social marginal benefit exceeds social marginal cost at the market quantity
(ii) Too much, because social marginal cost exceeds social marginal benefit at the market quantity
(iii) The efficient amount, because the plant sets price equal to its private marginal cost
(iv) Nothing, because the external cost makes production unprofitable

Show answer

(ii). This is a negative externality: social marginal cost (SMC) is the plant's private marginal cost plus the damage to the fisheries, so SMC lies above the supply curve. The market stops where supply meets demand, which ignores the damage, so it goes past the quantity where SMC equals social marginal benefit (SMB). On the extra units, SMC is greater than SMB, and that is the deadweight loss. His final review slide says "At Q1, SMB < SMC."

Full credit: (ii).

Question 2: Short answers, compounded interest in you (10 points)

Two points each. Show your work wherever there is a number.

2 points 2(a). Two hypothetical countries, Northland and Southland, start with the same real GDP per person. Northland's real GDP per person grows 2.8% per year, and Southland's grows 1.4% per year. Use the rule of 70 to find how long each country takes to double its income. After 100 years, roughly how many times richer per person is Northland than Southland? Show your work.

Show answer

The rule of 70 says the doubling time is about 70 divided by the growth rate in percent.

  1. Northland: 70 ÷ 2.8 = 25 years to double. In 100 years it doubles 100 ÷ 25 = 4 times, so it ends 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 times richer than it started.
  2. Southland: 70 ÷ 1.4 = 50 years to double. In 100 years it doubles 100 ÷ 50 = 2 times, so it ends 2 × 2 = 4 times richer than it started.
  3. Since they started equal, Northland ends up about 16 ÷ 4 = 4 times as rich per person as Southland.

Compounding exactly gives almost the same answer: 1.028100 ≈ 15.8 and 1.014100 ≈ 4.0, a ratio of about 3.9. This is the point of his class 18 slide on small differences in growth rates: a gap of a point or so per year looks tiny in any one year but compounds into a huge gap in living standards.

Full credit: doubling times of 25 and 50 years, growth of about 16 times and 4 times, so Northland is about 4 times richer. Answering "twice as rich" (because 2.8 is twice 1.4) misses the compounding.

2 points 2(b). A bond pays $50 one year from now and $1,050 two years from now. Find its price when the market interest rate is 5%. Then the Fed cuts rates and the market interest rate falls to 4%. What is the bond's new price, and why did it move that way? Show your work.

Show answer

The price of a bond is the present value (PV) of its fixed payments: PV = F1 ÷ (1 + i) + F2 ÷ (1 + i)2.

  1. At i = 5%: $50 ÷ 1.05 + $1,050 ÷ 1.1025 = $47.62 + $952.38 = $1,000.00.
  2. At i = 4%: $50 ÷ 1.04 + $1,050 ÷ 1.0816 = $48.08 + $970.78 = $1,018.86.

The price rises by $18.86. The payments are fixed, so when the interest rate falls, each future dollar is discounted less and is worth more today. Another way to see it: new bonds now pay only 4%, so investors bid up the price of this older bond that pays more. This is the same present value logic as his Silicon Valley Bank example on class 22 slide 28, run in the other direction: rising rates crushed the value of that bank's bonds, and falling rates raise bond values.

Full credit: $1,000.00 at 5% and $1,018.86 at 4%, plus "the payments are fixed, so a lower interest rate raises their present value."

2 points 2(c). Suppose a campus job paid $15 per hour in 2016, when the consumer price index (CPI) was 240. The same job pays $21 per hour in 2026, when the CPI is 320. (These are made-up round numbers.) Did the real wage for this job rise, fall, or stay the same? By what percent? Show your work.

Show answer

Convert the 2016 wage into 2026 dollars with $X2026 = $X2016 × (CPI2026 ÷ CPI2016), the formula on his class 18 slide 9.

  1. $15 × (320 ÷ 240) = $15 × 1.3333 = $20.00 in 2026 dollars.
  2. The job now pays $21.00, which is more than $20.00, so the real wage rose.
  3. Percent change in the real wage: $21 ÷ $20 = 1.05, so it rose 5%.

Check with growth rates: the nominal wage rose 40% ($21 ÷ $15 = 1.40) while prices rose 33.3% (320 ÷ 240 = 1.333), and 1.40 ÷ 1.333 = 1.05. Saying "the wage rose 40%" ignores inflation; the purchasing power gain is only 5%.

Full credit: $15 in 2016 is worth $20 in 2026 dollars, so the real wage rose, by 5%.

2 points 2(d). Two hypothetical neighboring countries have the same natural resources, the same climate, and access to the same technology. In the first, courts enforce contracts and the government rarely seizes property. In the second, officials often demand bribes and sometimes take over successful businesses. Which country will likely have higher average labor productivity in the long run? Explain using the determinants of productivity.

Show answer

The first country. Average labor productivity (output per worker) depends mainly on capital per worker (physical capital and human capital, meaning education and skills) and on technology. People only build factories, buy equipment, get educated, and adopt new technology if they expect to keep the returns. The first country has what Edwards calls social infrastructure or institutions: rule of law, property rights, and protection from government. In the second, an investor expects a bribe or a seizure to take the payoff, so less capital gets built and less technology gets used.

With more capital and technology per worker, the marginal product of labor is higher, so labor demand (MRPL) is higher and so are wages and output per worker. The same technology being available to both does not help the second country much, because nobody invests to put it to work.

Full credit: the first country, because rule of law and secure property rights give people the incentive to invest in physical capital, human capital, and technology, which raise productivity.

2 points 2(e). A U.S.-made laptop sells for $1,000 and a Japanese game console sells for ¥30,000. The exchange rate moves from ¥150 per $1 to ¥160 per $1. Did the dollar appreciate or depreciate? What happens to the yen price of the laptop, the dollar price of the console, and U.S. net exports? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. A dollar now buys more yen (160 instead of 150), so the dollar appreciated (got stronger) and the yen depreciated.
  2. Laptop in Japan: $1,000 × ¥150 = ¥150,000 before, and $1,000 × ¥160 = ¥160,000 after. It got more expensive for Japanese buyers.
  3. Console in the U.S.: ¥30,000 ÷ 150 = $200.00 before, and ¥30,000 ÷ 160 = $187.50 after. It got cheaper for U.S. buyers.

So U.S. exports fall (Japanese buyers face higher prices for U.S. goods) and U.S. imports rise (U.S. buyers face lower prices for Japanese goods), and net exports, NX = exports − imports, fall. His class 23 slide 8 puts the rule simply: "Currency appreciation raises export prices and tends to reduce exports."

Full credit: the dollar appreciated; the laptop goes from ¥150,000 to ¥160,000, the console from $200 to $187.50; exports fall, imports rise, so net exports fall.

Question 3: Too much of a good thing in Pacifica (24 points)

Pacifica is a hypothetical economy whose potential output is Y* = $40 trillion. Its marginal propensity to consume (mpc) is 0.75, and this system describes planned aggregate expenditure (PAE) in trillions of dollars, where T is taxes:

C = 6 + 0.75(Y − T)   T = 4   I = 4   G = 4   NX = −1   PAE = C + I + G + NX

2 points 3(a). Combine the expenditure terms in PAE and find the level of PAE when Y = 0, which is the vertical intercept in the Keynesian cross diagram. Show your work.

Show answer

Substitute T = 4 into consumption first: C = 6 + 0.75(Y − 4) = 6 − 3 + 0.75Y = 3 + 0.75Y. Then

PAE = (3 + 0.75Y) + 4 + 4 + (−1) = 10 + 0.75Y

When Y = 0, PAE = 10, so the vertical intercept (autonomous spending) is $10 trillion. This matches his slide formula PAE = (C̄ − cT + IP + G + NX) + cY, where c is the mpc: 6 − 3 + 4 + 4 − 1 = 10.

Full credit: PAE = 10 + 0.75Y and intercept 10. The most common error is forgetting the −0.75 × 4 = −3 from taxes and getting 13.

2 points 3(b). Set PAE = Y and solve for short-run equilibrium output. Is Pacifica at, above, or below potential? Show your work.

Show answer

Y = 10 + 0.75Y, so 0.25Y = 10 and Y = 40. Check: C = 6 + 0.75 × (40 − 4) = 6 + 27 = 33, and 33 + 4 + 4 − 1 = 40.

Output equals potential output, Y = Y* = $40 trillion, so the output gap is zero.

Full credit: Y = 40, equal to potential.

In 2027, an AI data center construction boom raises planned investment from 4 to I' = 5.5, and Congress passes a large infrastructure bill that raises government purchases from 4 to G' = 4.5. Nothing else changes.

2 points 3(c). Find the new expenditure line PAE' and its vertical intercept. Show your work.

Show answer

PAE' = (3 + 0.75Y) + 5.5 + 4.5 + (−1) = 12 + 0.75Y

The vertical intercept is $12 trillion, up $2 trillion: $1.5 trillion from investment plus $0.5 trillion from government purchases. The slope is still the mpc of 0.75, so PAE' is parallel to PAE.

Full credit: PAE' = 12 + 0.75Y, intercept 12.

2 points 3(d). Set PAE' = Y and solve for the new short-run equilibrium output. Show your work.

Show answer

Y = 12 + 0.75Y, so 0.25Y = 12 and Y = 48. Check: C = 6 + 0.75 × (48 − 4) = 6 + 33 = 39, and 39 + 5.5 + 4.5 − 1 = 48.

Full credit: Y = 48.

4 points 3(e). On the axes below, graph the original PAE line from (a), the new PAE' line from (c), and the 45-degree line. Show both equilibria, label the intercepts, and mark potential output Y* and the output gap.

Show answer
  1. Draw the 45-degree line from the origin through (40, 40) and (48, 48). It is every point where PAE = Y.
  2. Draw PAE from the intercept 10 with slope 0.75. It crosses the 45-degree line at (40, 40). A second point to aim for: at Y = 20, PAE = 10 + 15 = 25.
  3. Draw PAE' from the intercept 12, parallel to PAE, crossing the 45-degree line at (48, 48). At Y = 20, PAE' = 12 + 15 = 27.
  4. Drop dashed lines from both equilibria to the horizontal axis. Label Y* = 40 and 48, and mark the gap of +8 between them.

Full credit: 45-degree line through the origin; two parallel PAE lines with intercepts 10 and 12 and slope 0.75; equilibria at 40 and 48 labeled on both axes; Y* marked at 40. Graders look for labeled intercepts and equilibria more than for artistic precision, but the PAE lines must be flatter than the 45-degree line.

2 points 3(f). By how much did PAE change (at a given level of Y), and by how much did GDP change? Explain fully why the two numbers differ, and compute the multiplier.

Show answer

PAE rose by $2 trillion at every level of income (the vertical shift from intercept 10 to 12). GDP rose by $8 trillion, from 40 to 48 (the horizontal move of the equilibrium). The change in GDP is four times larger.

The reason is the multiplier: multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc) = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4, and $2 trillion × 4 = $8 trillion. The first $2 trillion of new spending becomes income for construction workers and suppliers. They spend 75% of it, which becomes someone else's income, who spends 75% of that, and so on: 2 + 1.5 + 1.125 + ... = 8. You can see the extra rounds in the numbers: consumption rose from 33 to 39, so $6 trillion of the $8 trillion increase is induced consumption. On the graph, the PAE line is flatter than the 45-degree line, so a small vertical shift moves the crossing point a long way to the right.

Full credit: PAE up $2 trillion, GDP up $8 trillion, different because a nonzero mpc creates a multiplier of 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4.

2 points 3(g). What is Pacifica's output gap after the boom? Is it a recessionary or an expansionary gap? What would you expect to see in Pacifica's labor market?

Show answer

The output gap is Y − Y* = 48 − 40 = +$8 trillion, which is 20% of potential output. Output is above potential, so this is an expansionary gap.

Firms are producing more than their normal, comfortable capacity, so they hire aggressively and work people overtime. Unemployment falls below its normal long-run level. This is the mirror image of his class 22 slide 11 point that a negative output gap means unemployment is above its long-run level.

Full credit: gap of +8, expansionary, unemployment below its normal level.

2 points 3(h). Suppose Congress wants to bring output back to potential using government purchases alone. By how much must G change, and in which direction? What will G be afterward? Explain fully.

Show answer

Output must fall by $8 trillion, and with a multiplier of 4, PAE only needs to fall by 8 ÷ 4 = $2 trillion. So G must fall by $2 trillion, from 4.5 to 2.5. Check: the new intercept is 12 − 2 = 10, and Y = 10 ÷ 0.25 = 40.

Notice that G ends at 2.5, which is $1.5 trillion below where it was before the boom. Repealing the infrastructure bill (cutting G by only 0.5) is not enough, because Congress also has to offset the $1.5 trillion private investment boom.

Two traps: cutting G by $8 trillion (the size of the GDP gap) forgets the multiplier and would cause a deep recession, with Y = (12 − 8) ÷ 0.25 = 16. And if Congress used taxes instead, it would need a bigger change, because only 75 cents of each tax dollar comes out of spending: 0.75 × ΔT = 2, so taxes would have to rise by about $2.67 trillion.

Full credit: decrease G by $2 trillion (to 2.5), because the PAE shift needed is the gap divided by the multiplier, 8 ÷ 4 = 2.

2 points 3(i). Suppose Congress does nothing. What happens to inflation in Pacifica right away, and what happens to it over the following year or two? Explain fully.

Show answer

Right away, inflation barely changes. Edwards calls this inflation inertia (nominal rigidity): prices are set by long-term contracts and printed menus, and firms have limited information, so prices and wages do not jump to new levels immediately.

Over time, inflation rises. With Y above Y*, firms are operating above their comfortable capacity, so as contracts expire and menus are reprinted they raise their prices by more than past inflation. With many firms doing this, the inflation rate rises, and it keeps rising as long as output stays above potential. His summary on class 22 slide 41 is the rule to memorize: "When Y > Y*, inflation rises."

Full credit: no immediate change because of inflation inertia, then inflation gradually rises because output is above potential.

2 points 3(j). The Fed follows a reaction function. Explain fully how the Fed's response brings output back to potential, and sketch the shift on your graph from 3(e).

Show answer

As inflation rises, the Fed follows its reaction function and raises the real interest rate r. A higher r reduces planned investment and the interest-sensitive parts of consumption such as housing and new cars, and it also tends to appreciate the currency, which lowers net exports. All of these lower PAE at every level of Y, so the PAE line shifts down, and output falls by the multiplier times that shift.

As long as Y is still above Y*, inflation keeps rising, so the Fed keeps raising r and PAE keeps shifting down. The process stops only when the PAE line crosses the 45-degree line at Y* = 40. That final line is the long-run expenditure line, PAELR.

One more layer that earns credit: inflation was rising the whole time Y was above Y*, so even after output returns to potential, inflation is higher than where it started. Getting it back down to the old level would take the Fed shifting its reaction function up, which causes a temporary recession, as in his class 22 slides 58 to 66.

Full credit: rising inflation leads the Fed to raise r; higher r lowers I and C (and NX); PAE shifts down until it crosses the 45-degree line at Y*. The sketch shows PAE' shifting down to a line through (40, 40).

2 points 3(k). Suppose each 1 percentage point increase in the real interest rate lowers autonomous planned investment and consumption by a combined $1 trillion at every level of Y. By how many percentage points must the Fed raise r, in total, to return Pacifica to potential? In the long run, which kinds of spending are larger and which are smaller than before the boom?

Show answer

PAE must shift down by the gap divided by the multiplier, 8 ÷ 4 = $2 trillion, the same $2 trillion as in 3(h). At $1 trillion per point, the Fed must raise r by 2 ÷ 1 = 2 percentage points. Check: the intercept falls from 12 to 10, and Y = 10 ÷ 0.25 = 40. (Answering 8 points forgets that the multiplier also works on the Fed's shift.)

In the long run, autonomous spending is back to $10 trillion, but its mix has changed. Government purchases are 4.5, which is 0.5 higher than before the boom. Private autonomous spending, C̄ − cT + I, was 3 + 4 = 7 before the boom, rose to 3 + 5.5 = 8.5 during it, and the higher r took away 2, leaving 6.5, which is 0.5 lower than before. So the higher interest rate crowds out $0.5 trillion of interest-sensitive private spending, exactly the size of the government's increase. The boom's extra spending only fits back inside potential output by squeezing out other investment and big-ticket consumer purchases such as homes and cars.

Full credit: 2 percentage points (PAE must fall by 8 ÷ 4 = 2), and in the long run G is higher while interest-sensitive private spending is lower.

Question 4: Almond joy, almond annoy (12 points)

Suppose the U.S. exports almonds to Europe, where they sell at a world price of €4.50 per pound. U.S. domestic demand for almonds is P = 8 − Q and U.S. supply is P = 2 + 0.5Q, where Q is billions of pounds per year and P is dollars per pound. All numbers are hypothetical. The exchange rate starts at €0.75 per $1.

4 points 4(a). The diagram below shows the U.S. market. First find the price and quantity with no trade. Then convert the world price into dollars, and adapt the diagram to show the world price, U.S. quantity demanded, U.S. quantity supplied, and exports. Briefly explain what you drew.

Show answer
  1. No trade: 8 − Q = 2 + 0.5Q, so 6 = 1.5Q, Q = 4 billion pounds, and P = 8 − 4 = $4 per pound.
  2. World price in dollars: €4.50 ÷ €0.75 per $1 = $6.00 per pound. This is above the no-trade price of $4, which is why the U.S. exports almonds.
  3. At $6: quantity demanded is 8 − 6 = 2 billion pounds; quantity supplied solves 6 = 2 + 0.5Q, so Q = 8 billion pounds.
  4. Exports = quantity supplied − quantity demanded = 8 − 2 = 6 billion pounds.

Full credit: a horizontal world price line at $6, above the $4 no-trade price, with quantity demanded (2) and quantity supplied (8) marked and the gap of 6 labeled as exports, plus one sentence saying the world price is above the domestic price, so growers sell the excess abroad.

The Fed raises interest rates and global investors seek out U.S. assets, so the dollar appreciates from €0.75 to €0.90 per $1. The world price in Europe stays at €4.50 per pound.

2 points 4(b). What price in dollars do U.S. almond growers now receive? What happens to U.S. quantity demanded, quantity supplied, and exports? Show the new price on your graph from 4(a).

Show answer
  1. New dollar price: €4.50 ÷ €0.90 per $1 = $5.00 per pound. European buyers still pay €4.50, but each euro now converts into fewer dollars.
  2. At $5: quantity demanded is 8 − 5 = 3 billion pounds; quantity supplied solves 5 = 2 + 0.5Q, so Q = 6 billion pounds.
  3. Exports fall from 6 to 6 − 3 = 3 billion pounds, cut in half.

U.S. growers cannot charge more than $5 at home either: any U.S. buyer offered a higher price could import at the world price, and any grower would rather sell abroad than at home for less. A European tariff would do the same thing to growers. For example, at the old exchange rate, a tariff of €0.75 per pound would leave growers with €3.75 ÷ €0.75 = $5.00 per pound.

Full credit: the dollar price falls to $5; quantity demanded rises to 3, quantity supplied falls to 6, and exports fall from 6 to 3, with the $5 line drawn below the $6 line.

2 points 4(c). Calculate the change in U.S. consumer surplus, U.S. producer surplus, and total U.S. surplus. Who wins and who loses? Show your work.

Show answer
  1. Consumer surplus (triangle under demand, above the price): at $6, ½ × 2 × (8 − 6) = $2 billion. At $5, ½ × 3 × (8 − 5) = $4.5 billion. Change: +$2.5 billion.
  2. Producer surplus (triangle above supply, below the price): at $6, ½ × 8 × (6 − 2) = $16 billion. At $5, ½ × 6 × (5 − 2) = $9 billion. Change: −$7 billion.
  3. Total U.S. surplus changes by 2.5 − 7 = −$4.5 billion.

Second check with the areas between the two prices: the consumer gain is the strip left of demand, ((2 + 3) ÷ 2) × $1 = $2.5 billion, and the producer loss is the strip left of supply, ((8 + 6) ÷ 2) × $1 = $7 billion.

U.S. almond buyers win. U.S. almond growers lose, by more than buyers gain, so the U.S. as a whole is worse off in this market. For an export good, a lower world price hurts the country because it is a seller on the world market.

Full credit: consumer surplus +$2.5 billion, producer surplus −$7 billion, total −$4.5 billion; buyers win, growers lose, net loss.

Now consider the labor market for almond workers. Labor demand is the marginal revenue product of labor, MRPL = MR × MPL, where MR is the price growers receive per pound and the marginal product of labor is MPL = 8 − 0.1L pounds per worker-hour, with L in thousands of workers. Labor supply is w = 4 + 0.5L, where w is the hourly wage.

4 points 4(d). Find the equilibrium wage and employment before the appreciation (price $6) and after it (price $5). Illustrate both on the axes below, showing all demand and supply curves and both equilibria, and briefly explain.

Show answer
  1. Before, at $6: MRPL = 6 × (8 − 0.1L) = 48 − 0.6L. Set equal to supply: 48 − 0.6L = 4 + 0.5L, so 44 = 1.1L and L = 40 thousand workers. Wage: w = 4 + 0.5 × 40 = $24 per hour (check: 48 − 0.6 × 40 = 24).
  2. After, at $5: MRPL = 5 × (8 − 0.1L) = 40 − 0.5L. Set equal to supply: 40 − 0.5L = 4 + 0.5L, so 36 = L, which is 36 thousand workers. Wage: w = 4 + 0.5 × 36 = $22 per hour (check: 40 − 0.5 × 36 = 22).
  3. Employment falls by 4,000 workers and the wage falls by $2 per hour.

The workers did not become less productive: each one still picks the same number of pounds per hour. What changed is the value of each pound to the grower. Since a worker is worth MR × MPL to the employer, a lower MR lowers labor demand, and wages and jobs fall in the export industry. This is the reverse of his class 16 slide showing labor demand shifting up for an export good when trade opens.

Full credit: L = 40 thousand and w = $24 before; L = 36 thousand and w = $22 after; a graph with labor demand (MRPL) shifting down, supply unchanged, and both equilibria labeled; and the reason: a lower price (MR) lowers MRPL = MR × MPL.
