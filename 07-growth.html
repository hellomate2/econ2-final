Class 18 · Long-run economic growth

Long-run growth

Why some countries are rich and others are poor, and why small differences in growth rates turn into huge differences in living standards. On the Fall 2025 final this unit was a 2-point multiple choice question on "social infrastructure" (the answer was all of the above: rule of law, property rights, protection from government), and the same deck's price-index slides are the tool behind the 2-point Big Mac question.

Built from Edwards' Fall 2025 slides for this class. It will be checked against his Fall 2026 slides after he teaches it on Tuesday, November 3, 2026.

The ideas, explained

Two questions macro asks

Edwards splits macroeconomics into two topics: the long-run trend in output, and short-run fluctuations around that trend (booms and recessions). This unit is the long-run trend. The next unit is the fluctuations.

In the long run, output is set by the economy's resources. The slides call the level the economy reaches at normal use of its resources potential output, written Y*. He adds that "a better name for potential output might be 'normal output.'" Recessions push output below Y* for a while, but the economy does not stay depressed forever. Growth is about what makes Y* itself rise over time.

Growth rates and compounding

A growth rate is the percentage change in a variable from one year to the next:

growth rate = (Ythis year − Ylast year) ÷ Ylast year. If income per person goes from $50,000 to $51,500, the growth rate is 1,500 ÷ 50,000 = 0.03, or 3%.

Growth compounds: each year's growth applies to a base that already includes all the earlier growth. So after t years at a steady rate g (written as a decimal),

Yt = Y0 × (1 + g)t. Y0 is the starting level, g is the yearly growth rate as a decimal (2% is 0.02), and t is the number of years.

His slides make the point with two countries, A and B, that start at the same real income per person, with A growing 1 percentage point faster every year (for example 1% versus 0%). After 1 year, A is 1% richer. After 2 years it is 2.01% richer, because 1.01 × 1.01 = 1.0201. After 70 years it is twice as rich, because 1.0170 ≈ 2. After two centuries it is more than 7 times as rich, because 1.01200 ≈ 7.3. A one-point gap that nobody would notice in a single year becomes the difference between a rich country and a poor one over a few generations.

The rule of 70

The textbook gives a shortcut for how long something takes to double:

years to double ≈ 70 ÷ (growth rate in percent). At 2% a year, income doubles in about 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years. At 7% a year, it doubles in about 10 years.

Two details matter on an exam. First, use the growth rate as a percent number (2, not 0.02). Dividing 70 by 0.02 gives 3,500, which is nonsense. Second, the rule is an approximation. The exact doubling time at 3% is 23.45 years, and the rule says 23.3. That is close enough for any question that says "about" or "approximately." The slide's 1.0170 ≈ 2 is the rule of 70 at a 1% rate.

The rule runs both ways. If a country doubled its income per person in 14 years, its growth rate was about 70 ÷ 14 = 5% a year. His slide 34 table (from Jones and Vollrath's textbook) has a "years to double" column next to average growth from 1960 to 2008, and the column matches 70 ÷ growth closely: the United States grew 1.6% a year, and 70 ÷ 1.6 ≈ 44 versus the table's 43; China grew 5.6% a year, and 70 ÷ 5.6 = 12.5 versus the table's 13. The small differences are rounding and the fact that the rule is approximate. The table also lists negative doubling times for the "growth disasters" (Venezuela, Haiti, Madagascar, Zimbabwe), because income there was shrinking.

Average growth over several years

If income goes from Y0 to Yt over t years, the average yearly growth rate g solves Yt = Y0(1 + g)t, so

g = (Yt ÷ Y0)1/t − 1. From 100 to 121 over 2 years: (1.21)1/2 − 1 = 1.1 − 1 = 0.10, so 10% a year. The total change was 21%, which is more than 2 × 10% because the second year's growth applies to the larger base.

Breaking income per person into two pieces

Living standards are measured by potential output per person. The slides split it into two factors that multiply together:

Y*/POP = (Y*/N*) × (N*/POP). Y* is potential output, POP is population, and N* is normal employment (the number of people working when the economy is at normal levels). Y*/N* is average labor productivity, output per worker. N*/POP is the employment-to-population ratio, the share of the population that works.

The identity is just algebra (the N* cancels), but it organizes the question. A country can have more output per person because each worker produces more, or because a larger share of its people work.

His slide 34 table lets you check it with real data. The United States in 2008 had GDP per worker of $84,771 and a labor force participation rate of 0.51, and 84,771 × 0.51 ≈ $43,233, close to the table's GDP per person of $43,326. China had $10,938 per worker and 0.59, and 10,938 × 0.59 ≈ $6,453, close to its $6,415. The two countries have similar shares of people working; the gap in income per person comes almost entirely from output per worker.

Average labor productivity in turn depends on two things:

Y*/N* = f(K*/N*, T). K*/N* is normal capital per worker and T is technology. Put together: Y*/POP = f(K*/N*, T) × (N*/POP). This is what the slides call the aggregate production function.

In words, from his slide 22: normal output per person is the employment-to-population ratio times normal output per worker, and output per worker depends on capital per worker and technology.

What raises average labor productivity

The slides group the determinants into capital (physical and human) and technology, with institutions counted as part of technology. The textbook lists the same causes in a longer list that adds natural resources and entrepreneurship. Know both versions.

  • Human capital. Education and job training. The slides count it as capital because, like a machine, it was built in the past and helps production now. His slide 50 (from the Economic Report of the President 2010) shows mean years of schooling rising steadily across birth cohorts in the 1900s. Slide 49 judges that "The increases probably account for a moderate amount of the observed rise in Y*/POP over time."
  • Physical capital. Machines, buildings, computers, and infrastructure such as roads, telecommunications systems, and dams. More tools per worker means more output per worker.
  • Technology. "The methods for producing things," and more broadly "Everything that affects how much output per worker we produce using a given amount of capital per worker." His components: production techniques, management techniques, economic institutions, and local culture.
  • Natural resources. (Textbook.) Land, minerals, energy, and so on. More resources per worker can raise output, but resources are neither necessary nor sufficient for being rich; many resource-poor countries are rich.
  • Entrepreneurship and management. (Textbook.) People who start businesses, bring new products to market, and organize production well raise productivity. The slides fold management into technology ("management techniques," "new methods of organization and management (assembly line, accounting)").
  • Social infrastructure, also called institutions. The basic building blocks that let markets work: the rule of law, property rights, and protection from government. His three key features of institutions that raise output per person are "A market-based system for allocating resources," "Government protection of property from others," and "Protection of property from government corruption, theft, arbitrary taxation, ...." Without them, people do not invest, because what they build can be taken.

The Fall 2025 final asked which of "the rule of law," "property rights," and "protection from government" is an important aspect of social infrastructure. The answer was (iv) all of the above. Edwards put the same three-item list on his Class 18 slides and on two slides of his final review deck, so memorize the trio word for word.

His Hall and Jones scatterplot (slide 40) shows countries with a higher index of social infrastructure having much higher output per worker. The slides draw the lesson that the kind of "technology" that differs most across countries is "probably not knowledge, but institutions and culture." Poor countries can read the same engineering textbooks as rich ones. What they often lack is the legal and political setting that makes it worth using that knowledge.

Diminishing returns to capital

Diminishing returns to capital means that if the number of workers and the technology stay the same, each extra unit of capital adds less output than the one before. One tractor on a farm with ten workers is a big help. The tenth tractor adds very little, because there are not enough workers to drive them all.

The slides use diminishing returns twice:

  • For capital: "doubling K*/N* less than doubles Y*/POP." So piling up physical capital alone cannot keep growth going. Each round of investment buys a smaller gain.
  • For the employment ratio: when N*/POP rises with a fixed capital stock, capital per worker K*/N* falls, so output per person rises "less than proportionally with N*/POP." On top of that, N*/POP "doesn't tend to change much, and it can't rise indefinitely," since it cannot go above 1.

The graph shows the difference between the two engines of growth. Adding capital is a move along the curve, and the curve flattens. Better technology shifts the curve up, so the same capital produces more. Only the second can keep going without running into diminishing returns, which is why the slides conclude that technology does most of the work.

What explains growth over time

The slides check each factor in the production function against the history of rich countries, and conclude:

  • Changes in the normal employment-to-population ratio are not very important. There have been periods when it mattered (slide 44 circles a stretch of rising U.S. employment-to-population ratio, and slide 45 shows the rise in women's labor force participation), but it cannot rise forever.
  • Increases in normal capital per worker, physical and human, are somewhat important. Physical capital runs into diminishing returns, and the observed increases are not large enough to explain much of the rise in output per person.
  • Improvements in technology are the main driver, "perhaps responsible for 2/3 of overall growth." His argument is by elimination: if it is not N*/POP or K*/N*, it must be T.

What explains differences across countries

The cross-country version of the same exercise (slide 41):

  • Differences in the employment-to-population ratio are not very important, because it does not vary much across countries (in the slide 34 table, the labor force participation rates of the U.S. and China are 0.51 and 0.59).
  • Differences in capital per worker (physical and human) and in technology are both very important. "A reasonable approximation" is that each accounts for half of cross-country income differences. His slide 37 table shows how big the capital gaps are: in 2008, physical capital per worker was $292,614 in the U.S. against $20,373 in India, and average years of education were 13.2 against 5.2.
  • The most important kind of technology difference is institutions, not know-how.

Notice the asymmetry. Across countries, capital and technology split about half and half. Over time in a rich country, technology dominates (about 2/3). A question that says "across countries" or "over time" is pointing you at one of these two answers.

Growth seen in the labor market

Edwards' own framing ties growth back to Class 12. The wage is labor's marginal revenue product, MRPL = P × MPL, where P is the price of the output (its marginal revenue in a competitive market) and MPL is the marginal product of labor, the extra output from one more worker. Labor demand is the MRPL curve. Anything that raises MRPL shifts labor demand out and raises wages and employment:

  • Higher product demand and price P, for example through openness to trade and exports, which raises a country's terms of trade (its export price).
  • Anything that raises MPL: more physical capital, more human capital (education), and better technology.
  • Underneath all of these, the social infrastructure that makes the activity possible.

Only permanent outward shifts in labor demand raise wages and employment in the long run. Large enough demand shifts can absorb a growing population (supply shifting out) and still keep wages rising.

The Malthusian trap

For most of history, income per person barely grew. His slides call this "the Malthusian trap" of stagnant population, income, disease, and death. Some countries have escaped it and others have not, which is a big part of why income gaps between countries are so large today.

His evidence is Gregory Clark's data on real wages in England. Real wages showed sustained increases only after 1800 (slide 29). Before that, a scatterplot of real wages against population for 1250 to 1450 traces out a single downward-sloping line (slide 30), which Edwards reads as a static labor demand curve: when plague cut the population, wages rose, and when population recovered, wages fell back, moving along the same demand curve. Around 1800 the pattern breaks (slide 32, "Here's the arrow"): population and real wages rise together, which can only happen if labor demand is shifting out.

Technology in history, and where it comes from

The slides show three forms technological change takes:

  • New production techniques: new machines (the electric motor, the tractor) and new ways to organize and manage (the assembly line, accounting). His slide 60 (Steckel and White) estimates the "social savings" from the farm tractor in 1954 at 8.0% of GNP with the 1954 crop mix, mostly from wage labor freed up for other work.
  • New products, such as the helicopter, air-conditioning, penicillin, the transistor, and the computer (a list from Nordhaus that the slide extends with "Not to mention the internet!").
  • Better institutions, such as opening up to trade and relying more on market forces.

Many things affect how much invention happens (slide 65): the strength of inventors' property rights, government subsidies or direct funding of research, more competition and lower barriers to entry, national emergencies, the scale of the market, education, consumer tastes for novelty, cultural attitudes toward innovation, and luck.

The free market almost certainly produces too little invention, because inventions have large positive externalities: other people benefit from an idea without paying the inventor. That is especially true of basic science. Recall the midterm material: when a good has a positive externality, its marginal social benefit is above the private benefit the market responds to, so the market quantity is below the efficient one. His policy list (slide 67): increase education, subsidize research and development (particularly basic science), make property rights more secure, and remember that better institutions are themselves a form of technological progress, which matters most for poor countries.

Wages versus GDP per person

His glossary slide separates two numbers people mix up. The wage is labor's marginal revenue product, a number like $30 an hour, which adds up to $60,000 a year over 50 weeks of 40 hours (30 × 40 × 50 = 60,000). GDP per person is average income, a number like $88,000 per person in the U.S. The difference is capital income per person, such as stock dividends and interest. The two are different, but they tend to grow at the same rate in the long run.

Comparing dollars across years

The start of the Class 18 deck finishes Chapter 18 on prices. To say what $XA in year A is worth in year B dollars, scale it by the ratio of the price index in the two years:

$XB = $XA × (PB ÷ PA), where PA and PB are the price index (for example the CPI, the consumer price index) in years A and B. Equivalently, $XB ÷ PB = $XA ÷ PA: the same purchasing power.

His two examples. Cal in-state tuition and fees were $1,296 in 1985 to 1986, when the CPI was 108.94; the CPI around 2023 was 300.536. In 2023 dollars: 1,296 × 300.536 ÷ 108.94 = $3,575. Actual 2023 tuition and fees were $14,796, so real tuition is much higher today. A good desktop computer cost about $4,000 in 1996, when the CPI was 156.85. In 2023 dollars that is 4,000 × 300.536 ÷ 156.85 = $7,664, and a good desktop now costs about $1,000, so the real price of a computer has fallen a lot.

This is exactly the tool for Fall 2025 Q2(a), the Big Mac question; see Worked Example 3.

Worked examples

Example 1: Doubling times and the rule of 70

Country A and country B both have income per person of $10,000 today. A grows 2% a year and B grows 1% a year (made-up numbers). (a) About how long until each one doubles? (b) What is each country's income after 70 years? (c) How much richer is A than B after 70 years?

  1. Rule of 70: A doubles in about 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years; B doubles in about 70 ÷ 1 = 70 years.
  2. Exact compounding: A has 10,000 × 1.0270 = 10,000 × 4.00 = $39,996. B has 10,000 × 1.0170 = 10,000 × 2.007 = $20,068.
  3. Check with the rule: in 70 years A doubles twice (×4) and B doubles once (×2). That matches.
  4. Ratio: 39,996 ÷ 20,068 = 1.99, so A is about twice as rich.

Answer: (a) about 35 and 70 years. (b) about $40,000 and $20,000. (c) about twice as rich, from a growth gap of just 1 percentage point. That is the slide 24 to 25 lesson.

Example 2: Splitting income per person into productivity and employment

A made-up country has potential output Y* = $600 billion, normal employment N* = 10 million, and population POP = 25 million. (a) Find average labor productivity, the employment-to-population ratio, and potential output per person. (b) If the employment ratio rose to 0.5 with productivity unchanged, what would output per person be? (c) Why would the real increase be smaller than that?

  1. Average labor productivity: Y*/N* = 600 billion ÷ 10 million = $60,000 per worker.
  2. Employment-to-population ratio: N*/POP = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4.
  3. Output per person: 60,000 × 0.4 = $24,000. Check directly: 600 billion ÷ 25 million = $24,000.
  4. With N*/POP = 0.5 and productivity still $60,000: 60,000 × 0.5 = $30,000, a 25% increase.
  5. But more workers with the same capital means less capital per worker, so by diminishing returns output per worker falls below $60,000. Output per person rises, but by less than 25%.

Answer: (a) $60,000; 0.4; $24,000. (b) $30,000 if productivity held. (c) Capital per worker falls, so productivity falls; output per person "rises less than proportionally with N*/POP."

Example 3: Real prices with a price index (the Big Mac, Fall 2025 Q2a)

The Fall 2025 final reported that a Big Mac cost $1.60 in 1986 and $5.65 in 2021, about 3.5 times higher, while the CPI was about 110 in 1986 and roughly 271 in 2021, about 2.5 times higher. Has the real price of a Big Mac gone up, down, or stayed the same?

  1. Compare growth factors. The Big Mac price rose by 5.65 ÷ 1.60 = 3.53 times. The CPI rose by 271 ÷ 110 = 2.46 times.
  2. The Big Mac rose faster than prices in general, so its real (inflation-adjusted) price rose.
  3. Same thing with the slide 9 formula: $1.60 in 1986 is worth 1.60 × 271 ÷ 110 = $3.94 in 2021 dollars. The actual 2021 price, $5.65, is above $3.94.

Answer: The real price of a Big Mac increased. If your income only kept up with inflation, you could buy fewer Big Macs today than in 1986. The key's answer was two lines: "The CPI rose by a factor of 2.5, while the Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5. Therefore the real price of a Big Mac has increased."

Example 4: Average growth rate over several years

Real GDP per person in a made-up country rises from $20,000 to $24,200 over 2 years. What was the average yearly growth rate? About how long would it take to double at that rate?

  1. Total growth factor: 24,200 ÷ 20,000 = 1.21.
  2. Yearly factor: square root of 1.21 = 1.10, so g = 10% a year. (Check: 20,000 × 1.1 = 22,000, then 22,000 × 1.1 = 24,200.)
  3. Doubling time: about 70 ÷ 10 = 7 years.

Answer: 10% a year, not 10.5% (21% ÷ 2), because growth compounds. It would double in about 7 years.

Example 5: A capital boom in the labor-market picture

Suppose firms install a wave of new equipment that makes workers more productive (a made-up scenario). Use the labor market to show the long-run effect on wages and employment, and explain whether more equipment alone can keep wages rising forever.

  1. Labor demand is MRPL = P × MPL. More capital per worker raises MPL, so MRPL rises at every quantity of labor.
  2. Draw W on the vertical axis and L on the horizontal. Draw upward-sloping S and downward-sloping D1. Shift demand out to D2. The new equilibrium has a higher wage and more employment.
  3. Keep adding equipment with the same workers and technology, and each round raises MPL by less (diminishing returns to capital). The demand shifts get smaller and smaller.
  4. Sustained wage growth needs technology, which shifts the production function up instead of moving along it.

Answer: Demand shifts out, so the wage and employment both rise. Capital alone runs into diminishing returns, so lasting growth in wages requires technological progress (including better institutions).

Common mistakes

Mistake: Plugging the growth rate into the rule of 70 as a decimal: 70 ÷ 0.02 = 3,500 years.

Correct: Use the percent number: 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years. In the compounding formula, use the decimal: (1.02)35.

Mistake: Adding up growth rates over time. "5% a year for 10 years is 50%."

Correct: Growth compounds: 1.0510 = 1.63, a 63% increase. Going backward, a 21% rise over 2 years is 10% a year, not 10.5%.

Mistake: Saying that a country can grow forever by investing in more machines.

Correct: Diminishing returns to capital: with the same workers and technology, each extra unit of capital adds less output. Doubling capital per worker less than doubles output per person. Lasting growth comes mostly from technology.

Mistake: Treating GDP per person and GDP per worker as the same thing.

Correct: GDP per person = GDP per worker × the share of people who work. Average labor productivity is per worker.

Mistake: Thinking "technology" only means gadgets and scientific knowledge.

Correct: In Edwards' slides, technology is anything that changes output per worker for a given capital per worker, including management, institutions, and culture. Across countries, the technology gap is mainly institutions.

Mistake: Comparing a price in two years without adjusting for inflation, or concluding a real price fell just because the dollar price rose less than you expected.

Correct: Compare the item's growth factor with the CPI's growth factor. If the item rose by more, its real price rose.

Mistake: Picking one item ("property rights") on the social infrastructure multiple choice.

Correct: His list is the rule of law, property rights, and protection from government, all three together. "All of the above."

Practice questions

2 points Which of these is an important part of the "social infrastructure" that lets markets function well?

(i) The rule of law
(ii) Property rights
(iii) Protection from government
(iv) All of the above

Show answer

(iv). Edwards' slides list all three as the basic building blocks of a society that let markets work, and call them "institutions" or "social infrastructure." Without them people will not invest, because what they build or earn can be taken by others or by the government.

Full credit: circle (iv).

2 points A country's real GDP per person grows at 3.5% a year. About how many years does it take to double?

(i) 2 years
(ii) 20 years
(iii) 35 years
(iv) 245 years

Show answer

(ii). By the rule of 70, doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 years. (The exact answer is 20.1 years.) Choice (iv) comes from multiplying instead of dividing, and (i) from dividing 70 by 35.

Full credit: circle (ii).

2 points "Diminishing returns to capital" means that, holding workers and technology fixed,

(i) Each additional unit of capital adds less output than the previous one
(ii) Adding capital reduces total output
(iii) Capital wears out over time
(iv) Capital per worker falls as the economy grows

Show answer

(i). Output keeps rising as capital is added, but by smaller and smaller amounts, so doubling capital per worker less than doubles output per person. (ii) is wrong because the extra output is still positive. (iii) describes depreciation, a different idea.

Full credit: circle (i).

2 points According to Edwards' slides, which factor accounts for the largest share of the long-run growth in potential output per person in rich countries over time?

(i) Increases in the employment-to-population ratio
(ii) Increases in physical capital per worker
(iii) Improvements in technology
(iv) Population growth

Show answer

(iii). His slides conclude that changes in the employment ratio are not very important, capital per worker is somewhat important, and technology is responsible for perhaps 2/3 of overall growth. The argument is partly by elimination: what the other two cannot explain must be technology.

Full credit: circle (iii).

6 points Two neighbors, one gap. Northland and Southland both have real income per person of $10,000 today. Northland grows 2% a year; Southland grows 1% a year. These are hypothetical numbers.

(a) (2 points) Using the rule of 70, about how many years until each country's income per person doubles? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) About what is each country's income per person after 70 years? Show your work.

(c) (2 points) A politician says "one percentage point a year is too small to matter." Explain fully why that is wrong.

Show answer

(a) Northland: 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years. Southland: 70 ÷ 1 = 70 years.

(b) Northland doubles twice in 70 years: 10,000 × 2 × 2 = $40,000 (exactly 10,000 × 1.0270 = $39,996). Southland doubles once: $20,000 (exactly 10,000 × 1.0170 = $20,068).

(c) Growth compounds: each year's growth applies to a base that already includes past growth. A 1-point gap is tiny in one year (Northland is 1% richer), but after 70 years Northland is about twice as rich, and after two centuries the gap would be more than sevenfold (1.01200 ≈ 7.3). Small differences in growth rates become large differences in living standards.

Full credit: (a) 35 and 70 years with 70 ÷ g shown; (b) about $40,000 and $20,000; (c) the word "compounding" or the idea that growth builds on a growing base, plus the "twice as rich after 70 years" result.

8 points Data centers and wages. Suppose a country's firms spend heavily on AI data centers and the software that runs on them, which makes many workers more productive. (This is a hypothetical scenario, not a forecast.)

(a) (2 points) In the aggregate production function Y*/POP = f(K*/N*, T) × N*/POP, which term or terms does this change? Explain.

(b) (4 points) Draw the labor market before and after, with the wage on the vertical axis. Show any curves that shift and the old and new equilibrium wage and employment. Explain briefly.

(c) (2 points) If firms keep adding more and more data centers but nothing else changes, will wages keep rising at the same pace? Explain fully.

Show answer

(a) The data centers are physical capital, so K*/N* rises. The software and new ways of working are technology, so T rises too. Both raise average labor productivity Y*/N*, and so output per person.

(b) Labor demand is MRPL = P × MPL. Workers' marginal product rises, so the MRPL curve shifts out from D1 to D2. Supply S is unchanged. The equilibrium moves up along S: the wage rises from W1 to W2 and employment rises from L1 to L2.

(c) No. With the same workers and technology, each extra data center adds less output than the one before (diminishing returns to capital), so MPL and labor demand rise by less each round. Wages keep rising only if technology keeps improving, which shifts the production function itself up.

Full credit: (a) capital per worker, and technology if you mention the software; (b) D = MRPL shifts right with S fixed, higher W and L labeled on both axes; (c) "diminishing returns to capital" and "technology is needed for sustained growth."

6 points Breaking down income per person. A made-up country has potential output of $900 billion, normal employment of 15 million, and a population of 36 million.

(a) (2 points) Compute average labor productivity and the employment-to-population ratio. Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Compute potential output per person two ways and show they match.

(c) (2 points) A new policy raises the share of people working, but the capital stock stays the same. Will output per person rise in proportion to the employment ratio? Explain.

Show answer

(a) Y*/N* = 900 billion ÷ 15 million = $60,000 per worker. N*/POP = 15 ÷ 36 = 0.4167 (5/12).

(b) Directly: 900 billion ÷ 36 million = $25,000. Through the decomposition: 60,000 × 5/12 = $25,000. They match because N* cancels.

(c) No. With more workers and the same capital, capital per worker K*/N* falls, so by diminishing returns output per worker falls. Output per person still rises, but less than proportionally with N*/POP. The ratio also cannot rise past 1, so this cannot drive growth for long.

Full credit: (a) $60,000 and about 0.42; (b) $25,000 both ways; (c) "less than proportionally" because capital per worker falls.

4 points Real prices. A concert ticket cost $25 in a year when the CPI was 125. Today the same kind of ticket costs $90, and the CPI is 300. These are made-up numbers.

(a) (2 points) What is the old ticket price in today's dollars? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) Has the real price of the ticket risen, fallen, or stayed the same? Explain.

Show answer

(a) $XB = $XA × PB ÷ PA = 25 × 300 ÷ 125 = $60.

(b) It has risen. Today's price, $90, is above the $60 that would have just kept up with inflation. Same answer with growth factors: the ticket rose 90 ÷ 25 = 3.6 times while the CPI rose 300 ÷ 125 = 2.4 times.

Full credit: $60 shown with the formula; "risen" with the comparison of $90 to $60 or 3.6 to 2.4.

6 points The plague and the arrow. Edwards showed that English real wages and population moved in opposite directions from 1250 to 1450, and then rose together after about 1800.

(a) (2 points) In a labor market graph, what does a fall in population do, and what happens to the real wage if labor demand does not move?

(b) (2 points) Why does a scatterplot of wages against population for 1250 to 1450 trace out a downward-sloping line?

(c) (2 points) After 1800, population and real wages both rose. What must have been happening to labor demand, and what could cause that?

Show answer

(a) Fewer people shifts labor supply to the left. With demand fixed, the economy moves up along the demand curve, so the real wage rises (and employment falls).

(b) Labor demand stayed in the same place. Population shocks moved supply back and forth, so each year's wage and population landed on the same demand curve, which slopes down. The points therefore trace out the demand curve itself.

(c) Labor demand must have been shifting out, and by more than supply, because a supply increase alone would lower the wage. Labor demand is MRPL = P × MPL, so it shifts out when capital (machines, infrastructure, education) and technology raise the marginal product of labor, or when trade raises the price of the country's output.

Full credit: (a) supply shifts left, wage rises; (b) "static labor demand curve"; (c) demand shifts out, from capital or technology raising MPL (or trade raising P).

4 points Growth over a stretch of years. Real GDP per person in a made-up country goes from $40,000 to $48,400 over 2 years.

(a) (2 points) What was the average yearly growth rate? Show your work.

(b) (2 points) At that rate, about how long does it take income per person to double?

Show answer

(a) Total factor: 48,400 ÷ 40,000 = 1.21. Yearly factor: square root of 1.21 = 1.1. So the growth rate was 10% a year. Check: 40,000 × 1.1 = 44,000, and 44,000 × 1.1 = 48,400.

(b) 70 ÷ 10 = about 7 years.

Full credit: 10% (not 10.5%) with the compounding shown; about 7 years.

4 points Too few inventions? A senator argues that the government should stay out of research because "the market will produce exactly the right number of new ideas."

(a) (2 points) Using a concept from the first half of the course, explain why Edwards' slides disagree.

(b) (2 points) Name two policies his slides list for encouraging technological progress.

Show answer

(a) Inventions have large positive externalities: people other than the inventor benefit from a new idea without paying for it. The inventor responds only to the private benefit, which is below the social benefit, so the market produces less invention than the efficient amount. This is especially true for basic science.

(b) Any two of: increase education; subsidize research and development, particularly basic science; make property rights more secure (for example, inventors' property rights); improve institutions, which are themselves a form of technological progress.

Full credit: (a) "positive externality" with marginal social benefit above private benefit, so too little; (b) two correct policies.

4 points Rich and poor. Edwards' slide 37 shows that in 2008 physical capital per worker was $292,614 in the U.S. and $20,373 in India, and average years of education were 13.2 and 5.2.

(a) (2 points) Which terms of the aggregate production function do these numbers measure, and which way do they push the income gap?

(b) (2 points) According to his slides, roughly what share of cross-country income differences comes from capital per worker, and what accounts for the rest?

Show answer

(a) Both are capital per worker, K*/N*: the first is physical capital and the second is human capital. Much more of both in the U.S. means much higher output per worker, so they help explain why U.S. income per person is far higher.

(b) A reasonable approximation is that capital per worker accounts for about half and technology for the other half. The technology differences are mostly institutions and culture, not knowledge.

Full credit: (a) physical and human capital per worker, raising U.S. productivity relative to India; (b) about half capital, half technology (institutions).

2 points A news story says "the average U.S. wage is about $60,000 a year, but GDP per person is about $88,000, so the data must be wrong." Using Edwards' glossary slide, explain the difference.

Show answer

The two measure different things. A wage is labor's marginal revenue product; $30 an hour for 40 hours a week and 50 weeks is $60,000 a year. GDP per person is average total income, which also includes capital income such as dividends and interest. So GDP per person is larger. The two tend to grow at the same rate in the long run.

Full credit: GDP per person includes capital income per person, not just wages.

One-minute review

  • Growth compounds: Yt = Y0(1 + g)t. A 1-point growth gap makes one country twice as rich in 70 years.
  • Rule of 70: years to double ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate in percent.
  • Y*/POP = (Y*/N*) × (N*/POP), and Y*/N* = f(K*/N*, T).
  • Productivity comes from physical capital, human capital, and technology; the textbook adds natural resources and entrepreneurship; institutions sit underneath all of them.
  • Social infrastructure is the rule of law, property rights, and protection from government. All of the above.
  • Diminishing returns: more capital alone gives smaller and smaller gains. The employment ratio cannot rise forever.
  • Over time, technology explains most growth (about 2/3). Across countries, capital and technology split about half and half, and the technology gap is mostly institutions.
  • Labor demand is MRPL = P × MPL; only lasting outward shifts raise wages and employment in the long run. England escaped the Malthusian trap around 1800.
  • Real prices: $XB = $XA × PB ÷ PA. If an item rose faster than the CPI, its real price rose.
